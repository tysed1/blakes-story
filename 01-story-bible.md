# THE BLAKES — Definitive Story Bible

*A ten-chapter narrative-driven open-world game. North Georgia, October 1974 – August 1976.*

This bible is canon. Chapters, maps and audits are written against it. Most of what it contains is never told to the player directly; it exists so that every character behaves consistently for fifty hours.

## 1. Premise

In October 1974, in a railroad town of 2,100 people in the north Georgia mountains, an eighteen-year-old mechanic named Ellis Blake plays a Thursday set at a tavern to sixteen people who mostly aren't listening. Eight days later, because the scheduled Friday band doesn't show, he ends up on the same tiny stage with a college philosophy student, a rich kid who drums like a car crash, and a bass player who came to fix the hum in the house amplifier. They play forty-five minutes. Something happens that none of them can name. The owner writes a name on the chalkboard by the door, and they are a band.

Over the next twenty-three months the player lives inside these four people as they become The Blakes: bars, a college radio signal you have to drive up a mountain to hear, a 45 pressed on credit, a green van that smells like something died in it, a record contract, a debut album nobody knows how to sell, a single that climbs, a myth that grows faster than the people inside it, and a festival in upstate New York where 150,000 strangers watch Ellis Blake come apart and believe they are watching the greatest performance of the decade.

The rock-band story is the surface. Underneath it: a boy who was driving the night his little sister died, who has been talking to a young woman named Clara for eighteen months, and who has never once asked himself where she lives.

## 2. Thematic architecture

**Central question:** *Who are you when everyone else has decided who you are?*

The game asks it of every major character, never out loud until the very end, and gives no single answer.

- **Ellis:** the world decides he is a tortured poet. His father decided he is the boy who killed Grace. His own mind decided Grace isn't gone. The nearest thing to an answer the game offers is a fourteen-year-old on a cassette: *You're my brother.*
- **Riley:** she renamed herself at thirteen. The world renames her "Ellis Blake's girlfriend."
- **Cal:** he is the one who is "smart," "every band needs one." The contract names Ellis the band. He knows he's the floor the whole house stands on and that nobody looks at floors.
- **Dean:** his father decided he'd be an accountant. He decided he'd never stop moving. Both are cages.
- **Wayne:** he decided grief had to be somebody's fault. It made him into a man he doesn't recognize.

**Secondary questions** (built into events, never announced):
1. *Can you leave a place without abandoning the people in it, living or dead?* (Ellis, Riley, Dean, Lorraine)
2. *Is being heard the same as being understood?* (the radio, the audience, Clara)
3. *Is a mistake that everyone follows still a mistake?* ("a turn")
4. *Who owns a person's story once other people start telling it?* (the press, the footage, the notebooks, the band name)
5. *What does love look like from people who can't say it?* (Wayne's half a tire, Cal's rearranged ledger, Roy's radio on the ridge)

**Thematic engines** (how the game makes the questions felt):
- **Names.** The band is named after a family that no longer exists. Clara is named after a grandmother and a dead girl's middle name. Riley chose her own. "Ellis Blake" becomes a brand while Ellis is still using it to sign for oil deliveries.
- **Leaving and staying.** The last real argument between Ellis and Grace was about leaving. The band's one sacred promise is "Nobody leaves somebody behind." Clara's recurring plea is "Take me with you." Grace's last words were permission to go.
- **Following.** The band's great skill is following Ellis when he goes off-map. It makes them great. At the end it makes them late.
- **Signal and noise.** Radio, feedback, rumor, hallucination: sounds that arrive without a visible source.
- **Interpretation versus event.** A professor says memory is reconstruction. A boy's memory of speeding was built from his father's accusations. A nation's memory of a nineteen-year-old is built from footage.
- **Home.** The first thing Ellis wants is to leave. The last thing the game asks you to do is take him home.

## 3. The world

### 3.1 The map

One continuous map, west to east. Three playable places and the country between them. (User-supplied layout, adopted as canon.)

```
 LAUREL CITY  ── US 19 (Laurel Gap corridor) ──  HOLLOW RIDGE  ── (Tanner Valley corridor) ──  TANNERSVILLE
 (west; big city,                                  (center; small town,                           (east; big city,
  Atlanta-like)                                     home)                                          Nashville-like)
        rail line ─────────────────────────────────────── runs south of Hollow Ridge ─────────────── to the Tannersville yard
                          North highlands (Stony Knob) above Hollow Ridge
                          South Fork highlands below Hollow Ridge
```

Drive times (game-scaled): Hollow Ridge to either city is roughly 40–50 minutes in period terms; in game time, a meaningful drive of several minutes that the story regularly uses for conversation. The cities are about 90 minutes apart.

Real Atlanta and Nashville are never named as separate places. The state is still Georgia. National history is real (Nixon, Ford, Saigon, Carter, the Bicentennial, real bands, real magazines, real television formats), in the tradition of fictional cities placed inside real history.

### 3.2 Hollow Ridge

**Population 2,100 (1970 census sign at the town line, which Dean will later deface).** A railroad and creek town in a narrow valley. Hollow Creek runs north to south through the middle of town; Cold Branch Falls sits a mile north on the road up to Stony Knob. The L&N-style main line runs along the south edge of town between the two cities, so every conversation in Hollow Ridge is eventually interrupted by a freight.

The town is older than it is poor, and poorer than it admits. The railroad used to employ two hundred men here; by 1974 it employs about sixty, and everyone knows the number is going down.

**Main Street** (a single block and a half of brick):
- **Marlon's Tavern** — at the foot of Main where it meets Depot Street, backing onto the tracks. Cinderblock and brick, a 1930s bar with a 1950s addition. Pool table, a Bally *Fireball* pinball machine, a jukebox, a cigarette machine, a chalkboard by the door listing the week, a stage eight feet wide made of plywood over two pallets. The back alley faces the rail line. A WIN button ("Whip Inflation Now") sits on the register from October 1974, ironically.
- **Ridge Pharmacy** with a soda counter; **Tate Hardware** (no relation to the family, a running local joke); **Western Auto**; **Piggly Wiggly**; **Hollow Ridge Feed & Seed**; a **barber**; the **Carnegie library** (one room, Mrs. Odom); the **post office**; the **depot** (freight office, passenger service ended 1968).
- **Hollow Ridge Methodist Church** and its cemetery on the hill above Main. The Blake family plot is on the east slope under a cedar.
- **Hollow Ridge Monument & Vault Co.** — a small yard of blank granite across from the cemetery gate.
- **Pettigrew Funeral Home** — also, as in many rural counties in the period, the ambulance service.
- **First Baptist**, **Church of God of Prophecy** out on the Tannersville road.

**Around town:**
- **Hollow Ridge Auto & Service** (Roy Cagle's): east edge of town on the Tanner Valley road. Two bays, two gas pumps, a parts counter, a tow truck named nothing, a radio that gets AM only unless you stand it in the window.
- **Carson's Tire & Recap**: used tires, a pit bull named Deacon.
- **The Blake house**: on Cold Branch Road north of town, halfway up toward Stony Knob. A four-room 1940s frame house plus a later bedroom addition. Porch that needs work. Single exterior bulb. Wayne's 1967 Ford F-100. A chained yard dog, Tater, who is not chained and never leaves anyway.
- **Stony Knob** (North highlands): the overlook pull-off at the top of the road, a fire tower, a gravel turnaround. The only place for thirty miles where the Tannersville college station's 10-watt FM signal comes in clearly, because FM is line of sight and Stony Knob can see the Tanner Valley.
- **Tolliver Road** (South Fork highlands): runs south along the South Fork past the **Tolliver farm** (an old mare named Dolly, dead by 1975) to **Tolliver Bend**, a blind right-hand curve with no shoulder and a white oak whose bark has grown back crooked around a scar. Ellis has not driven this road since April 12, 1973. The game never routes a mission down it until Chapter VIII.
- **The Laurel Gap truck stop**: on US 19 west of town. A Gulf station, a 24-hour diner called the **Starlite** (red vinyl booths, chess pie, a waitress named Lynette), a gravel lot full of pulpwood trucks.
- **South Fork Speedway** (dirt track, Saturday nights, optional content).
- **Hollow Ridge High School** (consolidated county school, the Mountaineers).

**What the town knows:** everything about the crash except what was said inside the car. Everyone knows Grace Blake died on Tolliver Bend on a Thursday night in the rain. Many know Ellis was driving. Some know he was on his way to play Marlon's for the first time. Nobody knows about Clara. This is the meaning, on replay, of "Everybody knows everything." / "Not everything."

### 3.3 Laurel City (west)

A Southern capital of half a million, loosely Atlanta-shaped, never named as Atlanta. New money in glass towers downtown; old money in the northern suburbs; a hippie district along **Tenth Street** fading into head shops and runaways; a black middle class building its own institutions; expressways (SR 400, the SR 9 Spur) still being cut through neighborhoods. A big reservoir to the north with a scatter of lake houses. Railroad city by origin (founded as a terminus), and it still feels like a place things pass through.

What Laurel City means in the story: **the rock city**. It embraces The Blakes before Tannersville does.
- **The Lantern** — an 800-capacity rock club in a former furniture warehouse; the room that changes the band (Ch IV). Theo Marchand runs sound here.
- **Southern Star Records** — a small independent label run out of a converted Victorian on a Tenth Street side street, distributing regionally, with one gold record from 1971 on the wall and a lot of hope.
- **WLRC 97.1 FM, "The Rock of Laurel City"** — the progressive/AOR station that breaks "Still Here" in October 1975.
- **WLSU 88.5** — the state university's student station, which plays the 45 first (Ch V).
- **The Coliseum** — the downtown arena (15,000), where the band opens for a national act in 1976.
- **The Paramount** — a 1929 Moorish movie palace saved from demolition in a 1975 civic campaign (Ch VIII headline show).
- **Engineers Park** — home of the Laurel City Engineers, a Double-A ballclub. Wayne likes them because of the name.
- **Linwood** — Riley's parents' leafy suburb; Presbyterian church with a pipe organ.
- **Midtown apartments** — Theo's place: plants, a reel-to-reel, a fire escape.
- **Emory-style medical district** — Dr. Harriet Lusk's office (Ch VIII).

### 3.4 Tannersville (east)

A river city of half a million on a deep bend of the Tanner River, loosely Nashville-shaped, never named as Nashville. A huge rail yard on the south side. The **SR 60 Expressway** loops the east side. It calls itself a music city and it means country music.

What Tannersville means in the story: **the establishment music city that doesn't want them**, and the place three of the four members live.
- **The Row** — converted houses on two avenues full of song publishers, demo studios and session players. In 1974 the Row is making countrypolitan records with strings, and the Outlaw sound is starting to rattle the windows. Nobody on the Row has any use for a loud mountain band that plays feedback on purpose, until they do.
- **The Tabernacle** — an 1892 brick gospel tabernacle that housed the *Tanner Valley Jubilee* radio show until March 1974, when the show moved to a new theme park in the suburbs. Since then the building has sat nearly empty and there is talk of tearing it down (Ch IX homecoming show).
- **Tannersville College** — a private liberal-arts college on the west side of the river. Brick quads, a philosophy department with one great teacher, dorms with parietal hours, and **WTCR 88.9 FM**, a 10-watt student station whose signal barely clears the river.
- **Vale Music** — an instrument shop on the edge of the Row with a repair bench in the back. Cal works here.
- **Mercer Radio & TV** — Walt Mercer's repair shop in a working-class east-side neighborhood by the rail yard.
- **Belle Grove** — the old-money suburb where the Holloways live.
- **Gaslight Alley** — a downtown alley of lounges and supper clubs where Cal plays paid backing gigs.
- **The Exit** — a 400-seat listening club near the college, the one rock room in town that books them early.
- **Dalton Sound** — Frank Dalton's studio in a former funeral home on the Row, where *Borrowed Stone* is made.
- **Front Street** — the old honky-tonk strip, half-empty since the Jubilee moved.

### 3.5 Off-map spaces

Leaving the map is leaving home. Off-map spaces are compact, dense mission zones reached by travel transitions: the van reaching the edge of the map, a road sign, a cut.

- **Regional tour towns** (Ch V, VII): Chattanooga, Knoxville, Birmingham, Jacksonville, Columbia, Charlotte. Each a single street, a venue, a motel, a diner.
- **The national tour** (Ch VIII): winter Midwest arenas and hotels; the tour bus.
- **New York City** (Ch VII, VIII, IX): a few dense blocks of lower Manhattan (a showcase club, a punk dive on the Bowery, a hotel), and a midtown television studio.
- **Western North Carolina** (Ch IX): the small mountain town where the crash witness now lives.
- **The festival** (Ch X): the **Arbor Jam**, Glen Arbor Raceway, Schuyler County, New York, Saturday August 28, 1976. About 150,000 people. A racetrack infield, a scaffold stage forty feet high at the rigging and twelve feet at the deck, PA towers, a film crew, helicopters, mud, a motel on Route 14.

### 3.6 Period texture (1974–1976)

Details are threaded into scenes, never delivered as lectures. The canonical palette:

- **Economy:** stagflation; the 1974–75 recession; gasoline around 55 cents a gallon in late 1974, rising after; minimum wage $2.00 (1974), $2.10 (1975), $2.30 (1976); railroad cutbacks; Dalton-style carpet mills in the next county hiring; WIN buttons.
- **Politics:** Nixon gone since August; Ford's pardon still making men at Marlon's angry; Watergate jokes; the fall of Saigon (April 30, 1975) watched on a motel television; Georgia's ex-governor announcing a long-shot presidential run in December 1974 and being the Democratic nominee by the festival; the Equal Rights Amendment fight in the Georgia legislature (Riley's campus group); the Bicentennial everywhere in 1976.
- **Culture:** *Deliverance* (1972) jokes aimed at every mountain kid who goes to a city; *The Waltons* on Thursday nights; *Jaws* (June 1975); CB radio (1975–76); Evel Knievel; streaking; the Outlaw country records; progressive FM; the first punk records out of New York (1975–76); *Wish You Were Here* (1975) playing in the van once, without comment.
- **Music technology:** private-press 45s from custom plants; 16-track studios; tape echo units; phasers; fuzz boxes; unfashionable used Fender Jazzmasters in pawnshops; Rickenbacker 12-strings; Ampeg flip-top bass amps; Polaroid SX-70 cameras.
- **Radio:** 10-watt college stations (Class D) with a few miles of range; FM line-of-sight; many cars still AM-only, under-dash FM converters, portable transistor FM sets.
- **Drugs:** marijuana (Mexican, "Colombian"), psilocybin mushrooms picked from South Fork cow pastures, LSD in the period's typical lower doses, cocaine as expensive glamour, amphetamines from truck stops, Quaaludes at parties.
- **Mental health:** Georgia's Central State Hospital at Milledgeville, the largest in the country at its peak, whose name is a threat ("They'll send you to Milledgeville"); deinstitutionalization; outpatient psychiatrists prescribing Thorazine, Stelazine or Haldol with heavy side effects.
- **Faith:** Methodist, Baptist and Holiness churches; Sacred Harp shape-note singings with dinner on the grounds; Ellis's grandmother took him to them.
- **Law:** Georgia's drinking age is 18; possession of marijuana can still wreck a life; sheriffs know every kid's daddy.
- **Rural medicine and death:** funeral homes running ambulance service; headstones bought on installment.

## 5. The crash (canonical truth)

Revealed to the player in layers across all ten chapters. This is what actually happened.

**Thursday, April 12, 1973.** Ellis is sixteen, seven months into his license. He has been practicing "No Name" all winter. Marlon Pettit, who has heard him through the garage wall at Roy's, has told him he can play a couple of songs at Thursday picking night, 8:30. It is the first time anyone has asked him to play in public.

Wayne is working late on a derailment cleanup east of town and tells Ellis to pick Grace up from the Tolliver farm, where she rides the Tollivers' old mare, before he goes anywhere. Grace takes forever with the horse. It is raining. They leave the farm at 7:32 in Wayne's 1968 Chevrolet Impala.

In the car:
- Grace wants to come to Marlon's. Ellis says no: it's a bar, and Daddy would kill them both.
- She says she's heard him practice "No Name" two hundred times; she should get to see it. She still hasn't named it.
- He tells her to name it "Grace Made Me Late."
- It turns. She has found a Laurel City newspaper ad for a music store job circled in his room. "You're gonna leave." He says maybe. She says, "Just like her." She still writes to their mother; their mother doesn't answer. He tells her to stop writing to somebody who left. She says he's going to be exactly the same: gone, and he won't even call.
- He says the thing: **"When I go, I'm not coming back for you. You can stay here with him."**
- She doesn't answer. She turns and looks out the window. He looks at her.

That is the second in which the pickup comes around Tolliver Bend with its high beams on, over the centerline. Ellis swerves right. The right-side tires drop off the edge of the pavement (there is no shoulder, just clay and a ditch). He overcorrects. The Impala rotates and the passenger side hits the white oak.

Estimated speed: 43–48 mph in a posted 45. The pickup never stops and is never identified.

**After:** Ellis's left arm is broken and he has a concussion; he is pinned by the steering column but conscious. Grace is pinned by the door and the oak. She is conscious for several minutes.

A seed salesman named **Hollis Beam**, driving the same direction about 150 yards behind, stops, runs to the car, sees he can't open it, runs to the nearest house (the Hensleys') to call, and comes back. Before he leaves and after he returns he hears pieces:

> ELLIS: I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I didn't mean it.
> GRACE: Stop saying sorry.
> ELLIS: I'm not going anywhere. I'm right here. Stay with me. Grace, stay with me.
> GRACE: *(a sound that might be a laugh)* Liar.
> *(time)*
> GRACE: It's okay.
> GRACE: Go on.

Hollis tries to pull Ellis out through the driver's window to get him away from the car (gasoline smell). Ellis fights him: he has to stay where she can see him. Grace dies before the Pettigrew combination car arrives at 8:05. Deputy Lyle Pruitt arrives at 8:09.

**The report** (Georgia State Patrol, with Deputy Pruitt's supplement) records: wet pavement; speed at or near the limit; no alcohol; tires worn but legal; no mechanical defect; witness statement describing an oncoming truck "encroaching center line"; possible contributing factor: unidentified vehicle; driver statement: "we were arguing and I looked at her."

**What Wayne did:** Pruitt told him about the witness the next day. Wayne said there wasn't any other truck, the boy was showing off for his sister, and walked out. He was given a copy of the report in May 1973 and put it, unopened, in the box with the funeral bills.

**What Ellis remembers in October 1974:** rain, headlights, the oak, the sound, his arm. He believes he was going too fast because Wayne has said so, in one form or another, a hundred times, and a concussed sixteen-year-old's memory rebuilt itself around his father's version. He does not remember the argument, what he said, or that Grace spoke afterward. He remembers that he said "I'm sorry." That is all. He cannot hear the words without flinching.

**What Clara is:** the part of Ellis that did what he promised in the car. He said "I'm not going anywhere. Stay with me." Grace said "Go on." He stayed. Clara is him staying.


## 6. Principal characters

Each profile lists what the player may eventually see and a great deal the player never will. Field order: surface, wants/fears/wrong/hides/loves, background, texture, arc.

### 6.1 ELLIS WAYNE BLAKE

**Surface.** Eighteen in October 1974. Five-eleven, thin in the way of boys who work and forget to eat. Dark hair a month past needing a cut. A white scar through his left eyebrow (eleven stitches, 1973). His left forearm aches before rain; he rubs it without noticing. Work boots, jeans, a thrift-store brown corduroy jacket (Chapter VII: a secondhand black leather jacket he buys in New York because it's cold, which becomes the jacket on the poster). Oil in the cracks of his knuckles and under his nails until the week he quits the garage, and then, for a while, he keeps looking at his clean hands like they belong to somebody else.

Looks older when he's quiet and younger when he laughs. Laughs more than his reputation will suggest.

**Wants:** to leave Hollow Ridge (I–IV); to be heard (III–V); to be understood (VI–VIII); to know himself (IX); to go home (X).
**Fears:** that he isn't entitled to a future; that he'll turn into Wayne; that he'll turn into his mother and leave; that Grace, if she could talk, wouldn't forgive him; Milledgeville; rain on a two-lane road at night.
**Wrong about:** that his suffering must be made useful to be survivable; that forgiveness has to come from Grace; that leaving a place is abandoning the people in it; that Clara is somebody he knows.
**Hides:** the notebooks; everything about the crash; Clara (without knowing he's hiding her until Chapter V); the sentence he said in the car (hidden from himself until IX).
**Loves:** Grace. Tater. The band. Riley. Roy. Wayne, worst of all.

**Childhood.** Grew up on Cold Branch Road. His mother, Lorraine, sang Patsy Cline while she ironed and left in 1965 while he was at school; there was a note on the kitchen table for Wayne and nothing for the children. He was nine. He became half a parent to six-year-old Grace, and he has been bad at it and devoted to it ever since. His grandmother Clara Tate Blake took him to Sacred Harp singings from age six: all-day singings at country churches, dinner on the grounds, forty people in a hollow square facing each other and singing at full voice in open fifths, the loudest and strangest and most beautiful sound in his childhood. He learned the tenor line and how to hold a drone. When she died in 1970 he sang at her funeral and stopped going to church.

**Education.** Hollow Ridge High, class of 1974, a C average held up by shop and English. Mrs. Pardue (English) told him in eleventh grade that he wrote better than anybody she'd taught in twenty years; he has never told a living soul. He reads Louis L'Amour off the drugstore spinner rack and, from the Carnegie library, whatever Mrs. Odom slides across the desk without comment: Whitman, Dickinson, a water-stained Hopkins, James Agee. He knows the King James Bible by cadence and the Isaac Watts hymns by heart. He can't read music.

**Class and money.** Poor, in a house that works hard to look otherwise. He owns a 1964 Silvertone guitar with the amplifier built into the case (Sears, secondhand, $35), a 1963 Plymouth Valiant with one bald tire and an AM radio, forty-one records, a box of notebooks and a plastic horse that isn't his. He counts money twice. When he finally has money he gives it away almost compulsively (Roy, Wayne, Tully, Lynette's boy) because he cannot stand being paid for pain.

**Voice.** North Georgia mountain speech, used lightly on the page: word choice and rhythm, not spelling. "Reckon." "Might could." "Fixing to." "Hm" (Wayne's syllable; they both say it and neither knows). Few words, deadpan, sudden precise images when he forgets to guard them. Deflects with mock-formality: "Allegedly." "I'll take it under advisement." "That's a matter for the courts." Swears as punctuation. Gets quieter when angry, not louder, until he doesn't.

**Humor.** Deadpan specificity and total commitment to a bit. His ranked list of every boiled-peanut stand between Laurel City and Tannersville (fourth place is "a crime against God and peanuts"). He is very good with children, who find him hilarious because he talks to them like coworkers. He is funniest at 3 a.m. and when things are going badly.

**Things Ellis likes that have nothing to do with trauma or music:**
1. Boiled peanuts, ranked.
2. Engines: he can hear a bad lifter from across a parking lot and it makes him happy to fix it.
3. Georgia football on the radio on Saturdays, with Wayne, in separate rooms, both turned up.
4. Tater, Grace's fat old dog.
5. Night driving (not in rain).
6. The *Fireball* pinball machine at Marlon's. He's good at pinball and terrible at pool and will argue the second point forever.
7. Chess pie at the Starlite.
8. Watching storms from the porch.
9. *Gunsmoke* reruns.
10. Fried bologna sandwiches, the one thing he cooks well.
11. Drawing maps, badly. His maps are famous in the band for being wrong.
12. Going fishing with Cal and never catching anything, on purpose, eventually.

**Politics.** None declared. Distrusts suits and anybody who says "folks." Resents college kids who find him interesting; resents rich people; admires Roy's kind of decency. Never voted.

**Religion.** Doesn't believe and doesn't disbelieve. Afraid that heaven is real and Grace is in it and heard what he said.

**Sex.** One high-school girlfriend (Donna Kay Sisk, now married to a Carson, works the register at the Piggly Wiggly). Shy, not cold. Fame will offer him more than he wants. He mostly refuses, partly for Riley and partly because being close to someone terrifies him. (One exception, Chapter VIII, a kiss in a hotel bar, reported in a magazine before he can tell Riley himself.)

**Drugs.** Winstons, beer, occasional weed before the game begins. Marijuana with Riley (II). Psilocybin mushrooms (III, VI). Truck-stop amphetamines to drive at night (VII). Cocaine on the national tour (VIII). Prescribed trifluoperazine (VIII–IX), stopped in secret (IX). LSD on the day of the festival (X). He is never an addict in Dean's sense; his use is about reaching and escaping states, and increasingly about Clara.

**Death.** Thinks about it the way people in pain do: passively, not as a plan. "If a train came through right now that'd be all right." One notebook line in Chapter VIII makes it visible to the player. He is not trying to die. That distinction matters to the ending.

**Music.** By ear. The Sacred Harp drone and hollow square; his mother's Patsy Cline; Wayne's radio (Hank Snow, the Louvin Brothers); then Marlon's jukebox and progressive FM (Hendrix, Neil Young, Creedence, Sabbath, the Velvet Underground once, at a party, which rewired him). He tunes obsessively, loves open tunings and drones, writes melody first and words last, and hears songs as places rather than stories.

**Private habits.** Writes in pocket memo books from the Western Auto, numbered, in carpenter's pencil; never tears a page out. Feeds Tater every night (so does Wayne; neither knows; the dog is enormous). Turns anything of Grace's face down. Knows what day of the week it is at all times. Keeps the plastic horse on his dresser. Calls his Silvertone "Loretta" and won't say who named it.

**Physical habits.** Rubs his left forearm. Cracks his knuckles before playing. Stands with his weight on one leg. Smokes with the cigarette cupped backward in his palm (garage habit). Plays with his shoulder half-turned toward the band, not the audience: the hollow square. When he wants a song to end he turns all the way around to face Dean.

**Would never say:** "I need help." "I'm scared." "Grace" (for months). "I love you" (he says it once by accident, Chapter VII, while fixing Riley's car in the rain).

**Misunderstands about himself:** thinks he's cold; he's the warmest person in the band. Thinks he's stupid; he's the most observant. Thinks music is how he'll get away; it's how he stays.

**Arc in one line:** a boy who believes he isn't allowed a future builds one with three other people, is turned into a myth by a country, remembers his sister telling him to go, turns toward the living, and doesn't get the time.

### 6.2 GRACE CLARA BLAKE (November 2, 1958 – April 12, 1973)

The player never meets Grace alive except in memory: fragments of the crash, the cassettes, one playable afternoon in the summer of 1972 (Chapter IX) and a post-credits tape. She must feel like a real fourteen-year-old and specifically *not* like Clara.

**Who she was.** Loud. Bossy. Braver with Wayne than Ellis ever was: she'd tell their father he was being hateful to his face and then eat his biscuits. A horse girl without a horse, she rode the Tollivers' twenty-two-year-old mare, Dolly, every afternoon she could get a ride out Tolliver Road. Sang alto in the Methodist youth choir and harmony with Ellis on their father's Louvin Brothers records, which they both pretended to hate. Ran a pretend radio station on a Panasonic cassette recorder: "*WGRC, Grace Radio, the only station in Hollow Ridge that plays anything good*," with Ellis as the reluctant in-studio guest. Loved Dolly Parton, the Jackson 5 and the Carpenters, and thought Ellis's music was noise but listened through the wall. Wrote in green felt-tip. Stole one of Ellis's Winstons, smoked it, threw up, and made him swear not to tell. Named everything: the dog (Tater), Ellis's guitar (Loretta), the song he couldn't finish ("later"). Wrote to their mother every month and got two cards in seven years, which she kept in a cigar box.

**Her jacket.** A faded jean jacket with a small embroidered horse patch she sewed onto the breast pocket herself, crooked. It hangs in her closet. Clara wears an adult-sized version of it in every scene.

**Her room (preserved).** Horse figurines on the windowsill. A Dolly Parton poster and a David Cassidy poster she'd outgrown and hadn't taken down. 4-H ribbons. School papers with green ink in the margins. A Bible with pressed dogwood. The cassette recorder and a shoebox of tapes labeled in green. The cigar box. A horse sticker at a six-year-old's height on the outside of the door.

**How she differs from Clara.** Grace is sillier, meaner in a fourteen-year-old's way, more childish, less wise. She teases instead of consoling. She doesn't know how to forgive gracefully because nobody that age does. Clara is kinder, older and more knowing because Ellis needed her to be.

### 6.3 CLARA

Clara is a young woman of about twenty-one whom Ellis sees and hears and talks to, and who is not objectively present. She is not a ghost, and she is not presented as a symptom. The camera believes Ellis because Ellis believes Ellis.

**What she is (never stated in these words to the player).** A construction built by Ellis's mind from Grace's face, their mother's face at twenty, his grandmother's name, Grace's jacket, Grace's harmonies and his own knowledge, grown up to the age of the older sister he needed. She is the part of Ellis that couldn't leave Grace in the car. She is "Stay with me" given a body.

She is possibly also what the clinicians of 1976 would call something. The game never decides which. Complicated grief with hallucinatory experience, trauma, a family vulnerability to psychosis, concussion, sleep loss, and later drugs, fame and exhaustion are all present in the evidence. The characters don't know. The player doesn't need to.

**Appearance.** Dark hair to her shoulders. Lorraine's cheekbones. Grace's eyes (the Blake grey-green, which Ellis and Wayne also have). A small gap between her front teeth (Grace had it; Lorraine had it; it's in the wedding photo). The denim jacket with the crooked horse patch. A red flannel shirt that is Ellis's. Jeans. Boots. She sits with her feet up on whatever's in front of her and perches on things: crates, car hoods, railings, amplifier cases.

**Voice.** Low alto, the same mountain accent as Ellis, unhurried. Hums the harmony under whatever is playing.

**Personality.** Dry, teasing, fond, perceptive, protective; bossy like a big sister; loyal; occasionally cutting; later possessive, then frightened, then tender. She must be likable. Players should look forward to her scenes for four chapters.

**What she says and never says.** She says "stay," "come back," "don't," "take me with you," "keep going" (always about the car, never about leaving her). She never says "go on." She says "Liar" exactly three times across the game (I, V, VIII), always as a tease when Ellis claims to be fine. Only Grace ever calls him "El"; Clara slips and says it once (V).

**Relationships.** Likes Riley at first ("Girl's good."), then feels displaced as Riley takes the harmony part. Thinks Cal doesn't like her ("He doesn't like me." / "He doesn't know you." / "Exactly."). Is fond of Dean ("Dean looks insane."). Avoids Wayne.

**Evolution.**

|---|---|
| I–II | Friend and confidant. Appears at night, when Ellis is alone, after shows. Never enters the house while Wayne is awake. |

### 6.4 WAYNE BLAKE

**Surface.** Forty-five. Six-one, heavy hands, a railroad man's burned neck. Khakis, a Case pocketknife, reading glasses he hides in the truck. Camels, no filter. Early Times, two or three a night, never falling-down. A 1967 Ford F-100 with a cracked dash.

**Wants:** his daughter back. His son safe. Not to be the last Blake. To be forgiven without having to ask.
**Fears:** losing Ellis. Milledgeville. Being alone in that house.
**Wrong about:** that blame would make grief bearable; that hardness keeps a boy safe.
**Hides:** the unopened accident report (until IX); the witness he refused to believe; that he drives up Stony Knob to hear his son on the radio; a glovebox full of every newspaper clipping about The Blakes; the wedding photo.
**Loves:** Grace, Ellis, Lorraine still, the railroad, his truck, the Laurel City Engineers on the radio.

**Background.** Son of a railroad section man who died in 1958 and of Clara Tate Blake. At nineteen he drove his mother and his uncle Raymond to Milledgeville, and the family never said Raymond's name again. Went to work on the railroad at twenty. Married Lorraine Mathis, a Laurel Gap girl with a beautiful voice who was smarter than Hollow Ridge, and loved her loudly and badly; he never learned how to live beside someone that bright. After she left he told the children "She's in Ohio" and nothing else, ever.

**Work.** Section foreman on the main line between the two cities: a stretch of track, a motor car, a crew that was eleven men in 1965 and is four in 1974. On call for washouts and derailments. In 1976 his section is consolidated; he's offered a yard job in Tannersville or early retirement, and takes neither well.

**After Grace.** Kept her room exactly as it was. Stopped going to church. Keeps feeding Tater. Waits up on Thursdays, every Thursday, because the last time one of his children drove to Marlon's on a Thursday night in the rain she didn't come home. Pays $11.50 a month on her headstone. Keeps the report in the funeral-bills box, unopened.

**Voice.** Few words. Flat. "Hm." Sarcasm as armor. Calls Ellis "son" only when he's frightened. Once in a great while a story comes out and you can hear who he was: a funny, loud young man who could sing bass.

**How he loves.** He fixes things without being asked. He buys half of what's needed so it doesn't look like a gift. He leaves money where it can be found. He waits up. He drives up a mountain in the dark to hear his son on a 10-watt station and says a deputy told him about it.

**How he wounds.** When he looks at Ellis and remembers the hospital corridor, he reaches for the sentence that will hurt most. He knows instantly. He does not apologize, until Chapter IX, and then it doesn't fix anything, and he does it anyway.

**Would never say:** "It wasn't your fault." "I'm proud of you." "I'm scared." "Lorraine."

**Misunderstands about himself:** thinks his anger is about the crash. It is about Raymond and Lorraine and every person who left him, and the crash is just the one he can blame on someone who's still in the house.

**Arc:** from the man who needs it to be Ellis's fault, to the man who hands Ellis the report that says it might not have been, to the father who drives nine hundred miles because his son asked him to come, to the only Blake left.

**Likes:** the Laurel City Engineers on WLC-AM; Georgia football on the radio; creosote; his truck; Hank Snow; fishing Cold Branch alone; fixing the dog's fence that the dog never tests.

### 6.5 RILEY (Margaret Anne Riley)

**Surface.** Nineteen. Five-five. Long straight brown hair parted in the middle, freckles, wire-rimmed reading glasses she forgets to take off (onstage, eventually, a look). Peasant blouses, jeans, bare feet whenever the floor allows. A 1968 Martin D-18 that was her mother's; later a Rickenbacker 360/12 twelve-string.

**The name.** She was christened Margaret, after a grandmother. At home she is Maggie. At thirteen her softball coach called everyone by their last name, and "Riley" sounded like somebody who does things, so she kept it. Only her mother still says Maggie. Ellis learns this in Chapter IV and never uses it; in his notebooks, poems about her are addressed "M." She finds that out after he's gone.

**Wants:** to make one record that's still good when she's forty; to be taken seriously as a musician and a mind; to understand things; later, Ellis, and later still, to stop being defined by him.
**Fears:** becoming her mother (music kept in a church loft for other people's God); being "the girl in the band"; being ordinary; that she broke Ellis.
**Wrong about:** that altered perception reveals truth; that understanding someone gives you a say in what happens to them; that she has special access to Ellis.
**Hides:** her guilt about the mushrooms; that she knew about Cal first; how badly school is going; how frightened she is by Chapter VIII.
**Loves:** music, arguments, her mother, Hannah, Ellis, the band, cold rivers.

**Family.** Linwood, a Laurel City suburb. Father Tom teaches high-school chemistry, loves puns, and cries at graduations. Mother Joan is the Linwood Presbyterian organist who gave up a place at Oberlin Conservatory in 1949 to marry, and who plays Bach chorales alone in the church on Saturday mornings. She taught Riley voice-leading at the piano: *every voice has somewhere it wants to go*. Brother Tommy, twenty-four, Navy, Norfolk, the family's safe success.

**Education.** Tannersville College, partial scholarship, philosophy (Professor Landry's "Mind and Self" seminars), music minor. Brilliant, disorganized, beloved by teachers who are also exasperated by her. Works Saturday mornings at the college library circulation desk for work-study.

**Politics.** Liberal, argumentative, involved in the campus women's caucus lobbying the Georgia legislature on the ERA. Reads *Ms.* Argues hardest with people she agrees with. "Those are the useful arguments."

**Religion.** Raised Presbyterian; now a seeker: William James, Huxley, Alan Watts paperbacks. Loves church music, not church.

**Sex.** Had a boyfriend freshman year ("an argument with sideburns"). Matter-of-fact in a 1970s-feminist way. With Ellis it goes slowly because he goes slowly, and she lets it.

**Money.** Comfortable middle class and embarrassed about it around Ellis; pays for things quietly until she learns that quiet is its own kind of loud.

**Drugs.** Marijuana routinely. Psychedelics several times before the game ("I spent forty minutes convinced a tree understood me." "Did it?" "It did."). Regards them as instruments of perception, the way William James regarded nitrous oxide. After watching Ellis in Chapter VIII, a trip at a party turns on her and she watches herself handing him the mushrooms at the farmhouse. She stops. By IX she is the one saying: "It doesn't reveal anything. It just turns everything up."

**Music.** Theory-literate, careful, inventive; voices chords the way Joni Mitchell and Jobim do; hears harmony as a place to stand. Her twelve-string drones and her layered vocals are half of what makes The Blakes sound like nobody else. Her songs: "Sunday Clothes" (about her mother), "Parietal Hours" (funny), "Linwood."

**Private habits.** Crosswords in pen. Talks to a spider plant named Gerald. Writes lyrics on the backs of class handouts. Reads in the bathtub. Keeps a list of words she likes on the inside cover of every notebook.

**Physical habits.** Pushes her glasses up with a knuckle. Sits cross-legged anywhere. Tucks her hair behind her ear when she's lying. Bites the inside of her cheek when she's angry.

**Would never say:** "I did it for him." "I'm just the girlfriend."

**Misunderstands about herself:** thinks she's the free one; she is the most dutiful person in the band. Thinks leaving college is rebellion; it's her living the life her mother didn't.

**Likes:** Joni Mitchell, Laura Nyro, Nick Drake, Jobim; William James; Gerald; constellations; crosswords; secretly, every Dolly Parton song; swimming in cold rivers; arguing; pancakes (the only thing she can cook).

**Arc:** a philosophy student who thinks altered perception reveals truth watches perception destroy someone she loves, and becomes the person who insists on remembering the ordinary man under the myth, and then makes her own record.

### 6.6 CAL MERCER

**Surface.** Twenty-one. Six-two, lanky, hair short for 1974 ("You look like a Mormon," Dean says. "Missionary," Cal says. "What?" "Mormon *missionary*. Be specific."). Pressed work shirts. A Timex. A Parker Jotter in his shirt pocket, always. A 1966 Fender Precision and an Ampeg B-15 flip-top he rebuilt himself.

**Wants:** a studio, "the room where good work happens"; the band to last; to be taken seriously; someday, Theo, openly.
**Fears:** chaos; being blamed; being seen before he chooses to be; being the reason something fails.
**Wrong about:** that every problem has a cause and a fix; that watching closely enough keeps people safe.
**Hides:** Theo. That Marlon told him where Grace was going the night she died (VI). The "Clara?" in his ledger. His own grief, afterward, for a year.
**Loves:** the craft, his father, his mother's noise, Theo, and, eventually, all three of these idiots.

**Family.** Walt Mercer, fifty-four, Mercer Radio & TV on the east side by the rail yard; Signal Corps radioman in Korea; says almost nothing; shows love through competence. Dorothy "Dot" Mercer, fifty, a switchboard operator at the phone company for twenty-five years, talkative, the warmth in the house. Sister June, twenty-six, a nurse with two kids. Cal rents a room over a dry cleaner two blocks from Vale Music.

**Background.** Grew up soldering in the shop. Found his grandfather dead at the bench when he was twelve, called the ambulance, and then finished the radio the old man had been working on, because it seemed like the thing to do. (He tells this once, to Ellis, on a dock in Chapter IX.) Bass in the Baptist youth band at thirteen, paid gigs at sixteen, union card at eighteen, first-call demo bassist on the Row by twenty. Reads charts and the Tannersville number system. Knows who played bass on every record you name, especially the ones nobody credited: the Tannersville session A-team's bass player, James Jamerson, Duck Dunn, Carol Kaye. "Everybody knows who sang it." One year of electrical engineering at the state technical college; left for money.

**Sexuality.** Gay. Has known since fourteen. Not tortured about it: careful, in a time and place where careless gets you fired or hurt. In Chapter IV he meets **Theo Marchand**, the Lantern's sound engineer, and they begin something that lasts. His discipline is not a symptom of hiding; it predates everything. If anything, Cal is more at peace with who he is than Ellis is with himself, which is part of why Ellis's cruelty in Chapter VIII lands so hard.

**Politics.** Pragmatic union man. Voted McGovern in 1972 and didn't tell his father.

**Religion.** Raised Baptist, stopped at eighteen, no hostility.

**Money.** Saves, budgets, hates debt. Lends Dean money and writes it down. Resents being the band's accountant and cannot stop.

**Drugs.** A beer or two. Tried marijuana twice and hated not being in charge of his own attention. Nothing else, ever. The designated everything.

**Music.** Melodic, rhythmic, structural bass. Thinks in arrangements. Records every rehearsal on a Panasonic cassette recorder and listens back on the bus. Treats music as sacred craft and would be embarrassed to hear it described that way.

**Voice.** Precise, dry, minimal. Corrects imprecision. Answers emotional questions with technical ones ("Play the bridge again"). Says "I know" instead of "You're right." Says "No" as a complete sentence.

**Private habits.** Lists. Labels on every case and cable. Science fiction paperbacks (Le Guin's *The Dispossessed*, a man who belongs to two worlds and neither). Fixing clocks. Sunday mornings at Theo's. One cup of black coffee at a time.

**Physical habits.** Taps his pen twice before speaking. Holds his bass high. Stands still onstage. Rubs the bridge of his nose. Folds bills in half, facing the same way.

**Would never say:** "I'm afraid." "I was wrong." "I love you" to a man in public. "He's my friend" (until Chapter V, bleeding).

**Misunderstands about himself:** thinks he's the least romantic about music; he's the most. Thinks he doesn't need credit.

**Likes:** fishing with his father without talking; pool (quietly excellent); clocks; Ray Brown records; maps; Theo's fire escape; a correctly wired anything.

**Arc:** the man who believes every problem has a cause finds the one person he can't fix and learns, too late, the difference between noticing and saving. "He wasn't so bad."

### 6.7 DEAN HOLLOWAY

**Surface.** Twenty. Five-ten, curly dark-blond hair, a big grin with a chipped front tooth (a drumstick, age fifteen). Never still. Wears whatever's loudest: a satin baseball jacket, a Hawaiian shirt in December. Expensive boots, destroyed. A 1971 Chevelle SS his father bought him. A Polaroid SX-70 his father also bought him. A Ludwig kit he covets upgrading to a Vistalite.

**Wants:** never to be forty and ordinary; everything, all at once; the band to be enormous; people around him at all times.
**Fears:** silence; stopping; 4 a.m. alone when the high goes; his father's disappointment; boredom, which he thinks of as death.
**Wrong about:** that momentum is life; that stopping is dying.
**Hides:** that he's good at numbers; how much he uses; how scared he is; how much his father's opinion matters.
**Loves:** people, his mother, his sister Patty, Lynette, drums, the band.

**Family.** Richard Holloway, fifty, CPA, partner in a Tannersville firm that does royalty accounting for Row publishers: decent, formal, anxious, funny only by accident. Carol, forty-seven, warm, wry, sees everything, garden club, secretly loved Elvis. Patty, fifteen, sharp and sarcastic, worships Dean, will write Ellis a letter in Chapter VII. A house in Belle Grove with a pool.

**Background.** Happy childhood. A drum kit at twelve that his father bought thinking it was a phase ("He bought me the thing that ruined his plan"). Thrown out of Tannersville Academy for releasing a greased pig at a pep rally; finished at public school. Enrolled at Tannersville College in business at his father's insistence; by October 1974 he attends nothing and his father doesn't know. Formally on probation by spring 1975.

**Secret competence.** Numbers. He can do the band's arithmetic in his head faster than Cal can on paper, because he grew up at his father's elbow and has spent eight years pretending not to have learned anything. In Chapter VII, awake on cocaine at 4 a.m., he quietly fixes the band's ledger and makes Cal promise never to tell anyone.

**Politics.** Anti-authority by temperament, not ideology. Loves Ali. His draft lottery number was high and he's never quite known what to do with that luck.

**Religion.** Episcopalian by upbringing. "Christmas-and-funerals man."

**Sex.** Enthusiastic, charming, never predatory. Many flings. One real thing: **Lynette Crowe**, twenty-two, the night waitress at the Starlite, single mother of a four-year-old named Bobby, who is not impressed by him and is the only person who can make him sit still. She ends it when he's using (VIII). He goes back to her sober (IX).

**Money.** Spends like water, generous to a fault, cut off by his father in Chapter VII, borrows from Cal (ledger), owes Ellis four dollars from the Starlite in Chapter II and never pays it.

**Drugs.** Cocaine from a Tannersville College supplier starting in 1974; speed occasionally; alcohol; weed; Quaaludes at parties. Progression: fun (I–III) → functional, for playing and staying up (IV–VI) → medicinal, for hurt (V onward) → dependence (VII–VIII) → an ER scare with an irregular heartbeat in Cleveland (VIII) → bottom and sobriety, with Cal sitting up with him (IX) → terrified, sober witness (X) → recovery (epilogue).

**Music.** Explosive, inventive, dynamic; the best room-reader in the band; learns restraint from Cal and discovers it makes the chaos bigger. Writes one song, "Tomorrow Problem," a raucous joke that becomes a crowd singalong.

**Private habits.** Talks to strangers, every time. Collects matchbooks from every venue in his drum case (by 1976, hundreds; a history of the band nobody else kept). Takes SX-70 pictures of everything.

**Physical habits.** Drums on every surface. Bounces his knee. Jaw tight when he's high. Grins right before he says the thing that will get him in trouble.

**Voice.** Fast, loud, delighted with himself, generous with nicknames. Calls Cal "Dad" (which lands badly, eventually), Riley "Professor," Ellis "Blake" and, rarely, "man."

**Would never say:** "I'm tired." "Stop." (until Chapter X). "Dad was right."

**Misunderstands about himself:** thinks he's running toward life; he's running from time. Thinks he's the shallow one; he sees people more clearly than anyone in the band.

**Likes:** Evel Knievel; his Honda CB750; George Carlin records; Keith Moon, Bonham, Buddy Rich; pinball, frisbee, bets; the SX-70; Lynette's laugh; Bobby's drawings; driving too fast; junk food.

**Arc:** the man who says yes to everything learns to say stop, in time for himself and not for Ellis. "He was funny. Nobody writes that."

## 7. Families

**The Blakes.** Wayne; Lorraine (gone); Ellis; Grace (dead); Clara Tate Blake (grandmother, dead 1970); Raymond Tate (great-uncle, Milledgeville, whereabouts unknown after the 1960s); Tater (dog, 11 years old in 1974, obese). The Blake family plot on the east slope of the Methodist cemetery: CLARA TATE BLAKE 1901–1970; GRACE CLARA BLAKE 1958–1973; space reserved.

**Lorraine Mathis Blake Hubbard.** Born 1934, Laurel Gap. Beautiful voice; a year of junior college in Laurel City before she married. Left in 1965, in the spring, for Dayton, Ohio, where a cousin worked at the Frigidaire plant. Married Ray Hubbard, a GM machinist, in 1968; no more children. Sent the children cards for two years and stopped because Wayne never let her talk to them on the phone and because stopping was easier. Came to Grace's funeral, stood at the back, and left before the dinner on the grounds. Ellis saw her and walked the other way. In late 1975 she sees her son's face in a magazine and writes him a letter care of the record company. He never opens it. At his funeral, Wayne hands it back to her unopened, and she takes it. The game never reveals what it says.

**The Rileys (Linwood, Laurel City).** Tom, Joan, Tommy (Navy), Margaret/"Maggie"/Riley. Joan's Saturday Bach. Tom's puns. A dining table with a leaf that goes in at Thanksgiving and an extra chair nobody sits in.

**The Mercers (east Tannersville).** Walt, Dot, June (and her two boys), Cal. The shop smells like solder and dust burning off tubes. The radio on the bench is always tuned to whatever the customer left it on, as a matter of principle.

**The Holloways (Belle Grove, Tannersville).** Richard, Carol, Patty, Dean. A pool, a housekeeper named Mrs. Bea, a Christmas Eve party for sixty, and a son's drum kit in the finished basement that Richard soundproofed himself in 1967 and has never mentioned.

## 8. Supporting cast

**Marlon Pettit** (58). Has owned Marlon's since 1955. Widower (Irene, 1971). Knew Wayne from boyhood; Wayne was the loud one. Heard sixteen-year-old Ellis playing through the wall of Roy's garage and invited him to Thursday picking night, April 12, 1973. Has never said this to anyone and never will to Ellis. Gave him the Thursday slot a year later because he didn't know what else to do. Pays exactly what he says and slides back the difference without looking. Keeps a shoebox behind the bar (the first handbill, a setlist, the listener letter's envelope, the chalkboard eraser) and denies it. Tells Cal the truth about that night in Chapter VI. In the epilogue the chalkboard hangs behind the bar, never erased.

**Roy Cagle** (57). Hollow Ridge Auto & Service. Son Danny, killed near Khe Sanh in 1968; a framed photo of a nineteen-year-old in fatigues and a flag in a triangle case sit on the office shelf, and Roy mentions Danny exactly once in the game (Chapter VII). Wife Evelyn brings his lunch in a paper sack. Roy lost a child and chose tenderness toward a boy who wasn't his; Wayne lost a child and chose anger toward the one he had. The game never points at this. Roy says "Go on" as a verb for everything ("Go on, get out of here"; "Go on and take it"). Gives Ellis the 1962 Jazzmaster a customer left for a transmission.

**Tully Voss** (24). The stranger who jumps into Dean's car during the raid in Chapter I ("Who are you?" "Drive." "Good enough."). Tanner Valley boy, infantry in Vietnam 1969–71, drifting since. Big, quiet, funny in a dry, dark way. Turns up at Marlon's in Chapter II and starts carrying amplifiers because nobody told him not to. First roadie, then crew chief. Watches the fall of Saigon on a motel lobby television in Chapter V and walks out to the parking lot; Dean follows him and says nothing for an hour, which is the right thing. At the festival he is the first person down into the gap.

**Theo Marchand** (27). From Lafayette, Louisiana. Live-sound engineer at the Lantern, then the band's front-of-house engineer from Chapter VII. Midtown apartment with a fire escape, plants and a reel-to-reel he can't fix (Cal can). Warm, unafraid in private, careful in public. The one person Cal can talk to about Ellis without being the responsible one. At the festival he is at the mixing tower eighty yards out and sees the whole thing.

**Lynette Crowe** (22). Night waitress at the Starlite truck stop. Son Bobby, four; ex-husband in the Army in Germany and not coming back. Unimpressed by money and charm, which is why Dean can't stay away. Ends it in Chapter VIII when he's high at Bobby's birthday. Takes him back, provisionally, in Chapter IX, sober.

**Patty Holloway** (15 in 1974). Dean's sister. Sarcastic, smart, a better judge of people than anybody in her family. Ellis is unexpectedly gentle with her, and Dean doesn't understand why until much later. Writes Ellis a letter in Chapter VII about a song; he writes back. In the epilogue she's in college and has the letter framed.

**Professor Alan Landry** (52). Philosophy, Tannersville College, "Mind and Self." Pipe, tweed, a sense of humor so dry most students never find it. Takes Riley seriously enough to be disappointed in her. Never tells her to quit the band. Teaches, in Chapter II, a lecture on memory as reconstruction that Ellis happens to sit in on. Sends Riley a short note after the funeral.

**Hannah Greer** (19). Riley's roommate. Education major from Macon, practical and funny. Drifts from Riley as the band takes over; becomes a fourth-grade teacher. Reappears in the epilogue.

**Martin Keller** (22). Station manager of WTCR, the college's 10-watt station, serious about radio. Plays them first in Chapter III. By Chapter VII he's the overnight DJ on WLRC in Laurel City and is the one who plays "Still Here" at 11:47 p.m. and breaks them.

**Harlan Vale** (64). Owner of Vale Music. Played guitar in territory bands in the forties. Kind, dry, lends them the delivery van, tells Cal that people don't have parts.

**Deputy Lyle Pruitt** (44). County sheriff's deputy. Worked the crash. Pulled Ellis out after Hollis Beam couldn't. Checks on him the way small-town deputies check on boys whose fathers they know. Brings Wayne the State Patrol file in Chapter IX when Wayne finally asks.

**Hollis Beam** (58 in 1976). The witness. A seed salesman in 1973; retired to Sylva, North Carolina. Honest about how little he's sure of.

**Ada and Floyd Tolliver.** The farm on Tolliver Road. Their mare Dolly (died 1975). Ada sends Wayne a buttermilk pie every April 12 with a card. Nobody eats it. Tater does.

**Wesley Tate** (11). Hardware-store kid who asks Ellis "You got a band?" at the garage in Chapter I. Watches through Marlon's window. Wears a homemade Blakes shirt in Chapter VII. Is fourteen in the epilogue, playing a secondhand guitar on the Marlon's stage on a Thursday.

**Mrs. Odom.** Carnegie librarian who hands Ellis poetry without comment.

**Eddie Farris** (36). Laurel City promoter who books the Lantern, then the band's manager from Chapter V. Practical, mostly honest, fifteen percent, loves them in a professional way that becomes a personal way. Not a villain. On the last night, when Cal says they aren't going on, Eddie says, "Your call," and means it.

**Harold Vance** (43). Runs Southern Star Records in Laurel City. Loves strange records, has one gold album from 1971 and a boss who bought the label as a tax shelter. Gambles on them. Loses control of them in Chapter VIII when the major-label distribution deal arrives.

**Frank Dalton** (46). Engineer/producer, Dalton Sound on the Row in a former funeral home ("Best room in town. Very quiet clientele."). Ex-session guitarist bored of strings. Calm to the point of comedy. Produces *Borrowed Stone* and the unfinished second album.

**Gil Tarver** (35). Product manager at Monarch Records, the major label that takes over Southern Star's distribution. Smooth, tireless, calls Ellis "the franchise," writes the key-man clause. Not evil; has quarterly numbers.

**Dex Lundgren** (31). Critic at *Rave*, a Detroit rock magazine. Brilliant, funny, running on speed and adjectives. Writes "WHO THE HELL IS ELLIS BLAKE?" (VII), rides the bus (VIII), pockets a notebook page, publishes Ellis's poems (IX), writes the myth after the death. In 1996, sober and older, says on camera: "I made him up. Some of him. He let me."

**Nina Sorensen** (29). Critic at a New York weekly. Writes the first serious piece about Riley as a musician (VIII). Writes, after the death, a short column that says only true things, including "He was nineteen."

**Russ Mandel** (48). Host of NBC's *Late Hour* (1:00 a.m., one guest, a black set, cigarettes, long pauses). Intelligent, genuinely curious, and a broadcaster who knows good television when it happens.

**Dr. Harriet Lusk** (44). Outpatient psychiatrist at the Laurel City university hospital. Careful, honest about uncertainty ("I don't know yet what this is. Grief can do this. So can other things."), explains side effects plainly. Prescribes trifluoperazine and benztropine. Not a villain. Writes to Wayne after.

**The festival film crew.** A New York documentary outfit shooting the Arbor Jam in 16mm for a concert film. Cameraman **Joel Perlman** (30) and his assistant **Kit Adair** (22), who holds a battery-powered sun-gun light. Doing their jobs.

## 9. The band as a character

**Who does what.** Ellis brings words and melodies. Riley brings harmony and arrangements. Cal brings structure, business, gear and the van. Dean brings energy, people, bookings nobody asked for, and the idea that any of this is going to work.

**The van** (from Chapter IV): a 1966 Ford Econoline cargo van, faded green with a cream roof, a dent in the rear door, a transmission that weeps, a heater that works only on the passenger side. Bought for $400 from a man in the South Fork. It smells like something died in it because something did (a possum, found in Chapter IV). Seating: Cal drives. Riley rides shotgun with the map and a crossword. Dean sits on the bench behind the driver drumming on the wall. Ellis sits on the rear wheel well with a notebook. Tully (from Chapter II) sleeps on the amplifiers. By Chapter VI the ceiling is covered in matchbooks, set lists and SX-70 prints.

**Load-out.** Cal packs the van like a puzzle and nobody else is allowed to. Dean carries drums. Ellis carries amplifiers (garage strength). Riley carries guitars and whatever Dean forgot.

**Stage signals.** Invented across Chapters I–II and used by the player throughout:
- **"Stay with me"**: Ellis, off-mic. He's leaving the arrangement. Follow him.
- **Home**: Ellis turns all the way around to face Dean. Bring the song to its end. (Cal defines it in the Chapter II rehearsal because Marlon's has no monitors and Ellis can't hear anybody from the front.)
- **Cal lifts his headstock**: watch me, change coming.
- **Dean clicks his sticks twice**: hold.
- **Riley nods**: again, one more time around.
- **A turn**: what they call a mistake the whole band follows into something new. Cal names it in Chapter I.

**Rituals.** Before a show, all four touch the kick drum (Dean's idea; everyone mocks it; nobody skips it). After a show, the Starlite. Cal writes set lists on the back of whatever paper is nearest. Dean takes an SX-70 of every venue's sign and keeps every venue's matchbook.

**Rules** (Chapter II): 1. Show up. 2. Know the songs before the gig. 3. (Riley's) "I'm not dropping out of school because you three can't use a calendar." 4. (Assigned to Ellis by Cal) Don't rewrite songs halfway through them.

**The van promise** (end of Chapter V): no limousines (Dean); no treating each other like employees (Riley); no band decisions alone (Cal); nobody leaves somebody behind (Ellis). Every one of them is broken by Chapter X.

**Running jokes.** "Demand." / "That's a tomorrow problem." / "Allegedly." / Cal's face ("That's just his face.") / Ellis's maps / "Dad" and "Professor" / the four dollars / "One of us isn't a boy." / Dean's bench / the possum.

**Money.** Cal's ledger (a green clothbound accounts book from the Western Auto). Every gig: 20 percent to the band fund, the rest in four equal shares. Every loan recorded.

## 10. The music

### 10.1 Ancestry of the sound

The Blakes are ahead of their time, not time travelers. Their sound comes from specific places:
- **Sacred Harp**: Ellis's childhood. Open fifths, drones, singers facing each other in a hollow square at full volume. The root of their drones, their harmonies and the way Ellis faces the band instead of the crowd.
- **Riley's mother's Bach**: voice-leading; every voice has somewhere it wants to go.
- **Cal's session craft**: Motown and the Row; the bass as melody and floor at once.
- **Dean's rock**: Keith Moon's chaos, Bonham's weight, and, from Cal, the discovery that silence makes the hit.
- **Accidents of equipment**: a cheap Silvertone amp that breaks up early; a 1962 Jazzmaster whose tremolo arm lets Ellis bend whole chords out of tune and back (the "glide"); Riley's Rickenbacker twelve-string ringing like a room full of bells; a tape-echo unit at a Laurel City studio; Frank Dalton recording the room instead of the amplifier.
- **Progressive FM and a Velvet Underground record at a party.**

What critics decades later will call proto-shoegaze, space rock, alternative metal or post-rock, the band calls "the loud part" or, when Dean names it for an interviewer in Chapter V, "space death."

### 10.3 Song catalogue

Lyrics below are fragments for tone; full lyrics are written in the chapters where the songs are made.

- **"No Name"** (Ellis, winter 1972–73). E minor. About a kitchen light left on for somebody who said they'd be back. Written about his mother. The song he was driving to play for the first time the night Grace died. Grace said she'd name it later. Dean names it by accident onstage at Marlon's in Chapter I. *"There's a light in the kitchen that stays on all night / for somebody who swore they'd be back by nine."*
- **"Low Water"** (all four, Chapter II). Born from Dean tapping on a table. About the low-water bridge on the South Fork: when the creek comes up you can't get across, so you sit on the hood and wait. The first song that belongs to all of them.
- **"Ice Machine"** (Ellis/Riley, Chapter IV–V). A motel at 3 a.m. *"The only thing awake in Room 12 is the ice machine / and me, and you pretending."*
- **"Borrowed Stone"** (Ellis, Chapter V). About the coupon book on the kitchen table. The band thinks it's metaphor. *"There's a book in the truck, the pages torn out / one for every month since the rain."*
- **"Still Here"** (Ellis, Chapter V). The Clara song. The verses quote their first exchange in Chapter I almost word for word. The public hears a love song. *"You said the second one was better / I said nobody asked / you said I did, by playing it at all."* The song that makes them famous: strangers singing Clara's words back to him.
- **"Sunday Clothes"** (Riley, Chapter IV–VI). About her mother playing Bach to an empty church on Saturday mornings. *"She plays for the Lord at a quarter to eleven / and for nobody at all on Saturday."* Their 1976 hit single, re-sung by Ellis at the label's insistence; the press credits him.
- **"Parietal Hours"** (Riley). Funny, fast, about dorm curfews.
- **"Linwood"** (Riley). The suburb.
- **"Shape Note"** (instrumental, Chapter VI). Nine minutes of drone built on a Sacred Harp tune Ellis's grandmother sang. Frank records it in the dark.
- **"Stony Knob"** (Ellis, Chapter VI). The overlook; the radio signal.
- **"Tomorrow Problem"** (Dean). Raucous joke song; crowds shout the title back. Never on a studio album.
- **"New Skin"** (Ellis, Chapter VIII–IX). After the photographs. About giving someone birthdays she never had. *"I gave you a birthday every year you didn't get one / I gave you a jacket that was already yours."*
- **"Who Are You?"** (Ellis, Chapter IX). The last song. The final verse is never written; in the finale he improvises it, and that improvisation is what 150,000 people hear.

### 10.4 Records

- **Private 45** (Ch IV): "No Name" b/w "Low Water." 500 copies from a custom plant on the Row, $412 on credit. Hand-stamped paper sleeves.
- ***Borrowed Stone*** (recorded May–Aug 1975 at Dalton Sound; released Sept 15, 1975, Southern Star). Side A: No Name · Low Water · Ice Machine · Sunday Clothes · Borrowed Stone. Side B: Still Here · Linwood · Shape Note · Stony Knob. Then ten seconds of Dean shouting "That's ten!" (Ellis wanted nine songs, Dean wanted ten.) Peaks at #61 in spring 1976; #1 in October 1976, after.

## 11. Systems and rules

### 11.1 The switch (character-transfer grammar)

During story missions the narrative chooses who you play. There is no character wheel, no name card and no splash screen.

**The grammar.** The current character passes near another protagonist. Ambient sound narrows. The image catches with the faintest frame-stutter, like film jumping in a projector gate. Depth of field shifts from the current character to the other one. The HUD fades. The first character keeps walking; the camera doesn't. Control returns and you are someone else.

**Evolution.**
- Ch I: proximity switches between strangers (Ellis passes Riley; Riley passes Dean; a damaged amplifier is carried to Cal's bench).
- Ch II–III: switches along the band's new connections; in Chapter III the song itself carries the camera from radio to radio.
- Ch IV: switches mid-performance; the Monday relay (four lives resuming).
- Ch V: simultaneous journeys in two vehicles.
- Ch VI: **the switch as revelation**: Ellis → Riley in the same room; Clara vanishes.
- Ch VII: the Four Rooms: switches without proximity, across distance, the band apart.
- Ch VIII–IX: the camera begins to *catch* on Clara, stutters, and fails, snapping back to Ellis. A violation of the grammar the player has learned.
- Ch X: rapid switching through the final song, "different organs of one body"; then the last switch into Ellis; then, after the fall, the camera tries to find Ellis, stutters, fails, stutters, fails, and lands on Riley. Later it lands, for the first time in the game, on **Wayne**.

### 11.2 The Room (performance system)

Music is never a rhythm game. The player is not pressing colored buttons on time. The song plays; the player shapes it.

**Shared verbs:** intensity (how hard), space (when not to play), attention (who you're looking at), and the band signals.

**Per member:**
- **Ellis:** vocal phrasing (lay back, sit on it, push), dynamics, *reach* (extend or improvise a section: "Stay with me"), eye contact with bandmates and crowd, *home* (turn to Dean to end). From Chapter VI, the tremolo *glide* and controlled feedback.
- **Riley:** harmony choice (above, below, unison, or silence) chosen by listening to Ellis's line; chord voicing color; from Chapter VI, twelve-string drone.
- **Cal:** *lock* (sit with Dean or drag him back), *follow* (catch someone's deviation and turn it into a change), *floor* (drop out, or hold a single note under everything).
- **Dean:** tempo feel (push, hold), fills (when and whether), dynamics (drop out, explode), *read the room* (Dean alone sees crowd energy as a readable field).

**The crowd model.** No score, no meter. The room reacts with visible behavior: a woman stops stirring her drink; a pool player delays his shot; a conversation near the door dies; people move forward; someone sings along; people leave. Presence, not perfection.

**Mistakes are events.** A wrong chord, a late entrance or a rushed fill opens a short window in which another member (controlled or AI) can *follow* it. Followed mistakes become **turns**: new transitions, extended sections, sometimes the best moment of the night. Unfollowed mistakes are just mistakes, and the band recovers.

**Growth.** The band's responsiveness improves with shared hours (rehearsals, gigs, van time). In Chapter I the AI bandmates follow Ellis slowly and imperfectly; by Chapter VI they follow instantly; by Chapter X they follow him anywhere, which is the problem.

### 11.3 Clara rules

1. Clara never affects the objective world. Anything she "does" physically is either unverified by the camera or is Ellis's own action.
2. Only Ellis speaks with her. Other characters' lines in her scenes are blocked so they plausibly address Ellis.
3. She never appears in an objective shot, a photograph or a recording. Objective wide shots are framed (shadow, occlusion, distance) so her absence isn't noticed on first play.
4. Tater never reacts to her.
5. She never enters Grace's room until the end of Chapter IX.
6. She never knows anything Ellis could not know, consciously or unconsciously.
7. She never goes to the cemetery.
8. She never stays long in a room with Wayne.
9. The switch never catches on her until Chapter VIII, and then it fails.
10. She never says "go on."

### 11.4 The objective camera

Whenever the camera is with Ellis, it shows Ellis's perception. The game breaks this deliberately three times:
1. **Chapter V (midpoint):** the camera pulls back across the street while Ellis hugs Clara, and he is alone under a streetlight holding air.
2. **Chapter VI:** the Ellis → Riley switch at the farmhouse.
3. **Chapter X:** the crowd's view and the film crew's footage: Ellis pointing at an empty stage edge.

The aftermath uses the festival footage itself.

### 11.5 The notebook (Observe)

In quiet moments a small cue appears: Ellis has noticed something. Pressing Observe makes him stop, look, and write a line in his memo book. The player can read the notebook at any time. It looks like a collectible journal. It is the hidden-poet system. Every observed line is a line of Ellis's poetry, drawn from the place and moment where the player stopped. In Chapter IX, pages leak and are published without his consent; the excerpts the world reads (and praises as genius) are built from lines the player collected, with an authored fallback set for players who never stopped to look. The player helped write the poems the country mythologizes.

### 11.6 Money and time

- **Chapters I–IV: scarcity.** Every purchase costs another. Strings or the tire; food or gas; Wayne's rent. No morality meter. You simply can't buy everything.
- **Chapters V–VII: enough.** The band fund; per diems; the first real check.
- **Chapters VIII–X: too much.** Money stops mattering to Ellis and starts mattering to everyone else. The UI shows it less.
- **The calendar.** The HUD always shows the day of the week. Ellis always knows what day it is. Thursdays are quieter.

### 11.7 Player agency

Major story events are fixed. The player shapes the life remembered:
- how characters arrive emotionally at fixed events (what was said the night before, who you spent time with);
- optional conversations and optional revelations (what Ellis tells, and to whom);
- performance style and the band's shared vocabulary;
- side activities that leave marks (Dean's matchbooks and Polaroids, Ellis's peanut rankings, Cal's ledger entries, notebook lines);
- small callbacks: characters in later chapters and in the 1996 interviews refer to specific things the player did.

Dialogue is never a nice/neutral/asshole wheel. Choices are about timing (say it now, or hold), topic (what to ask about) and disclosure (how much Ellis lets out). Silence is always an option: hold the button and he says nothing, and people react to that.

## 12. Tone and dialogue rules

1. **Ordinary first.** For every scene about grief there should be two about food, money, weather, gear or nothing.
2. **The player must laugh constantly.** These are funny people, and sometimes idiots.
3. **People don't articulate their themes.** They interrupt, mishear, change the subject, lie, joke, trail off and apologize badly. When someone finally says something true and clear, it should be rare enough to land.
4. **No scene ends on a clever button line by default.** Let scenes run out, get interrupted, or end on a practical line ("Get in the van.").
5. **Dialect lightly.** Rhythm and word choice, not apostrophes.
6. **No horror grammar around Clara.** No stingers, no glitches, no chromatic aberration, no whispers.
7. **No ghost story.** Clara is never supernatural. Nothing she "knows" exceeds Ellis.
8. **Drugs are neither glamorized nor moralized.** They are fun, useful, beautiful, frightening and dangerous at different times for different people, and the game shows all of it.
9. **The death is not the meaning.** Ellis's life mattered before it ended. The game never implies his suffering was necessary for his art or that dying completed him.
10. **Banned phrases in authored prose** (see the anti-AI audit): haunting, raw, burning bright, ghosts and demons as metaphors, shattered, "something in his eyes," "the weight of," "silence hangs," "almost imperceptibly," "voice barely above a whisper," and the negation-pivot rhythm ("Not X. Y.") as a habit. "For the first time" is rationed.
