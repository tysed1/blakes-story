# THE BLAKES — Performance and Direction Bible

*Supplementary deliverable (V8). On the page the story is finished. What can still break it is performance, sound, camera and marketing. This document is binding for casting, voice direction, performance capture, audio, cinematics, music production, level art and marketing. Where it and a chapter disagree, the chapter's design notes win on story, and this document wins on execution.*

---

## 1. The three rules that protect everything

1. **Clara is played as a person, not a mystery.** She's a bossy older sister at a county fair: funny, impatient, affectionate, embarrassing. If she's ever played wistful, ethereal or knowing, the game is over by Chapter II.
2. **Wayne never cries on camera.** Not in the ambulance, not at the grave, not at the vet. The actor may come close once, in the ambulance, and must stop.
3. **The death is not beautiful.** No score, no slow motion, no gold, no crowd reaction shot. See §6.

---

## 2. Principals

### Ellis Blake (18–19)
- **Cast for:** a mechanic first and a singer second. Big hands, a real mountain accent (north Georgia, not Hollywood Southern), a face that's funnier than it is pretty. The actor must be able to sing live in a performance-capture volume. The finale verse is sung, not overdubbed.
- **Voice:** mock-formal when he's cornered ("That's a matter for the courts."), sudden specific images when he forgets to guard them, and mountain markers ("reckon," "might could," "fixing to") that thicken around his father. He's funniest when things are going badly.
- **Direct against:** brooding. The tortured-poet reading is the myth the game argues with. Play him as someone who's busy.
- **Key deliveries:**
  - "At least mine isn't somebody I keep in a hotel room" (VIII M7). This is Wayne's cruelty coming out of the son: instant, flat, exactly aimed, not shouted. The actor should study Wayne's "You got to keep yours" (I M9) and deliver it the same way.
  - "That's not— I'm not him" (X M8). Quiet, almost polite, into a mic that 150,000 people can hear. No anguish.
  - The open verse (X M8). Sung plain, a little behind the beat, and cracked where it cracks. It must not sound written.
- **Never:** a single tear on camera before Chapter VIII. Screaming. Addressing the camera.

### Riley (19–21)
- **Cast for:** a thinker who argues for fun. Glasses she pushes up with a knuckle. A real harmony singer who can find the third above anybody.
- **Voice:** argument form ("Define 'fine.'" "Name one. I'll wait."), crossword precision, words from the list inside her notebook covers. "Fair." and "Strong X." are hers alone.
- **Direct against:** the girlfriend who watches. In every late scene she has her own want: the song, her mother, school, the record. Her eyes should be on her own business at least half the time.
- **Key deliveries:**
  - "Stay there" (VIII M15), on a pay phone. Practical, not tender.
  - "I can't watch you choose her" (IX M10). Plain, with nothing held back and nothing performed.
  - "I'm carrying him" (Ep M2), to a funeral director. Polite, and not negotiable.

### Cal Mercer (21–23)
- **Cast for:** stillness. The best listener in the band. The actor should be able to fix something on camera.
- **Voice:** corrections and technical questions ("Be specific." "Which verse?"). "No." as a complete sentence. "I know." instead of "You're right."
- **Direct against:** the gay-best-friend register, and the scold. He's the floor the house stands on, and he's funny in one-word corrections.
- **Key deliveries:**
  - "She isn't real, Ellis" (VIII M7). A statement of fact, and the worst thing he's ever said. He knows both.
  - "Theo's mine. That's real. You don't get to use it" (VIII M8). Measured, not wounded.
  - "He wasn't so bad" (1996). The only line he gives the documentary. The pause before it is longer than the line.
- **Never:** crying except once, in the van (Ep M3), with the camera in the passenger seat looking at the side of his face.

### Dean Holloway (20–22)
- **Cast for:** a comic who can drum. Chipped tooth, restless weight. The jokes are load-bearing: the audience must like him too much to judge him.
- **Voice:** nicknames (Dad, Professor, Blake), overstatement, bets, "That's a tomorrow problem." "Exactly." is his alone.
- **Direct against:** the party-guy cliché. His using is sad in the gaps between the jokes, never in the jokes themselves.
- **Key deliveries:**
  - "Stop" (X M6). He has never said it. It should come out too loud, and he should be embarrassed by the volume.
  - The gap monologue (X M9), "I owe you four dollars." Fast, running on, and funny until it isn't.
  - "Of *course*" (Ep M4), laughing at a rock in a cemetery.

### Wayne Blake (45–47; 67 in 1996)
- **Cast for:** a railroad section foreman's body: heavy shoulders, bad knees, hands that don't know what to do when they aren't working. A voice with no performance in it.
- **Voice:** flat sarcasm, few words, "Hm." He says "Son" only when he's frightened. Never "It wasn't your fault," "I'm proud of you," or "Lorraine," except where the ambulance breaks them.
- **The "Hm." lexicon.** The actor records at least eight distinct readings, and the dialogue system selects them by context:
  - acknowledgement;
  - refusal;
  - amusement he won't admit;
  - hurt;
  - the vet's office, when he understands;
  - approval;
  - "I heard you";
  - the 1996 door.
- **Direct against:** the gruff dad who softens. He never softens on camera. What changes is what he does: the dome light, 88.9, forty miles an hour, the coffee can, feeding the dog twice.
- **Key deliveries:**
  - "I couldn't have it be nobody's" (IX M4). The truest thing he says. Say it looking at the window over the sink. It must not be offered as an explanation.
  - The ambulance (X M9). "It wasn't your—" stops because his throat stops, not because he chooses to. "Son." is the only thing that comes out whole. No sob.
  - "She carries" (Ep M2). Two words that end an argument.
  - "He could sing" (Ep M7), at a ballgame, looking at the field.
- **Never:** tears on camera. Raising his voice after Chapter III.

### Clara (21, as Ellis sees her)
- **Cast for:** the face of Lorraine Blake at thirty (the gap in her front teeth is essential), with a bossy country-girl comic energy. The same actress's likeness is used for the 1953 wedding photograph of Lorraine and the 1964 Olan Mills portrait, aged and styled accordingly. Players must be able to check the face in VIII M13.
- **Voice:** big-sister orders in mountain idiom ("Hush." "Eat something." "Quit that."), teasing, "your daddy" never "Wayne," and no send-offs. Her help lines (bible §11.8) are nags, not whispers: "Rent's Friday." "Look at that man's hat." "Not that way."
- **Mix and light her like anyone else in the room.** No reverb, no filter, no breathiness, no rim light, no soft focus, no sting when she appears or leaves.
- **Direct against:** knowing. She never knows she's a figment, never hints at it, never looks at the camera, never plays a line as if it means something twice. The double meanings are the writer's job, not hers.
- **Key deliveries:**
  - "Liar" ×3. A tease every time, like a sister catching a brother in a fib about homework.
  - "I made you." / "Does that make me less real?" (VIII M14). Curious, not wounded.
  - "Late, is what. Push your hair out of your eyes" (X M7). An order.
  - "Come on. They're waiting on you." Impatient and fond.

### Grace (14; heard on tape and in memory)
- **Cast for:** an actual thirteen-to-fourteen-year-old, recorded on period cassette equipment in a real bedroom. Loud, bossy, braver than her brother, bad at radio-announcer voices on purpose.
- **Key deliveries:**
  - "You're my brother. Unfortunately."
  - "Hi, El. It's me. You're probably famous now. … Don't get weird." Close to the mic. The giggle is real.
  - In the car (X M8) she's heard in fragments under rain: the laugh, then "It's okay. Go on." overlapping. Record them separately and overlap them in the mix. Never clean.

## 3. Supporting cast (one line each)

- **Roy Cagle.** Easy, loyal, a gas-station cap. "Go on, get up there" is said the way he says it about oil changes. The weight is the player's, not his.
- **Marlon Pettit.** Wipes spots that are already clean. Never makes a speech.
- **Joan Riley.** A church organist who gave up Oberlin. When she leads "Holy Manna" (IX M9), she's a different woman: loud, sure, beating time.
- **Tom Riley.** Puns delivered with total commitment.
- **Dex Lundgren.** Speed, adjectives, real charm. In 1996, sober and quiet. "I sold a lot of magazines" isn't a confession; it's a fact he's lived with.
- **Nina Sorensen.** Kind by economy. She reads "He was nineteen" off a clipping and puts it down.
- **Lynette Crowe.** Unimpressed, exhausted, funny. "Keep him alive till Tuesday" is a joke she needs.
- **Tully Voss.** Silent, competent, a veteran who doesn't talk about it. Sitting with Kit is the whole performance.
- **Theo Marchand.** Warm, dry, Cajun cadence. Never the object of a joke.
- **Hollis Beam.** Honest about how little he's sure of. "I couldn't make it out" with no drama.
- **Kit Adair.** Twenty-two, doing a job. Never framed as responsible. After the fall, her hands shake and she doesn't speak.
- **Dr. Harriet Lusk.** The best adult in VIII: direct, unhurried, and she declines to name a diagnosis because she doesn't know yet.

## 4. Music direction

- **The band.** Early-1970s Southern rock-and-folk, loud and unpolished: a Jazzmaster through a Twin with an Echoplex; a twelve-string over a drone; a P-bass on the floor; Ludwig drums. Reference the era's live recordings and demos, not its hit records. Nothing in the production can postdate 1976: no gated reverb, no Auto-Tune, no modern compression, no click track feel.
- **The loud part.** Every Blakes song has a place where the band gets quieter and quieter under Ellis's repeating note and then comes back. Protect it in every arrangement.
- **Sacred Harp.** Record real shape-note singers in a hollow square, loud, with the room. No choir polish. The Tabernacle, "Shape Note" and the graveside use the same approach. The class sings at full voice, not reverently.
- **The open verse.** Record Ellis's actor live at the take, with the band vamping. Pick a take with a real crack in it.
- **Riley's records.** *Occasionally Astonishing* (1979) should sound like a different, quieter artist. It's hers, not the band's.

## 5. Audio principles

- **Clara is mixed as a person in the room.**
- **The switch cue** (narrowing ambience, a projector-gate stutter, a short rumble) is identical every time it fires, including when it fails (VIII M4, VIII M10, IX, X M9). That consistency is what makes a failed switch read as grammar and not a bug.
- **No score under grief scenes.** Diegetic sound only: the refrigerator, the siren, the rain, the radio.
- **The radio is a world system.** It plays what the chapter says it plays. It never plays the Blakes in a scene the player is meant to be alone in, unless the chapter says so.
- **The car memory in X M8.** Rain under the whole verse. His voice under the rain. Her laugh with a word the rain keeps. "Stay with me" clear. "It's okay. Go on." low and overlapping. The mix never lets the player hear "Liar" cleanly.

## 6. Camera and light

- **No horror grammar.** Clara is never revealed in a mirror, window or reflection, and never closer between cuts. The camera never looks into a mirror with Ellis in it.
- **The objective breaks** (V M16, VI M9, X M9) are the only times the camera leaves Ellis's perception while it's with him. Each is a slow, unshowy move. No music sting. The embrace in V M16 continues while the camera pulls back.
- **The aging passenger** (VIII M14). Five looks, five ages. Subtle age makeup and VFX. The player should doubt the first change.
- **The fall** (X M8; see the chapter's binding spec):
  - real time, about 2.5 seconds;
  - the sun-gun's cold white, with no gold, bloom or flare;
  - handheld height, in his eyes, no cut to a wide;
  - no score, no scream;
  - the crowd still cheering at black.
- **Lenses and grade.** Period-appropriate. 16mm grain only for the film crew's footage and the cold open. The game's own picture is clean; the footage is the myth's picture.

## 9. Marketing guardrails

- **Never show the fall,** the gap, the sun-gun, the footage, or the stage edge at dusk. Never use the phrase "final performance."
- **Never reveal Clara.** She may appear in marketing only as Ellis's friend, in scenes with Ellis alone. No copy may say "the girl only he can see."
- **Never cut from the medicated porch to the Tabernacle,** or pair any medication imagery with a "come alive" beat. The game doesn't pick a side on medication, and the marketing mustn't either.
- **Don't sell genius.** No "tortured," "doomed" or "legend" copy. Sell the band: four people in a green van, a gorilla, a chalkboard, a kitchen.
- **No ELLIS work-shirt merchandise.** The epilogue is sad about exactly that.
- **Trailers lead with jokes.** At least half of any trailer's runtime is ordinary life.
- **Taglines that fit:** *Four people who didn't mean to be a band.* *Every band needs a floor.* *It's a tomorrow problem.*

## 10. Level-design guardrails (the fictional cities)

- **Laurel City** (west) resembles a 1970s Southern capital in massing and texture only. Its skyline, street grid, landmarks and signage must not be Atlanta's. No real street names except those the design keeps deliberately (Tenth Street, SR 400 from the user's map).
- **Tannersville** (east) has a river bend, a rail yard, the Row and the Tabernacle. The bend must not be the Cumberland's. The Tabernacle's façade must not be the Ryman's. No state is ever named for the Row.
- **Hollow Ridge** is the heart of the map. Every building listed in bible §3.2 exists and is enterable where the chapters say so.

## 11. Content notes (front matter)

Shown before the title screen, skippable, and available from the pause menu:

> *The Blakes contains depictions of grief and the death of a child; a fatal car crash; an adult character who experiences hallucinations; psychiatric medication and the decision to stop it; recreational drug use (marijuana, psilocybin, cocaine, amphetamines) and addiction; alcohol; an outing; a passing thought of suicide; and an accidental death on a stage. It depicts one person's experience of illness and treatment and does not recommend any course of treatment. If you're struggling, please talk to someone you trust or contact a local crisis line.*

At ship, localize the last line with region-specific crisis-line numbers.
