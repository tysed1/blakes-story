# V1 — Ingest and Autopsy of the Existing Draft

Source: the user's Chapters I–VII, the Chapter VIII setup paragraph, and the combined Chapters IX–X finale, as supplied in the project brief (2026-09-28). The repository was empty; this document is the first record of the existing material and a ruthless critique of it.

Verdict in one paragraph: the existing draft has an unusually strong instinct for the right kind of story. Its best moments are small (four dollars slid across a bar, a blanket left folded instead of spread, a ledger margin reading "Clara?"), and its core mechanic (the narrative choosing who you play, with a frame-stutter handoff) is original and thematically loaded. Its failures are structural: a timeline that cannot hold its own ages, a mystery that spends its best reveals in Chapters V–VII and coasts afterward, a missing Chapter VIII, a Wayne who fights Ellis in the same shape eight times, fame that arrives impossibly fast, and prose that repeatedly explains its own meaning. The rebuild keeps nearly every great small moment and replaces most of the load-bearing structure.

## 1. Story bible extracted from the draft (as written)

**Timeline as written:** Ch I Oct 1974 · Ch II Oct 1974 · Ch III Nov 1974 · Ch IV Nov–Dec 1974 · Ch V Jan–Mar 1975 · Ch VI spring/early summer 1975 · Ch VII summer–autumn 1975 · Ch VIII (never written) · Ch IX–X "late summer 1975".

**Characters as written:** Ellis Blake (18, singer/guitarist, Hollow Ridge mechanic), Grace (younger sister, dead), Clara (hallucinated), Wayne (father), Riley (college student), Cal Mercer (21, bass), Dean Holloway (20, drums), Marlon (tavern), Roy (garage), Mr. Vale (music shop), Hannah (roommate), Professor Landry, Eddie Harlan (promoter), Harold Vance (Southern Star Records), Frank Dalton (engineer), a deputy, a witness, Riley's mother, Dean's parents, Cal's father.

**Locations as written:** Hollow Ridge, Tannersville (campus city), Laurel City (older railroad city), plus Atlanta, Athens, Chattanooga, Birmingham, Nashville, Jacksonville, New York, an upstate New York festival. (Superseded by the user's map: Laurel City is the Atlanta analog, Tannersville the Nashville analog; see WORKLOG.)

**Songs as written:** "No Name," a collaborative rehearsal song (unnamed), "Borrowed Stone," "Sunday Clothes," "Still Here," "New Skin," "Who Are You?" Album: *Borrowed Stone*.

**Objects as written:** diner Polaroid, listener letter, Grace's cassette, notebooks, Roy's guitar, Lantern poster, van, Cal's ledger, the accident report, Ellis's list.

**Mysteries as written:** Who is Clara → what is Clara → Clara is built from Grace → what happened in the crash → what did Grace say.

## 2. Contradictions and continuity failures

These are not matters of taste. Each one breaks the story if left in.

| # | Problem | Where | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | Grace is "a little girl" in the photo, her room holds "a child's" objects, and Wayne sits in "a child's bedroom years after she died." Later she dies at 15 with Ellis at 17. | I, VI, VII, IX | The crash would be about a year before the game, not "years." |
| C2 | Clara is "early twenties." If Grace died at 15 a year or two ago, she would be 16–17 now. The draft has Grace "aging into" Clara across 15→19. | I, VI | The core explanation of Clara ("what Grace would look like now") is arithmetically false. |
| C3 | Ellis was "seventeen" at the crash and is 18 now, but his notebooks, Clara and the family's silence imply years. | VI, VII | Same as C1. |
| C4 | The mother "left" and "died" in the same flashback ("I know Mom left" / "I wish you would've died instead of Mom"). Elsewhere she's simply absent. | VII, IV, V | The pivotal cruel line depends on an undecided fact. |
| C5 | The schizophrenic relative is "my mother's uncle" (III) and then Wayne's mother's brother (VI). | III, VI | Clue contradiction. |
| C6 | Chapter VII runs "summer–autumn 1975" and ends in New York; Chapters IX–X are "late summer 1975." | VII, IX | Time runs backward. |
| C7 | Chapter VIII does not exist. | — | The fracture chapter, where the band should break, is missing. |
| C8 | *Borrowed Stone* is released in August 1975, and Ellis plays to 100,000+ people the same month as a near-headliner. | VII, X | Implausible, and it cheats the "earned success" requirement. |
| C9 | Dean has the diner Polaroid at the festival; after Ellis dies, Cal finds it in Ellis's belongings. | X | Object continuity. |

## 3. Chapter-by-chapter autopsy

Rating key: **E** exceptional (preserve) · **G** good (keep only if it earns its place) · **W** weak (rewrite) · **X** cut.

### Chapter I — Before the Noise

- **E** Cold open: a guitar being tuned in the dark, sixteen people, nobody knowing history is occurring.
- **E** Marlon counts the money, Ellis counts it again, Marlon slides the difference back "without looking." Human, funny, and it tells you who Marlon is in six lines.
- **E** Clara's introduction: no horror language, just "Second one was better." The instruction that players must *like* her is the most important directing note in the draft.
- **E** "He come?" / "No." The player assumes she means the father. The replay meaning is richer than the draft realized (see rebuild).
- **E** Wayne: "You're real good at dealing with cars after the fact." One line carries the whole backstory.
- **E** The closed door, the sticker low on it, Wayne's "Don't" said in fear rather than anger.
- **E** The blanket left folded beside Wayne instead of put over him. Perfect characterization through a withheld action.
- **E** The notebook: THE BLAKES written, crossed out, not torn out.
- **E** The character-switch grammar (proximity, sound narrowing, frame stutter, HUD drop). Genuinely original.
- **E** Dean's loving family: no secret abuse, rebellion against a comfortable cage. "Choose the cage."
- **E** Cal's father: "Still buzzing?" / "Nobody hears it." / "You do." The thesis of Cal in three lines.
- **E** Cal following Ellis's wrong chord so the mistake becomes a transition. This is the game's central metaphor and it needs a name (rebuild: "a turn").
- **E** "This is nothing." / "Not yet."
- **E** The deputy who never acknowledges Clara; "Everybody knows everything." / "Not everything."
- **G** Riley's philosophy seminar ("Maybe reality's the event"). Too thesis-shaped for a first chapter. Rebuild uses a funnier, more oblique seminar.
- **G** The party/raid set piece. Fun, but it needs a reason to exist beyond fun. Rebuild gives it one: the stranger in Dean's car becomes a recurring character, and he is the first person in the game asked "Who are you?"
- **W** "You got to keep yours" and "cars after the fact" land within the same chapter. Both are strong, but together they make Wayne a monster before the player has seen him do one kind thing. Rebuild: keep both, and give Wayne one wordless kind act between them.
- **W** The Coke deduction (C15).
- **X** Authorial commentary embedded in the scene text ("That matters." "Nobody knows history is occurring. That matters."). Useful to the writer; poison to a team reading the doc. Rebuild moves it into flagged design notes.

## 4. Patterns that read as AI-written (to purge in the rebuild)

1. **One-line-paragraph cascades** ("Tiny. / Tiny. / Tiny."). Occasional use is a rhythm; constant use is a tic.
2. **Self-annotation**: "That matters." "This is important." "On replay, this becomes unbearable." Every scene tells the reader how to feel about it. Rebuild confines this to clearly labeled design notes.
3. **Symmetrical sparring**: "You're high." / "You're high." "Both can be true." Fine once; the draft does it constantly.
4. **"Nothing." / "Say it."** repeated in almost every Wayne and Clara scene.
5. **"For the first time"** used dozens of times.
6. **Every character is articulate about theme** (Landry, Clara, Riley, Cal, Dean, Wayne all say the thesis aloud at some point).
7. **Scenes ending on quotable button lines.**
8. **Negation-pivot constructions** ("Not X. Y." "Not romance. Recognition.") everywhere.
9. **Three-item lists** as the default rhythm.

## 5. Gameplay gaps

- Long stretches are cutscene-dialogue with a "drive here" between. Too few missions where the story happens *through* verbs: fixing, carrying, driving, playing, choosing what to buy, waiting.
- The performance system is described well in principle and then left behind. It needs named verbs per character, a readable crowd model without a score, and a shared vocabulary (stay with me / turn / home) that the finale can pay off *mechanically*.
- The open world's evolution is described in passing. It needs a per-chapter change list (posters, radio rotation, NPC recognition, prices, vehicles, Marlon's walls).
- Side content is a list of activities without character function.
- There are no mechanics that carry grief after the death except the failed switch. Rebuild adds a playable Wayne.

## 6. Historical-authenticity gaps

- Cassette sales (C14), AM car radios (C11), $4 of Cokes (C15).
- Real Atlanta/Nashville (superseded by the map).
- A 100,000-person festival slot within months of a debut (C8).
- No 1974–76 texture beyond a Watergate joke: no WIN buttons, no fall of Saigon, no Carter, no ERA fight, no *Deliverance* jokes aimed at mountain kids, no punk in New York, no deinstitutionalization, no funeral-home ambulances, no Sacred Harp, no railroad decline. The rebuild threads these in without making anyone a history lecture.

## 7. What the draft does better than most stories of its kind

It refuses to make Wayne a villain, Dean a cautionary tale, Riley a muse or Cal a scold. It understands that the saddest objects should start as jokes. It knows the audience must want the fame before it hurts. It understands that the character-switch grammar can be broken as an act of grief. The rebuild's job is to build a frame strong enough to carry those instincts for fifty hours.
