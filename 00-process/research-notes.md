# Research Notes: Period Fact-Check (Oct 1974 – Aug 1976, plus 1996 epilogue)

Verdict key: **Confirmed** = claim holds as stated. **Corrected** = claim wrong or materially incomplete (see fix). **Uncertain** = could not pin down from reliable sources; best estimate given.

Items marked with ⚠ are ones the lead writer should look at (the claim is false, or the framing needs to change).

---

## 1. Georgia drinking age 1974–76
**Confirmed.** Georgia lowered the drinking age to 18 in 1972 (part of the wave that followed the 26th Amendment). It went to 19 in 1980, 20 in 1985, and 21 in 1986. So 18-year-olds could legally drink throughout 1974–76.
*Source: Wikipedia, "History of alcohol minimum purchase age by U.S. jurisdiction"; AJC Q&A; New Georgia Encyclopedia (1980 petition).*

## 2. Georgia marijuana possession penalty, 1974–75
**Confirmed: misdemeanor for under 1 oz, from 1974.** A 1974 act (Ga. L. 1974, p. 221) made possession of 1 oz or less a misdemeanor, punishable by up to 12 months in jail and/or a $1,000 fine. More than 1 oz is a felony, 1–10 years. That line is still in O.C.G.A. § 16-13-30 today. Before 1974 the penalties were harsher. A small-amount bust in Oct 1974 or later is a misdemeanor. A larger stash is a felony.
*Source: Justia, O.C.G.A. § 16-13-30 history note; NORML Georgia penalties page.*

## 3. Gasoline prices
**Confirmed (numbers).** These are U.S. city averages for leaded regular, per gallon (BLS series APU000074712):
- **October 1974: 53.4¢.** It was down slightly from a mid-1974 peak of about 55.8¢. The embargo-era gas lines were over by spring 1974.
- **June 1976: 59.2¢; July: 59.9¢; August 1976: 60.3¢.** Unleaded ran about 2–3¢ more.
- The national 55 mph speed limit was in force from January 1974. This matters for road-trip scenes.
*Source: FRED/BLS series APU000074712; EIA Table 5.24.*

## 4. Federal minimum wage
**Confirmed.** It was **$2.00** from May 1, 1974, **$2.10** from Jan 1, 1975, and **$2.30** from Jan 1, 1976 (nonfarm workers). Farmworkers had lower phased rates.
*Source: U.S. Dept. of Labor, "History of Federal Minimum Wage Rates."*

## 5. Effects gear
- **Roland RE-201 Space Echo: Corrected/Uncertain ⚠.** Released in Japan in 1974. Roland had no U.S. subsidiary until 1978. Beckmen Musical Instruments became Roland's distributor for the **western** U.S. only in **1976**, and Roland Corp US was formed in 1978. A Space Echo in a small north-Georgia band's hands in 1974–75 would be a rare import. Late 1976 is more plausible. The period-correct American tape echo is the **Maestro Echoplex**.
- **Maestro Echoplex: Confirmed as widely available.** The solid-state EP-3 was sold from 1970 on. The EP-4 (with an LED meter and tone controls) came in the mid-'70s and was sold alongside it. Both were built by Market Electronics (Ohio) for Norlin's Maestro brand through 1979.
- **MXR Phase 90: 1974.** Keith Barr designed it and small runs appeared from 1972–73. Real production began in late 1973, and it launched MXR in 1974. The first units were orange boxes with "script" logos.
- **Electro-Harmonix Big Muff π: 1969.** The "triangle" knob-layout version dates from 1969–73.
- **EBow: 1976 ⚠.** Invented by Greg Heet and introduced at the **summer 1976 NAMM show in Chicago**. Jerry Garcia was the first buyer. It did not exist commercially before mid-1976.
*Sources: Wikipedia (Roland Space Echo; Echoplex; MXR Phase 90; Big Muff; EBow); Sound On Sound "History of Roland"; Reverb.com.*

## 6. Used Fender Jazzmasters in the mid-'70s
**Confirmed.** Jazzmasters were out of fashion and sat in pawn shops. Several guitar histories say you could get one for **under $100** second-hand by the mid-'70s. Tom Verlaine (Television) paid about **$150** for his in the early '70s "because nobody else wanted one." Elvis Costello and the new-wave players made them hip again from about 1977.
*Source: New Atlas "All That Jazzmaster"; Stringjoy history; Wikipedia (Tom Verlaine). Treat the dollar figures as approximate and anecdotal.*

## 7. Polaroid SX-70
**Confirmed with a date nuance.** Land announced it in April 1972. It test-launched in Miami in late 1972 and rolled out nationally in 1973. The launch price was **$180**. A cheaper plastic-bodied **Model 2 (1974)** cost about $150. A 10-shot film pack cost about **$6.90**. The print ejects automatically and **develops in ordinary light**, emerging over a few minutes, and is fully developed in about **10 minutes**. Nothing to peel, no timing.
*Source: Wikipedia "Polaroid SX-70"; Camera-wiki; mikeeckman.com.*

## 8. FM range, college stations, car radios
- **10-watt Class D college stations: Confirmed.** These were common until 1978, when the FCC stopped licensing new Class D stations and pushed them to at least 100 W. Range was typically a few miles. Georgia Tech's WREK signed on in 1968 at 10 W with roughly a **10-mile radius** (a good-case figure with a decent antenna). Vanderbilt's WRVU began in 1971 on a 10 W permit.
- **FM in cars: Confirmed.** FM car radios first appeared around 1963 and were an **extra-cost option**. AM-only was standard on most ordinary cars through the 1960s and still common into the mid-'70s. AM/FM spread through the '70s, and FM listening passed AM only in **1979**.
- **Under-dash FM converters: Confirmed.** Audiovox, Motorola, Kraco and others sold boxes that fed FM through the car's AM set. Motorola made them from the late '60s, and installers fitted them through the mid/late '70s.
*Sources: FCC docs / insideradio.com (Class D 1978); WREK history; WRVU Wikipedia; Wikipedia "FM broadcasting in the United States"; Nuts & Volts; collector listings for Audiovox/Motorola converters.*

## 9. Atlanta radio
- **WKLS 96.1 "96 Rock": Confirmed, 1974.** It flipped from an older easy/big-band format to album rock in 1974 as "96 Rock," reportedly starting with "Baba O'Riley." It was Atlanta's first major commercial FM rock station. The exact month is not confirmed.
- **WRAS 88.5 (Georgia State): Corrected on details ⚠.** It signed on **January 18, 1971** at **19,500 watts** ERP, not a 10-watt station, so it covered much of metro Atlanta. It was student-run with album/progressive programming, and its slogans were "**The Stereo Alternative**" and "Stereo Odyssey." **The "Album 88" name dates from 1982.** Do not use "Album 88" in 1974–76 scenes.
- **WREK 91.1 (Georgia Tech): Confirmed.** On air March 25, 1968 at 10 W, noon to midnight. It was student-engineered with eclectic, non-commercial programming, and its power rose over later decades.
*Sources: tangentsunset.com Atlanta Radio History; insideradio; Wikipedia "WRAS (FM)"; GSU history pages; WREK history page.*

## 10. Atlanta venues 1974–76
- **Richard's:** 931 Monroe Dr NE (Midtown, across from Grady High), run by Rich Floyd and Richard Bryan. It was the key rock club of 1973–74: the Stooges' week-long stand in Oct 1973, the New York Dolls, Lynyrd Skynyrd (July and Oct 1973), Roy Buchanan (Mar 1974). **Closing date and capacity are uncertain** (it was likely gone by about 1975–76).
- **Alex Cooley's Electric Ballroom: Confirmed.** Opened **March 13, 1974** in the Grand Ballroom of the Georgian Terrace Hotel (663 Peachtree St). Capacity was about **1,100–1,300**. It ran to 1979 and then became the Agora.
- **Great Southeast Music Hall: Confirmed.** Opened **Oct 30, 1972** in the Broadview Plaza shopping center (2581 Piedmont Rd, near Lindbergh). Capacity was about **500–530**, with a laid-back feel and beer by the bucket. The Sex Pistols' U.S. debut was there on Jan 5, 1978.
- **The Omni: Confirmed.** Opened **Oct 14, 1972**, seating about **16,000** (16,378 for basketball). Elvis sold it out repeatedly from 1973 to 1976.
- **Fox Theatre: Confirmed with nuance.** 4,665 seats. In 1974 Southern Bell planned to demolish it for an office tower. Atlanta Landmarks' "Save the Fox" campaign (about $3M) and a land swap saved it in 1974–75, and it reopened as a live venue in 1975. Fundraising and debt continued afterward. Lynyrd Skynyrd recorded *One More from the Road* there on **July 7–9, 1976** and donated part of the proceeds to Save the Fox.
*Sources: Wikipedia (Electric Ballroom; Omni Coliseum; Fox Theatre); sealplace.blogspot (Music Hall); Creative Loafing (Richard's, Iggy); Atlanta Magazine; AJC; Georgia Trust.*

## 12. Late-night TV
- **Tomorrow (Tom Snyder), NBC: Confirmed.** It ran Oct 1973 – Dec 1981, **Monday–Thursday about 1:00–2:00 a.m.** after Carson. The format was one-on-one interviews with **no studio audience**, with Snyder smoking and conversational. The John Lennon interview aired April 28, 1975.
- **The Midnight Special, NBC: Confirmed.** It aired **Fridays 1:00–2:30 a.m.** (1973–81) in the Tomorrow slot. It had mostly live performances (unusual when most TV was lip-synced), guest hosts, and Wolfman Jack as announcer/host.
- **Don Kirshner's Rock Concert: Confirmed, with a correction:** it was **syndicated**, not network, so the day and time varied by local station, usually weekend late night. It premiered Sept 27, 1973 (Rolling Stones, Doobie Brothers) and featured real live performances.
- **Saturday Night Live:** premiered **Oct 11, 1975** on NBC as "**NBC's Saturday Night**" (the "Live" title came later). George Carlin hosted, with Billy Preston and Janis Ian as musical guests.
*Sources: Wikipedia (Tomorrow Show; Midnight Special; Don Kirshner's Rock Concert; SNL season 1).*

## 15. Big festivals
- **Summer Jam at Watkins Glen, July 28, 1973: Confirmed.**
  - About **600,000** attended, a Guinness record. Only about 150,000 **$10** tickets were sold, so it became a de facto free show.
  - Headliners: Allman Brothers, Grateful Dead, The Band.
  - Crowds arrived during the July 27 soundchecks. Traffic jammed for miles and people walked 5–8 miles in.
  - Bill Graham's FM Productions did the staging, lights and a **50,000-watt** PA.
  - It used an early **digital delay** setup: Eventide units fed speaker towers at about **200, 400 and 600 ft** from the stage, delayed about 175/350/525 ms.
  - A thunderstorm hit during The Band's set (Garth Hudson improvised "Too Wet to Work").
  - One death: a skydiver whose flare ignited his suit. Two teenage hitchhikers went missing.
  - **Stage height: not found (Uncertain).**
- **California Jam, April 6, 1974 (Ontario Motor Speedway): Confirmed.**
  - About 168,000–250,000 attended. Tickets were **$10 advance / $15 gate**.
  - **The band stages ran on rails** (two stages on about 600 ft of track): one rig was set up while the other played, then rolled into place, so the show ran *ahead* of schedule.
  - PA was about 54,000 W RMS with two **50-ft speaker towers**.
  - ABC's *In Concert* televised it. Ritchie Blackmore smashed an ABC camera (about $10,000 damage).
- **Summer 1976 U.S. outdoor festivals** (fewer and smaller than 1969–74; states and counties had passed mass-gathering laws):
  - **Stompin' 76**, **Aug 6–8, 1976**, near Galax, Virginia. More than 100,000 came to a bluegrass/folk/blues bill (Bonnie Raitt, Earl Scruggs, Doc Watson, John Prine, Nitty Gritty Dirt Band). Tickets were $12–15. It brought traffic jams on I-77, walks of up to 11 miles, flash flooding, Pagans bikers and moonshine vendors. The county passed festival laws afterward.
  - **Sunday Break**, Austin: May 2, 1976 (about 56,000). Sunday Break II, Labor Day weekend (about 28,000 paid).
  - Knebworth (Rolling Stones, Skynyrd) was **Aug 21, 1976**, in England.
- **⚠ New York note:** after Woodstock and Watkins Glen, New York regulates "mass gatherings" of **5,000+ people lasting 24+ hours** under a state health-department permit system (Sanitary Code Subpart 7-4) with sanitation, medical and water rules. A huge late-Aug 1976 upstate festival would need a state permit and would face local resistance. That is story friction, not a blocker. The exact year Subpart 7-4 was adopted was not pinned down.
*Sources: Wikipedia (Summer Jam; California Jam; Stompin 76); PS Audio "Copper" Summer Jam memoir; Billboard (Woodstock 50 permitting column); Cornell LII 10 NYCRR 7-4; Michael Corcoran (Sunday Break).*

## 16. Central State Hospital, Milledgeville
**Confirmed.** By the 1960s it had about **12,000 patients** (some sources say 13,000), 200+ buildings and about 1,750–2,000 acres, and was called the **largest mental hospital in the world**. The population fell through the 1970s with deinstitutionalization. Georgians called it simply "**Milledgeville**." Parents threatened, "**I'm going to send you to Milledgeville!**" Novelist Terry Kay: "Milledgeville. City of the crazies... a word of fear and mystery." Jack Nelson's 1959 Atlanta Constitution exposé of hospital conditions (Pulitzer, 1960) helped cement its grim reputation (from general knowledge, not re-checked here).
*Sources: Georgia Public Library Service (Omeka exhibit); Wikipedia; Atlanta Magazine "Asylum"; visitmilledgeville.org.*

## 17. Antipsychotics 1975–76
**Confirmed.** Standard first-generation drugs: **Thorazine (chlorpromazine)**, **Mellaril (thioridazine)**, **Stelazine (trifluoperazine)**, **Haldol (haloperidol)**, and **Prolixin (fluphenazine)**, which came in a long-acting depot shot often given at outpatient clinics. Commonly reported effects:
- heavy sedation, dry mouth, weight gain, dizziness on standing
- sun sensitivity (Thorazine)
- **akathisia** (inner restlessness, pacing, can't sit still)
- drug-induced **parkinsonism** (stiffness, mask-like face, tremor, shuffling gait); the "**Thorazine shuffle**" is usually this stiff shuffle, and some sources attribute it to akathisia
- **tardive dyskinesia** with long use

Cogentin (benztropine) or Artane was routinely co-prescribed for the stiffness.
*Sources: Cleveland Clinic (akathisia); PMC review of antipsychotic movement disorders; general clinical literature.*

## 18. Wild *Psilocybe cubensis* in the Southeast
**Confirmed (habitat); 1970s picking well attested but anecdotal.** *P. cubensis* grows on cow (sometimes horse) dung in pastures across the Gulf Coast and Southeast: Florida (documented near Gainesville as early as the 1940s), Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi, Louisiana, Texas. It fruits mostly **May–September**, after warm rain, and year-round in Florida. Pasture picking was a well-known 1970s counterculture pastime, and farmers posted no-trespassing signs. Psilocybin has been Schedule I since 1970.
*Source: Wikipedia "Psilocybe cubensis"; ScienceInsights habitat summary.*

## 19. LSD potency
**Corrected (partly) ⚠.** 1960s underground doses often ran **100–250+ µg**; Owsley's standard was about 270 µg. By the early/mid '70s street doses were already lower and more variable: **PharmChem's 1973 analysis of 189 samples averaged about 67 µg**. So 1974–76 acid was typically about 50–100 µg, still stronger than the 20–80 µg the DEA reported in the 1990s. Write it as "not like the '60s acid," not as uniformly huge doses.
*Sources: Erowid (Microgram/PharmChem 1968–74 compilation); DOJ/NCJRS LSD report.*

## 20. Cocaine street price
**Confirmed (approximate).** In January 1975, street cocaine ran about **$70–100 per gram**, or about **$1,500–1,800 per ounce**, heavily cut with mannitol or lactose. It was the "champagne of drugs," an expensive status drug for musicians and the affluent.
*Source: Ann Arbor Sun, Jan 17, 1975 (via Ann Arbor District Library); Wikipedia "Cocaine boom."*

## 21. Sacred Harp / singings in north Georgia
**Confirmed, with nuance.** In the 1960s–70s, north Georgia foothill counties each held annual **singing conventions**: all-day singing in a church, school auditorium or courthouse with "**dinner on the grounds**" at midday. **Sacred Harp** (four-shape) is strongest in **west Georgia** and east Alabama. The **Chattahoochee Musical Convention** (founded 1852, Coweta County) is the oldest surviving one. In the Blue Ridge counties, seven-shape gospel "convention" singing was also common; that last point comes from general folklore literature, not a specific source here.
*Sources: Wikipedia "Chattahoochee Musical Convention"; The Bitter Southerner "Let Everybody Sing"; UWG Sacred Harp LibGuide.*

## 22. L&N in the north Georgia mountains
**Confirmed (line); Uncertain (1970s job cuts).**
- The L&N's "**Old Line**," nicknamed the **"Hook and Eye,"** ran Marietta – Canton – Jasper/Tate – Talking Rock – Ellijay – Blue Ridge – Mineral Bluff – Copperhill/McCaysville – Etowah, TN. L&N has controlled it since 1902.
- The "hook" was tight reverse curves near Talking Rock/Jasper. The "eye" was a loop around a hill.
- A 1906 bypass to the west (now a CSX main line) left it a secondary, mineral-hauling route: marble at Tate, talc, and Copper Basin products.
- Decline was steady. L&N **abandoned the Murphy (NC) branch in the late 1970s**.
- L&N became part of Seaboard System (1982) and then CSX (1986). CSX sold or leased Marietta–Ellijay to the Georgia Northeastern in 1987 and abandoned Copperhill–Etowah in 2002. Blue Ridge now hosts a scenic railway.
- **No specific source found for 1970s layoff numbers.** Declining branch-line employment fits the record but is not documented here.
*Sources: american-rails.com (GNRR history); abandonedrails.com; railga.com.*

## 23. *Deliverance* (1972)
**Confirmed.** It was filmed in 1971 in **Rabun County** (northeast Georgia) on the **Chattooga River** (the film's "Cahulawassee"), with **Tallulah Gorge** and other local sites. The "Dueling Banjos" boy, Billy Redden, was a local kid. The film cemented the violent, degenerate-hillbilly stereotype. Locals and Appalachian scholars protested, and resentment persists. It also set off a whitewater-rafting boom (and drownings) on the Chattooga, which was designated a National Wild and Scenic River in 1974. Expect "Deliverance" jokes whenever outsiders meet mountain kids.
*Sources: New Georgia Encyclopedia "Deliverance"; CNN 2012; Marketplace 2012.*

## 26. Record-contract economics, mid-'70s
**Confirmed (general ranges), specifics approximate.**
- Artist royalties in the '70s ran about **5–15% of suggested retail**. A brand-new band on a small label would sit at the low end, about 5–8%.
- Royalties were typically paid on **90% of sales** (a relic of the old "breakage" allowance), after **packaging/"container" deductions** (commonly 10–20%), with **no royalty on "free goods"** and **reserves** held back against returns.
- **Advances and all recording costs were recoupable** from the artist's royalties only. The label recovers first, so a modest seller can owe the label money forever.
- Big labels' top-end advances for hot bands reached about $150,000. A small label might pay a few thousand dollars or nothing beyond studio time; no firm figure found.
- A **"key man" clause** lets the artist leave (or follow) if a named person, usually the A&R exec or label head, departs. Confirmed as a standard concept.
- The songwriter mechanical rate was fixed at **2¢ per song per record** until **Jan 1, 1978**, when it became 2.75¢.
*Sources: AWAL/Hypebot "History of Record Deals"; Sound On Sound "Recording Contracts Explained"; Univ. of Oregon *Pay for Play* text; mechanical-rate histories (Copyright Office M-200A; Jacobson Firm).*

## 27. Record prices; private-press cost
- **LP list price: $6.98 in 1974–75** (it rose from $5.98 about 1972–73). **$7.98 list began appearing in 1976** on major releases. Chain stores routinely discounted below list.
- **7-inch single:** one retail-price history gives **$1.49 list from 1975**. Actual shelf prices were often lower, about $0.99–1.29. Uncertain.
- **Private-press 45s: Uncertain.** No firm mid-'70s price list found. As a benchmark, a mid-1960s Iowa custom plant charged about **$300 for 1,000 45s**. A mid-'70s run of 300–500 45s with mastering and plates would plausibly cost a few hundred dollars. Custom plants took mail orders from local bands: **Rite Record Productions** in Cincinnati did custom pressings 1955–1985, and Nashville had custom pressers too.
*Sources: ratherrarerecords.com retail-price history (via search summary); Wikipedia "Iowa Great Lakes Recording Company"; 45rpmrecords.com (Rite).*

## 31. Funeral-home ambulances
**Confirmed, but it was fading in 1974–76.** Through the 1960s, funeral homes ran much of the ambulance service in rural and small-town America. They used "**combination cars**" (Cadillac-chassis coaches by Superior, Miller-Meteor, Cotner-Bevington, Hess & Eisenhardt): back from a funeral, put the red light on top and load cot and oxygen. The late '60s and '70s brought federal and state standards: the 1966 Highway Safety Act, the **EMS Systems Act of 1973**, EMT training and equipment rules. Combination cars were built until about 1979. In rural north Georgia in 1974–76, a funeral-home ambulance is plausible but increasingly old-fashioned as counties and hospitals set up EMS.
*Sources: Wikipedia "Combination car (ambulance)"; Hagerty; Ripley's; EMS history sources.*

## 33. 1970s concert filming
**Confirmed.** The workhorses were **16mm Éclair NPR and Arriflex 16BL** cameras with crystal sync to a **Nagra** recorder. Magazines pre-loaded for quick changes (NPR). They were handheld, with the operator on or beside the stage. Battery **"sun gun"** handheld lights were standard news/documentary kit for dark backstage and offstage moments. Their battery belts gave only short runs of light (general knowledge). Onstage, crews usually relied on stage lights and pushed fast film.
*Sources: RedShark News (16BL/NPR/Aaton); Science Museum Group (Éclair NPR); ADAPT TV history.*

## 34. Pay phones
**Uncertain; 10¢ most likely for Georgia 1974–76.** Rates were set state by state. Some Bell territories went to 15–20¢ in the '70s. But Southern Bell's Louisiana phones were still **5¢ until 1978**, and as late as **end-1981, 28 states still had a 10¢ local coin rate** (Bell was then pushing 25¢). Use a **dime** in Georgia and a dime (possibly 20¢ in some cities) in New York. **Uncertain.**
*Sources: Washington Post, Dec 14, 1981 "Bell Pushes 25 Cents..."; Quora/MetaFilter anecdotal histories.*

## 35. Cheap Southern motel, 1975
**Approximately confirmed.** Days Inn (founded in Georgia, 1970) advertised rooms from **$8.88** through most of the '70s. Motel 6 was about $6.95–8.95. A mom-and-pop rural motel would run about **$8–12 a night** in 1975.
*Sources: Wikipedia (Days Inn; Motel 6); anecdotal forum recollections.*

## 36. Dorm "parietals" at Southern colleges, 1974
**Partly corrected ⚠.** At big state schools, women's **curfews were largely gone by the early '70s**. UGA dropped all special women's rules except curfew by 1971, after the 1968 sit-in, and the curfew went soon after. **Opposite-sex visitation hours ("open house" windows) were still common in 1974**, especially at small, church-affiliated Southern colleges, which kept strict rules much longer. Keep parietals at a Baptist or Methodist college. At a big state school, make them "visitation hours," not curfews.
*Sources: Red & Black (UGA women's rules); History of Education Quarterly "Protests and Pushback... Deep South"; UT Volopedia (1970 curfew fight); USC "Dress Codes and Curfews."*

## 37. Ford Econoline generations
**Confirmed.** First generation was **1961–67** (flat-nose, Falcon-based, forward control). Second was **1968–74** (engine moved forward, short hood). Third was **1975–91**. A band van in Oct 1974 is 1974-or-older. A 1975+ model would be brand new and costly.
*Sources: Wikipedia "Ford E-Series"; Classic.com; Curbside Classic.*

## 38. *Wish You Were Here*
**Confirmed.** Released **Sept 12, 1975** in the UK (Harvest) and **Sept 13, 1975** in the U.S. (Columbia). "**Shine On You Crazy Diamond**" is a tribute to **Syd Barrett**. Barrett, heavy and head-shaved, wandered into Abbey Road during the mixing on **June 5, 1975**, and the band did not at first recognize him.
*Sources: Wikipedia (album; Shine On); Ultimate Classic Rock.*

## 39. "Hillbilly Highway"
**Confirmed.** About **3 million** people left Appalachia between 1950 and 1970. The main routes were US 23 (Pikeville–Portsmouth, then Cincinnati, Akron, Detroit) and **US 25/the I-75 corridor into southwest Ohio (Dayton, Hamilton, Middletown)**, for factory jobs. Cincinnati's Urban Appalachian Council was founded in 1974. The migration was mostly from Kentucky, West Virginia and Tennessee. North Georgians more often went to Atlanta or Dalton, though some went north.
*Sources: Wikipedia ("Hillbilly Highway"; "Urban Appalachians"); Kentucky Lantern 2025.*

## 40. Dalton carpet mills
**Confirmed.** Dalton, about 30 mi south of Chattanooga, was the "**Carpet Capital of the World**." It grew out of the chenille-bedspread cottage industry. Tufted carpet was about 90% of U.S. carpet by 1970. By 1963 about 63% of U.S. carpet mills were within 50 miles of Dalton, with roughly 400 mills in the early '70s. It was a big employer for mountain families willing to commute or move. Dalton reportedly had more millionaires per capita than any U.S. city in the '70s.
*Sources: New Georgia Encyclopedia (Dalton; Carpet Industry); Parker & Patton, *Carpet Capital*.*

## 41. Georgia driver's license age, 1972–73
**Uncertain (likely 16).** No primary source found for the 1970s statute. Before the 1997 graduated-license law (TADRA), Georgians generally licensed at **16**, and 15-year-olds could get an instructional permit. Treat 16 as standard and avoid a plot point that turns on a 15-year-old being licensed.
*Sources: Georgia DDS (TADRA history); pasttensega.com (license history, age not stated).*

## Nashville 1974–76 as model for Tannersville

- **The Opry leaves the Ryman.** The last Opry at the Ryman was **Mar 15, 1974**. The new Grand Ole Opry House at the Opryland theme park (out on a river bend in the suburbs) opened **Mar 16, 1974**; President Nixon attended.
- **The Ryman's near-death.** Owner National Life's consultants said the building held "nothing of value." The plan was to **demolish it and use its bricks for a chapel at Opryland**. Public outcry and a push for landmark status stopped it. The Ryman then **sat largely empty for almost 20 years**, with occasional tours and film shoots and recurring demolition rumors, until its 1990s renovation.
- **Lower Broadway goes seedy.** With the Opry gone, the honky-tonk strip behind the Ryman declined into **pawnshops, adult bookstores and peep shows, and rough bars**. Tootsie's Orchid Lounge (Tootsie Bess, owner until 1978) hung on. This decline deepened through the late '70s and '80s.
- **Session culture.** The original "**A-Team**" (Grady Martin, Hank Garland, Floyd Cramer, Bob Moore, Buddy Harman, Boots Randolph, Harold Bradley, Pig Robbins, Charlie McCoy and others) defined the late-'50s/'60s Nashville Sound. By the mid-'70s a newer crop of pickers did the work in the same union system:
  - **3-hour AFM sessions** (Local 257), with a norm of cutting about **four songs per session**
  - charts in the **Nashville Number System**
  - the **leader paid double scale**, with top players often demanding double scale too
- **Session pay: Uncertain.** No reliable 1975 dollar figure for Nashville master scale was found. A lower **"demo scale"** existed for publisher demos that would not be released, and lots of non-union "kitchen" demo work happened off the books. Say "union scale" without a number, or flag any number as invented.
- **Publishers.** Music Row (16th and 17th Avenues South) ran on song publishers: **Tree** (Buddy Killen), **Acuff-Rose**, **Combine** (Bob Beckham, Kris Kristofferson's publisher), **Cedarwood**, and many smaller shops. Staff writers worked on weekly draws against future royalties. Publishers paid for cheap **demos** to pitch songs to producers. Songwriter money depended on the **2¢-per-song mechanical rate** (unchanged since 1909 until Jan 1, 1978) plus BMI/ASCAP airplay.
- **Countrypolitan vs. Outlaws.** The establishment sound was slick, string-laden **countrypolitan**: Billy Sherrill at Epic (Tammy Wynette; Charlie Rich's "Behind Closed Doors" and "The Most Beautiful Girl," 1973) and Chet Atkins at RCA. The **Outlaw** backlash came from Waylon Jennings winning creative control and cutting *Honky Tonk Heroes* (1973), Willie Nelson's *Red Headed Stranger* (1975), and the Glaser brothers' independent studio, "Hillbilly Central." RCA's compilation ***Wanted! The Outlaws*** (Waylon, Willie, Jessi Colter, Tompall Glaser), released **Jan 12, 1976**, hit #1 country and #10 pop and became the **first country album certified platinum**.
- **Clubs.** The **Exit/In** opened in 1971 on Elliston Place near Vanderbilt. It started as a roughly **200-seat genre-agnostic listening room** (the first show was Jimmy Buffett, Oct 23, 1971) booking rock, folk, bluegrass, jazz and comedy. It was enlarged to about 500 in the early '80s.
- **The Station Inn** (bluegrass) opened in 1974, originally near Centennial Park, and moved to 12th Ave S in 1978.
- **Muther's Music Emporium** was a converted fabric store run by promoter Joe Sullivan, open only late 1973 – 1974. It booked Springsteen (Jan 1974), Kiss, Bob Seger and ELO, then folded over costs and legal trouble about underage crowds. It is a good model for a doomed rock club in a country town.
- **Colleges.** Vanderbilt was the elite private school with the Elliston Place and "Rock Block" scene next door. Belmont was then a small Baptist college whose pioneering music-business program was launched in 1971–73. Nashville also had Fisk, Tennessee State, Peabody and Lipscomb.
- **Student radio: WRVU 91.1.** Vanderbilt's station got a **10-watt** construction permit in Jan 1971 (call letters May 1971) and a full license in Aug 1973. It was raised to **430 W** by permit in Dec 1973 and license in July 1974. So in 1974–76 it reached beyond campus, roughly a 10–15 mile radius (estimate), but was not citywide the way it was later at 14,500 W.
- **Pop-culture mirror.** Robert Altman's film ***Nashville*** (released **June 11, 1975**) interweaves a music-city festival with a presidential campaign, which parallels this game's material closely.
- **Royalty-statement tricks an accountant would catch (1970s):**
  - A **"breakage" allowance** (royalty paid on only 90% of sales), a holdover from shellac 78s still applied to vinyl and tape.
  - **Packaging/"container" deductions** of about 10–20% off the royalty base.
  - **"Free goods"**: records shipped to retailers "free" (and sold) that carry no artist royalty, sometimes beyond the contractual cap.
  - **Reserves against returns** held for years and never liquidated.
  - **Cross-collateralization**: one album's losses or advances offset another's royalties, or publishing income offsets record debt.
  - **Recoupment of everything** (studio time, tour support, some promo) from the artist's share only.
  - **Half-rate royalties** on foreign, record-club and budget sales, and **no royalty on "cut-outs."**
  - Counting sales at a lower "wholesale" base while the contract specifies retail.
  - Mechanicals on "free" or "promotional" copies not paid.

  The general mechanics are documented. The exact percentages varied by contract.

The Outlaws; Billy Sherrill; Exit/In; Station Inn; WRVU; Nashville (film); Belmont University); Klick Tee / Brucebase (Muther's); HMDB (Tootsie Bess marker); Hits Daily Double and Music Business Research (breakage); Univ. of Oregon *Pay for Play*; Copyright Office mechanical-rate history.*

**Fall 1974**
- Sept 8: the Ford pardon and Evel Knievel's failed Snake River Canyon jump, the same day.
- Oct 8: "Whip Inflation Now" and WIN buttons.
- Oct 30: Ali knocks out Foreman in Zaire ("Rumble in the Jungle").
- Nov 5: George Busbee elected Georgia governor (took office Jan 14, 1975). Maynard Jackson had become Atlanta's first Black mayor in Jan 1974.
- Hank Aaron's record season just ended. He was traded to Milwaukee in Nov 1974.
- "Sweet Home Alabama" on every Southern radio. *The Longest Yard* (Aug 1974) was filmed at the Georgia State Prison in Reidsville with Gov. Carter's cooperation. *The Texas Chain Saw Massacre* (Oct 1974).
- The national 55 mph limit is less than a year old. Gas is about 53¢.

**Winter 1974–75**
- Dec 12: Carter, a lame-duck governor, announces for president. Most Georgians laugh.
- Jan 1, 1975: Watergate cover-up convictions (Mitchell, Haldeman, Ehrlichman).
- Jan 12: Super Bowl IX (Steelers).
- New TV: *Wheel of Fortune* (Jan 1975), *The Jeffersons* (Jan 1975), *Barney Miller* (Jan 1975).
- The Georgia Senate kills the ERA, 33–22.

**Spring 1975**
- April: the South Vietnam collapse. "Operation Babylift." Saigon falls Apr 30.
- May 12–15: the *Mayaguez* incident.

**Summer 1975**
- June 11: Altman's *Nashville*. June 20: ***Jaws*** opens (the first summer blockbuster; beaches emptied).

- Two attempts on Ford's life in California: Sept 5 (Squeaky Fromme) and Sept 22 (Sara Jane Moore).

- Oct 21: Carlton Fisk's World Series Game 6 homer. The Reds win Game 7 on Oct 22.

- Jan 12: *Wanted! The Outlaws*.

**Summer 1976 (to late August)**
- July 4: the **Bicentennial**: tall ships in New York harbor, fireworks and red-white-and-blue everything. The Entebbe raid happens the same day.
- July 7–9: Skynyrd records *One More from the Road* at the Fox (released Sept 13).
