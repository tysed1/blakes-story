## 1. Scene verdicts

### 1. Chapter I, Mission 9 (HOME): the kitchen
**Plays: yes.** This is the best-written fight in the game. It's short, it runs on money, and it lands on "You got to keep yours" with nothing after it but the hold. **Wayne** is on his sheet for every line but one: "I'm trying to keep you from pissing your life away" says his protective motive out loud, which is exactly what he hides. **Ellis** is on his sheet ("Reckon so," "Might could," "That's a matter for the courts," the unguarded image in the notebook). **Clara** drifts into counsel once ("You don't have to believe everything he says"), which is the register V3 moved to orders. "He's right though" has no clear referent: it follows Ellis's "Asshole." about Wayne's snoring, so it reads as Clara siding with Wayne. Pruitt is perfect.

### 2. Chapter IV, Mission 8: "You want me to tell her?"
**Plays: yes.** It's one of the three best scenes in the game. **Wayne** is clipped and on his sheet ("Where." "For what." "Hm." "Then go."). Keep his "Go on.": the X M7 design note needs Wayne among the people who say it. There are only two rhythm notes. One Wayne question breaks his clipped pattern, and Ellis doesn't contract under pressure.

### 3. Chapter V, Mission 16 (WHERE DID WE MEET?)
**Plays: yes.** Wayne's walk-in is exactly one gesture and stops in time. The booth scene works until "You used to need me," which says the subtext one line before the flicker shows it. Depot Street and the pull-back are clean. **Cal** breaks his sheet once in the toast ("Now we're dreaming" is a joke that isn't a correction). **Riley** repeats, nearly word for word, the joke Ellis made on her behalf at the Starlite in Chapter I. **Clara** is on her sheet everywhere else ("Drink your beer." "like a mailbox").

### 4. Chapter VI, Mission 12 (Raymond) and Mission 16 (Marlon tells Cal)
**Both play.** Raymond is the best adult speech in the game, and it earns its length because it's all fact and no feeling. **Wayne** misses the one trigger his sheet gives him: he's "terrified" and says "Ellis—". The first "Son." in the game is in VIII M13, where the text says "He says it the way he says it only when he's frightened," which assumes the player has heard it before. "Watch your tone" is sitcom-dad phrasing. **M16** is clean. Marlon talks in fragments, and Cal's "No." and "I won't." are on his sheet. No fixes.

### 5. Chapter VII, Mission 12 (Room 614) and Mission 14 (Thanksgiving)
**M12 plays in its second half and argues in its first.** The bucket, the "Ice machine working?" / "Somebody left a bucket," and the ketchup breakfast are superb. The radiator scene is where the actors will stall. **Ellis** speaks Riley's seminar ("That's what knowing somebody is"). **Clara** gives a thesis sentence, then a symmetrical "knows me better" / "knows you better" volley. Her comic line ("Lord, you combed your hair. For bed.") is the only thing keeping her on her sheet. **M14 plays beautifully.** "Loud." "Your mama set a place." "Goodnight, Maggie." The one weak line is a player option written as therapy-speak. Joan and Mr. Halvorsen both say "Nineteen sixty-eight" within sixty lines of each other.

### 6. Chapter VIII: Missions 7, 12, 14, 15, 17
- **M7 (the fight): plays.** "She isn't real, Ellis" and the hotel-room line land exactly as designed. **Cal** gets a triad of questions in one option when his owned phrase fits the moment and cuts deeper.
- **M12 (Riley and Wayne): plays clean.** "Clara was my mama's name," the box, "Raymond didn't sleep." This is Wayne's best scene. No fixes.
- **M14 (Tolliver Road): plays.** Grace is right: meaner, sillier, specific. "I've got your shirt" is the best Clara line in the game. Her second line at the oak argues a proposition ("You made the songs...") where she should tease.
- **M15 (the payphone): plays.** "It's closed." "In what?" "Like a knuckle." **Riley** borrows Clara's "your daddy" marker forty lines after Clara's one "Daddy" slip. One option is post-1990 relationship talk ("what we are right now").
- **M17 (Dr. Lusk): the frame plays and the doctor over-talks.** The parking deck, "I got a radio," "He's got a radio," and the Starlite are perfect. **Lusk** announces her own honesty, explains the game's naming rule in a paragraph, and makes the April connection for Ellis just before the narration says he hasn't made it. "Anything from the road?" is unclear to the ear.

### 7. Chapter IX: Missions 4, 5, 10, 12, 15

- **M5 (Sylva): plays.** "You got tall." "I'm on some medicine." / "Well. Good." The tailgate is the warmest scene in IX. It ends one line late: Ellis explains the joke after Wayne's complaint has already ended it. "The two cities" is design-document language in Ellis's mouth.

### 8. Chapter X: Missions 6, 7, 9

- **Coda: plays clean.** "Hm." / "Hm." / "Good." No fixes.

## 3. Ten lines that must not be touched

1. **"Yeah. You got to keep yours."** (Wayne, I M9). The whole game in six words, from the man who can't say any of it.
2. **"She sings my part."** (Clara, V M16). Jealousy, harmony and Grace in four words, and nobody on a first play hears all three.
3. **"He seems all right." / "He is." / "Can I have the ketchup."** (Ellis and Cal, VII M12). An acceptance scene done entirely in condiments.
4. **"Your mama set a place."** (Ellis, VII M14). Funny, then not, in the same breath, and Riley's hand over her mouth does the rest.
5. **"Clara was my mama's name."** (Wayne, VIII M12). The reveal, delivered as a fact about his mother, to a girl he met twice.
6. **"I've got your shirt."** (Clara, VIII M14). The best Clara line: she answers "You've got her eyes" with the one part of her that's his.
7. **"I couldn't have it be nobody's."** (Wayne, IX M4). A three-year confession in mountain grammar, with no explanation after it.
8. **"I counted more."** (Cal, IX M12). What noticing without saving costs, said as arithmetic.
9. **"Your biscuits are terrible." / "I know."** (Ellis and Wayne, X M6). The last exchange between them, and the only reconciliation the game allows.
10. **"Then somebody used to be giving him more."** (Dr. Coker, Ep M7). A stranger tells Wayne about his son by weighing a dog.

Close behind: "Loud." (VII M14), "In what?" (VIII M15), "He's got a radio." (VIII M17), "I'm on some medicine." / "Well. Good." (IX M5), "It's written down." (X M6), "She carries." (Ep M2), and "Hm." / "Hm." / "Good." (Coda).

## 4. Verdict

**8/10.** The dialogue's floor is high and its best scenes are as good as anything in the genre. Wayne is the best-written character in the project: every word he says is load-bearing, and his thaw really does come one gesture per chapter. The V3 comic engines hold in the scenes that matter ("Allegedly." / "Be specific." at Walt's bench is the voice sheet working perfectly). The two-handers (Ellis and Wayne, Ellis and Cal, Riley and Wayne) mostly play with no notes. The failures cluster in three places, and each can be fixed with a targeted pass rather than a rewrite. **(1) Clara, from VII on, argues propositions** ("stop pretending they know you," "You made the songs," "I didn't go anywhere. You did."), and in VII M12 Ellis starts arguing back in Riley's register. Her sheet gives her orders and teases, and she's at her best when she follows it ("Late, is what"). **(2) Player-choice menus** labelled "the true thing" are written as the label (therapy-register feelings, X/Y pivots) rather than as lines a person says. Most of the over-articulation in VII–IX sits in options, not in scripted dialogue. **(3) Professionals explain the design:** Lusk above all, and once Hollis. **What would raise it to 9:** a Clara-only pass over VII–X with the rule "if she's making an argument, make it an order or a jab"; rewriting every "true thing" option as something the character saw or did; and holding owned *markers* ("your daddy," "Be specific," "son") to their owners as strictly as the owned stock replies already are.
