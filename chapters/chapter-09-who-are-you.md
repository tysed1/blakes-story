# CHAPTER IX — WHO ARE YOU?

> **Stony Knob · Hollow Ridge · New York · Sylva, North Carolina · Tannersville · Monday, May 10 – Sunday, August 22, 1976**
> Main-story runtime: ~6 hours · Playable: Cal, Ellis, Riley, Dean, and, for one afternoon, Ellis at fifteen

## Chapter thesis

The world discovers Ellis the poet at the moment Ellis discovers what he said in the car. Everyone in this chapter is asked who they are: by a television host, a state patrol report, a witness in North Carolina, a psychiatrist's pill bottle, a magazine, a Sacred Harp class, a bass player at a repair bench, and a fourteen-year-old in a hallway. Grace, on a cassette she made when she was thirteen, has already answered for him, rudely.

This is the chapter of the second album. It's made in a lodge on a mountain above the house Ellis grew up in, and the band is healing and coming apart at the same time.

## World state at open

- Summer 1976. The Bicentennial on every flagpole, on every paper cup, on the side of the Hollow Ridge water tower in new red-white-and-blue paint. Carter is winning. "Sunday Clothes" (Ellis's single version) peaked at #14 in April and is still on the radio. The Bowery photograph is on dorm walls everywhere. The *Night Stage* silence has been rerun twice.
- Fans have started writing to *Rave* with theories about Clara. The most popular is that she's the dead sister from the January article.
- Hollow Ridge is sick of tourists and proud of them. Somebody has stolen the town-limit sign outright; the county hasn't replaced it.
- Ellis has been on trifluoperazine for a month. He sleeps. Clara is gone.

## COLD OPEN — THE TRUCK

Monday, May 10, 6:40 a.m. Fog on the Stony Knob road, the switchbacks above Cold Branch Falls.

A truck is coming up the mountain in low gear: a 1964 Divco bread truck painted flat gray, with DALTON SOUND · REMOTE stenciled on the side in white, over the old lettering bleeding through (HOLSUM), and a cable reel bolted to the back bumper. Frank Dalton bought it from a gospel radio program in Alabama that went off the air.

Frank is walking backward in front of it with a flashlight in the fog, guiding it around the hairpins with small calm gestures, the way he rides a fader. Tully is on the running board. The camera belongs to nobody. It watches the truck come around the switchback where, in November 1974, a pickup sat at a gravel pull-off with its engine off and its dome light on.

The truck passes the pull-off without stopping. The pull-off is empty. The fog moves over it.

Then the van comes around the bend behind the truck, the old green Econoline with THE BLAKES on the rear doors in Riley's lettering, and the camera finds Cal at the wheel through the windshield, and the switch settles on him.

**CHAPTER IX**
**WHO ARE YOU?**

## MISSION 1 — THE LODGE

**Playable:** Cal · **Where:** Knob House, Stony Knob · **When:** Monday May 10, 7:00 a.m. – 11:00 p.m. · **Length:** ~25 min

### Knob House

Above the overlook, past the fire tower, at the end of a gravel lane with a chain across it that Eddie has a key to, there's a house nobody in Hollow Ridge has been inside.

A Laurel City lawyer named Aldridge built it in 1958 out of chestnut logs salvaged from barns all over the North highlands, and fieldstone, and big windows facing west. He's used it maybe twenty times. Eddie rented it for the summer for less than the band paid for the Vistalite.

The player, as Cal, gets the first walk-through, because Cal is the one who has to figure out where everything goes. It's the chapter's hub, and it's small and dense:

- **The great room.** Two stories. A fieldstone fireplace you could stand up in, with a chimney that goes up thirty feet. Chestnut beams. A wall of windows onto the long view west toward the Laurel Gap. Frank walks in, looks at the chimney, claps once, listens, and says, "Well. There's the elevator shaft."
- **The kitchen.** A Chambers range from 1958, a long pine table with eight chairs, a screen door onto a back porch.
- **The bedrooms.** Five, upstairs, off a gallery that looks down into the great room.
- **The lake.** Behind the house, down a path through rhododendron: a four-acre pond Aldridge dammed in 1959, spring-fed, cold, dark, with a dock and an aluminum johnboat upside down on it.
- **The truck.** Parked by the kitchen door with its back open. Cables go in through the kitchen window, which Frank has removed from its frame.
- **The phone.** One. In the front hall. A party line with three other houses on the mountain. Cal writes the rules for it on an index card and tapes it to the wall.

### Rooms

Cal assigns the rooms, because somebody has to and it's always him. The player does it by walking each person's bags upstairs, or telling them where to go.

- **Riley** gets the room at the west end with the best window, because Cal decides she should, and she argues, and loses.
- **Dean** gets the room next to the bathroom, for reasons Cal doesn't explain.
- **Ellis** gets the room at the east end, over the kitchen, with a view of the lake. He brought his guitar, a duffel, a white pharmacy bottle, the green notebook Riley gave him, and Tater. Wayne said, "Take him. He's too fat for the stairs at the house anyway." Tater looks at the stairs at Knob House, which are worse, and lies down at the bottom.
- **Cal's own room.** The player carries two bags into it. One is Cal's. The other is Theo's.

Theo is here as himself. He'll run the live-room microphones for Frank. He and Cal will share the room at the top of the stairs. Nobody in the band says anything about it, except Dean, who carries three of Theo's plants up the stairs at once and asks where he wants the "big dumb one," meaning a fiddle-leaf fig, and Theo says "By the window," and Dean says "It's going to need a name," and Theo says "It has one," and doesn't say what.

The player can stand in the doorway of the room with Cal, and look at two bags on one bed, and nobody pretending.

### The pill

Evening. The kitchen. Dean cooks spaghetti for eleven people in a pot meant for six. Frank and his assistant engineer, Lamar Pickett (a quiet boy from Macon who calls Frank "Mr. Dalton"), come in from the truck. Tully, Theo, Riley, Eddie, who's staying one night.

At 8:00 p.m. exactly, Ellis goes to the kitchen sink, takes a small blue tablet out of the white bottle, and takes it with water from a jelly glass he brought from home. He doesn't look at anyone. Nobody says anything.

Cal sees it. The player, as Cal, can look, or not. Everybody in the kitchen saw it.

Ellis has been on the medication for a month. The player, as Cal, can see what it looks like from outside. Ellis is slower. He's polite. He laughs a half-second late, and means it. He ate two plates of spaghetti. He's gained a few pounds. He hasn't talked to anyone who isn't there since April. He doesn't talk much to anyone who is.

### The empty chair

After dinner. Everyone at the long pine table, loud, full, the dishes pushed back. Eight chairs. The one at the far end, by the screen door, is empty.

The player, as Cal, gets up to get another beer from the refrigerator, and has to pass behind Ellis's chair.

The switch begins. The sound narrows. The frame catches.

The depth of field starts to shift, and it doesn't shift to Ellis. It shifts past him, to the far end of the table, to the empty chair by the screen door, where in any other chapter the player would have expected to find a young woman in a jean jacket with her boots up on the rung.

The chair is empty. Nobody is in it. The focus pulls toward it anyway. The camera starts to go.

It stutters. Catches. Fails. Snaps back to Cal.

Then, a second later, clean, with the old grammar, it goes to Ellis, and the player is Ellis, sitting at a crowded table, looking down the length of it at an empty chair by a screen door.

> **Design note — the third failure.** Twice in Chapter VIII the switch tried to go to Clara and couldn't. Here it tries to go to the place she would be, and there's nothing there even from Ellis's side. The camera is looking for her too.

## MISSION 2 — UNDER GLASS

**Playable:** Ellis · **Where:** Knob House; the great room; the dock; the kitchen table · **When:** Tuesday May 11 – Saturday June 5 · **Length:** ~35 min

### The medicated free-roam

For four weeks of game time, compressed into a set of days the player moves between freely, the player is Ellis on trifluoperazine, at a lodge on a mountain, making a record.

The body, as in Chapter VIII: slower. A beat of lag in the camera. Two-stage standing up. His hands are steady and heavy. Once in a while, when he sits too long, his legs start to move on their own, a restless jiggle the player didn't cause; the benztropine helps.

The world is the same color. The lake is the same green-black. The sound is the same. Clara isn't anywhere, and Observe is quiet, and the lines are short: *Lake. Still.* / *Frank asleep in the truck.* / *Tater in the water to his chest, looking at a frog.*

And there are things the player has never been able to give Ellis before:
- **He sleeps.** Every night. The player can go to bed at eleven and skip to morning. He wakes up at seven with the light coming across the lake.
- **He eats.** Dean's pancakes, Riley's pancakes (burnt), Theo's gumbo on a Sunday, which the whole house fights over.
- **He swims.** The lake is fed by a cold spring and the water is a shock. Riley goes in off the end of the dock every morning at seven. Ellis starts going in with her in the second week. The player can make him jump. Riley surfaces screaming about how cold it is every single morning like it's news.
- **Tater.** The dog has decided the lodge is his. He sleeps across Ellis's door at night. He goes in the lake to his chest and stands there.

### The record

The second album gets made the way *Borrowed Stone* was, in pieces, but slower, because Ellis is slower, and everyone adjusts to it without saying so.

The player can take part in sessions in the great room: the Room system in a studio, the fireplace chimney as an echo chamber (Frank hangs a microphone thirty feet up inside it on a rope), the band facing each other in a square on the rag rugs. The songs this summer (a playable list the player can work through in any order):

- **"New Skin."** See below.
- **"Knob House."** An instrumental built around the chimney's echo.
- **"Parietal Hours"** re-cut, faster, because Dean insisted.
- **"The Bend."** Riley's. A song about a road she's driven once, at night, in the rain. She doesn't tell anyone what it's about. Ellis knows.
- **"Who Are You?"** Not yet. That comes in July.

Ellis plays slower. The glide is gentler; he doesn't push the tremolo arm as far. His voice is steadier and has less in it. Cal adjusts first, playing a little more, filling what Ellis leaves. Dean pulls back. Riley's harmonies carry more of the weight. Frank watches all of it from the truck and doesn't say anything, and his takes are shorter.

> **Design note.** The medicated Ellis is steadier, simpler, and less able to take the band somewhere nobody planned to go. In the Room he has no *reach* and fewer turns, and he finishes things.

### Fishing

A morning in late May, 5:40 a.m. The dock. Mist on the lake. Cal is already out there with two rods, a tackle box, a thermos, and the upside-down johnboat turned right-side up.

**CAL:** You fish?

**ELLIS:** Badly.

**CAL:** Good. So do I.

The player rows. (A rowing control; the johnboat goes in slow circles at first.) Out in the middle of the lake, anchored with a coffee can of concrete on a rope. Two lines in the water. Nothing biting. Mist.

They haven't talked like this since Kenosha. It's not about Kenosha. The player chooses topics, but mostly the player waits, and Cal talks, which Cal almost never does.

He talks about his grandfather.

**CAL:** My granddaddy had the shop before Daddy. Mercer Radio. It was just radio then. He'd been in the Navy in the first war, on a ship, working the wireless.

He reels in, checks the bait, casts again.

**CAL:** I was twelve. I came in after school one day and he was at the bench with a Philco in front of him, the back off, and a soldering iron in his hand, still plugged in. He was sitting up. I thought he was thinking.

Ellis doesn't say anything.

**CAL:** I called the ambulance. From the phone on the wall. I told them the address. They said stay on the line and I did. And while I was waiting, I looked at the radio. He'd found the bad capacitor. He had the new one right there in the tweezers. So I put it in.

The mist is lifting. A fish jumps somewhere behind them.

**CAL:** By the time they came, it was playing.

**ELLIS:** What'd you do with it?

**CAL:** Put the back on it. Put it on the shelf over the bench. It's still there. Daddy never sold it.

A long pause. No timer.

**ELLIS:** Why'd you finish it?

**CAL:** It seemed like the thing to do.

That's all he says about it.

The medicated Ellis can't do the thing Ellis used to do, the sudden specific image. He can only listen, and he listens well. The player can see Cal notice that. After a while, Ellis says the only thing that comes to him:

**ELLIS:** I've never caught a fish in my life.

**CAL:** I know. I've watched you.

They don't catch anything. They row back at eight. Riley is on the end of the dock in a towel, and says, "Anything?" and Cal says, "No," with great satisfaction.

> **Setup.** The Philco sits on the shelf over the bench at Mercer Radio & TV. In Mission 12, Ellis sees it, and knows what it is.

### New skin

The first week of June. A weekday afternoon. The kitchen table at Knob House, the long pine one, in daylight. Everyone is down at the lake. Ellis has a Western Auto memo book, number 70, and a pencil, and the green notebook, still blank, under his elbow.

He's trying to write "New Skin." The first half of it was on a page Dex Lundgren tore out of memo book 68 on a bus in January. He can remember it, more or less. The player can see him write the first lines from memory, and they come out a little different:

> *I gave you a birthday every year you didn't have one.*
> *I gave you a jacket that already was yours.*

(*didn't get one* has become *didn't have one*; *was already* has become *already was*.)

And then he writes the rest of it, slowly, the way you'd work on an engine with the manual open. The songwriting system is here, the same one as "Still Here" in Chapter V, and the lines Ellis offers are plain. There are no leaps. The song, when it's done, doesn't reach. It just says it.

> *I gave you a voice for the high part.*
> *I gave you a road you never had to drive.*
> *I kept you as old as I needed you to be,*
> *and I needed you older than I was, to be alive.*

The player picks the order. The last line is fixed:

> *You'd have hated every word of this.*

He finishes it at 4:15 p.m. He puts the pencil down on the table.

Riley comes up from the lake in a towel with her hair dripping and sees him sitting there with the notebook, and the player sees her see his face: not shining, the way it did when he finished "Still Here." Quiet. Done.

**RILEY:** You finished something.

**ELLIS:** Yeah.

**RILEY:** Can I hear it?

He plays it for her on the Jazzmaster unplugged at the kitchen table, slowly, and sings it plainly, and it's two and a half minutes long. When it's over, she doesn't say anything for a while.

**RILEY:** Play it for Frank.

They cut it that night in the great room in one take, the band in the square, Ellis singing it straight into a microphone with the chimney mic open thirty feet above him, and Frank, in the truck, says on the talkback: "That's the one," and nobody asks for another.

> **Design note.** "New Skin" is finished on the medication, in daylight, in the kitchen. The pills took some of Ellis's gift and left a man who could finish a song about his sister without reaching for anything. Riley thinks it's his best song. Ellis, later, won't be able to remember how he wrote it.

### Clara?

One evening, the player can take Ellis down to the dock alone at dusk. The lake still. The frogs. The lodge lights coming on behind him.

There's a new prompt: *Call.*

If the player presses it, Ellis says, quietly, to the lake:

**ELLIS:** Clara?

Nothing answers. A frog. The water. The lodge.

He doesn't say it again.

## MISSION 3 — LATE HOUR

**Playable:** Ellis → Riley · **Where:** Studio 6B, a network building, Midtown Manhattan; Knob House · **When:** Thursday June 10, 5:00 – 7:00 p.m. (taping); Friday June 11, 1:00 – 2:10 a.m. (broadcast) · **Length:** ~25 min

### Russ Mandel

*Late Hour* airs at one in the morning on a network, four nights a week: one guest, one host, a black set, two chairs, an ashtray on a table between them, and long silences nobody's allowed to cut. **Russ Mandel**, forty-eight, gray-haired, heavy-lidded, genuinely curious, smokes through the whole hour and asks the second question nobody else thinks to ask. People go on *Late Hour* to be understood. Sometimes they go on to be taken apart.

Monarch booked it the week after the *Night Stage* rerun, because Gil Tarver can read a Nielsen number: the silent boy from Georgia was the most talked-about forty seconds of television all spring. *Late Hour* wants the silent boy.

They don't get him.

### The green room

Ellis flew up that morning with Eddie. He took his pill at eight on the plane with a Coke. He's calm. He's polite to the makeup woman, to the page, to the stage manager. He eats a sandwich. The player can walk him around the green room: a coffee urn, a bowl of fruit, a signed photograph of Orson Welles on the wall. Eddie is on the phone to Gil.

No Clara on the counter. No looking at empty doorways. Nobody counting.

### The interview

The black set. Two chairs. Russ Mandel lights a cigarette and doesn't say anything for four seconds, on purpose, to see what the kid does.

This is the chapter's dialogue scene, and it's built on the same grammar the game has used for eight chapters: timing, topic, disclosure, silence. But the medication is in the system now, and the player can feel it in their hands:

- **Every answer option is fluent.** The options the game offers are polite, clear, complete sentences. The mock-formal deflections (*Allegedly.* / *That's a matter for the courts.*) aren't there. Neither are the sudden images.
- **Silence doesn't hold.** The player can press and hold the silence input, the way they have all game. Ellis holds it for a second, two, and then fills it anyway, pleasantly, with a sentence. No limit on tries. It never holds.

Some of what's said:

**RUSS:** You're from a town called Hollow Ridge.

**ELLIS:** Yes sir. Two thousand one hundred people. Somebody painted that on the sign, and then somebody stole the sign.

The audience laughs. Russ laughs.

---

**RUSS:** I understand you rank boiled peanuts.

**ELLIS:** Every stand between Laurel City and Tannersville.

**RUSS:** Who's number one?

**ELLIS:** A lady named Mrs. Tharpe on US 19. She puts a ham hock in the pot. It's not fair to the others.

**RUSS:** And the worst?

**ELLIS:** Fourth place is a crime against God and peanuts. I won't say where. They know who they are.

(It's the peanut-ranking bit the band has heard a hundred times in the van, word for word, and he delivers it perfectly, on time, to the laugh.)

---

**RUSS:** On *Night Stage* this spring, in the middle of your song, you stopped. For almost a minute. What happened?

**ELLIS:** I lost the line. It happens. My band covered for me. They're very good.

**RUSS:** That's it?

**ELLIS:** That's it.

Russ looks at him through the smoke for a long time. The player, holding silence, watches Ellis fill it:

**ELLIS:** I was tired. We'd been on the road since January.

---

**RUSS:** Who's Clara?

**ELLIS:** An old friend.

**RUSS:** Is she here tonight?

**ELLIS:** No sir.

It's true, and nobody knows it.

---

**RUSS:** There was an article in *Rave* in January. About your sister.

The audience goes quiet. Russ lets it.

**ELLIS:** Grace. She was fourteen.

**RUSS:** Would you tell me about her?

**ELLIS:** She'd have liked the record. She'd have told me the drums were too loud. She rode horses. She was bossy.

It's a perfect answer. The audience is moved. Russ is moved. Every word of it is true, and none of it is anything Ellis would have said on television.

### The broadcast

Stutter, through a television. The camera goes into the black set on a studio monitor, and out of a portable Zenith on the kitchen counter at Knob House, and finds Riley.

Friday, 1:00 a.m. Knob House. The whole band and Frank and Theo and Tully around the kitchen table in the dark, watching *Late Hour* on a twelve-inch black-and-white set with rabbit ears aimed at the window, which is the only way it comes in on the mountain.

The player, as Riley, watches the interview on the little screen with the others. They laugh at the peanut bit. Dean laughs hardest.

**DEAN:** He's funny.

**CAL:** He's polite.

**RILEY:** He's never been polite in his life.

**FRANK:** Country's going to love him.

On the screen, Ellis says *She'd have told me the drums were too loud,* and Dean stops laughing.

Riley can look around the kitchen at their faces in the television light. Cal has his pen out and nothing to write on. Theo is watching Cal. Tully is watching the screen with the expression he had on the Saigon night in Chapter V.

**RILEY:** That's not him.

**CAL:** He slept eight hours. That's him too.

Nobody says anything after that.

### After

It's the best thing that ever happened to the franchise, in Gil Tarver's phrase, said on the phone to Eddie on Friday morning. *Borrowed Stone* sells more copies in the week after *Late Hour* than it did in March. Monarch's switchboard takes nine hundred calls. The mail triples. The letters are about how *nice* he was, how *humble*, how *sad about his sister*.

Dex Lundgren, in a bar in Detroit, watches the rerun on the bar television and says to nobody, "What did they do to him," and orders another drink, and takes out his notebook, and doesn't write anything.

> **Design note — the medicated interview.** Two televised silences in a row would repeat, so the second television appearance has no silence in it: the player can't make silence hold. The country loves the medicated Ellis. Riley doesn't recognize him. Cal does.

## MISSION 4 — THE REPORT

**Playable:** Ellis · **Where:** the Blake kitchen, Cold Branch Road · **When:** Saturday June 19, 4:00 – 5:30 p.m. · **Length:** ~20 min

### Wayne asks

Wayne Blake watched *Late Hour* at 1 a.m. in his chair in the front room, alone, with Tater gone to the mountain and the house very quiet.

He heard his son, on television, in front of the whole country, say *She'd have told me the drums were too loud* in a pleasant voice, and it did something to him that nothing in three years had done.

On Monday he called Deputy Lyle Pruitt at the sheriff's office and asked for the file.

On Saturday he calls the party line at Knob House and says four words to Cal, who answers: "Tell him come down."

### The kitchen

Ellis drives down the mountain in the Valiant. The kitchen at Cold Branch Road. The oilcloth. The bills pile. Coupon 39 of 48.

On the table: two things.

A manila folder, dog-eared, with *PRUITT — SUPP.* written on the tab in a deputy's ballpoint. Pruitt brought it himself on Wednesday, sat in the truck in the yard for a while, handed it through the window, and drove off without getting out.

And a long brown envelope from the Georgia State Patrol, postmarked May 1973, addressed to Mr. Wayne Blake, Cold Branch Rd. It's been in the funeral-bills box in Wayne's closet for three years. It's never been opened. The flap is still sealed.

Wayne is at the table. Ellis sits down.

**WAYNE:** I never opened it.

He takes out his Case pocketknife and opens the blade and holds it out, handle first, across the table, to his son.

The prompt: *Open it.*

No timer. When the player presses it, Ellis slits the envelope with his father's knife.

### The document

This is a document-reading scene. The report is a real object: a Georgia State Patrol motor vehicle accident report, carbon-copy form, typed, with a diagram hand-drawn in pencil on the back. The player can read every field, turn it over, go back. Ellis reads with them. Wayne watches Ellis read.

What it says:

> **DATE:** 4-12-73 **TIME:** 1940 **DAY:** THURS
> **LOCATION:** TOLLIVER RD, 0.3 MI S OF HENSLEY RES.
> **WEATHER:** RAIN **ROAD SURFACE:** WET ASPHALT **SHOULDER:** NONE
> **VEH 1:** 1968 CHEV IMPALA · DRIVER: BLAKE, ELLIS W., 16 · INJ: FRACT L ARM, HEAD LAC.
> **PASSENGER:** BLAKE, GRACE C., 14 · **FATAL**
> **VEH 2:** UNIDENTIFIED PICKUP TRUCK, DARK COLOR, NORTHBOUND · LEFT SCENE
> **EST. SPEED VEH 1:** 43–48 MPH · **POSTED:** 45
> **ALCOHOL:** NONE **MECH. DEFECT:** NONE · TIRES WORN, WITHIN LIMITS
> **CONTRIBUTING FACTORS:** WET PAVEMENT. POSS. UNIDENTIFIED VEH ENCROACHING CENTER LINE PER WITNESS.
> **DRIVER STATEMENT** (taken 4-13-73, county hospital): *"We were arguing and I looked at her."*

And the diagram on the back: the curve, a box for the Impala, an arrow, a little circle for the oak, and a second box coming the other way, drawn with a dotted line, over the center line, labeled *V2 (per wit.)*.

And then Pruitt's supplement, in the manila folder: two typed pages and a carbon of a witness statement.

> **WITNESS:** BEAM, HOLLIS R., 55, seed salesman, Tanner Valley Rd.
> *"I was 150 yds behind the Chevrolet going the same way. A pickup truck came around the bend with its high beams on over the line. The Chevrolet went right and the right wheels went off the edge and it came back across and turned around and hit the tree on the passenger side. The truck did not stop. I ran up to the car. I could not open the doors. The boy was saying I'm sorry. The girl said stop saying sorry. I ran to the Hensley house to call. When I came back the girl was talking but I could not make out what she said. I tried to get the boy out of the car because of the gasoline. He fought me. He said he had to stay where she could see him."*
>
> *Supplement (Dep. L. Pruitt): Witness contacted 4-13. Witness stated Chevrolet was not exceeding his own speed of approx. 45 mph. Current address of witness (1976): Sylva, N.C.*

### What he reads

The player can read in any order. The scene can't end until three things have been read:

*We were arguing and I looked at her.* He doesn't remember arguing. He doesn't remember saying this to anyone.

*A pickup truck came around the bend with its high beams on over the line.* There was another truck.

*The girl said stop saying sorry.* She spoke. After.

Ellis puts the pages down on the oilcloth, square, the way his father would.

The medication is in the scene: the camera slow, Ellis's hands steady on the paper, his face not doing anything. Underneath it something is happening very slowly, like weather behind a mountain.

### Wayne

**ELLIS:** There was a truck.

**WAYNE:** Yes.

**ELLIS:** You knew.

**WAYNE:** Pruitt told me. The next day. In the hospital parking lot.

**ELLIS:** What'd you say?

**WAYNE:** I said there wasn't any truck.

A long pause. Here, at home, the silence input holds.

**ELLIS:** Why?

Wayne looks at the window over the sink. The same window he looked at when Riley asked him about the box.

**WAYNE:** I couldn't have it be nobody's.

That's all of it. He doesn't explain further. He doesn't apologize.

The player gets Ellis's options. The medication makes them few and plain:

- **"You let me think I killed her."** Wayne: *"Yes."* That's the whole answer.
- **"You said I was speeding. A hundred times."** Wayne: *"I know what I said."*
- **Silence.** Ellis holds it. Wayne holds it. The refrigerator runs. Tater isn't here to fill it.

Whatever the player chooses, the next thing Ellis says is the same:

**ELLIS:** Where's he live? The man.

**WAYNE:** Sylva. North Carolina. Pruitt looked it up.

**ELLIS:** I want to go.

## MISSION 5 — SYLVA

**Playable:** Ellis · **Where:** the road north out of the map; Sylva, North Carolina (off-map); a roadside stand on the highway south of town · **When:** Saturday June 26, 6:00 a.m. – 7:00 p.m. · **Length:** ~30 min

### North

Wayne picks Ellis up at the foot of the Knob House lane at six in the morning in the F-100. The player gets in on the passenger side.

The road north from Stony Knob goes up through the North highlands and off the edge of the map: the fire tower going by, the last Georgia mailbox, a hand-painted sign for a Holiness revival, and then the travel transition, a green sign, NORTH CAROLINA WELCOMES YOU, and the mountains getting bigger and darker and wetter.

  **ELLIS:** How do you know?
  **WAYNE:** Somebody did the chickweed.

If the player talks, Wayne answers with one word. Except once. ### Hollis Beam

Sylva: a mountain county seat with a courthouse on a hill at the head of Main Street, up a long flight of steps, and a river, and a tannery smell when the wind's wrong.

Hollis Beam lives three miles out, in a small brick house on a hillside with a garden bigger than the house. He's fifty-eight, in overalls, in the tomato rows with a hoe when the truck pulls in. He's been expecting them; Pruitt called him on Wednesday.

He comes down to the truck and stops a few feet away and wipes his hands on his overalls and then goes to the spigot by the porch and washes them properly before he'll shake. He shakes Wayne's hand. Then Ellis's, and holds it a second too long, looking at his face.

**HOLLIS:** You got tall.

His wife, **Nell**, brings iced tea out to the porch and sets it down and goes back in and doesn't come out again. That's the manners of the place.

### The porch

Three chairs on a porch facing the mountains. Hollis in one, Wayne in one, Ellis in the middle.

This is a conversation the player drives by topic (*the truck; the car; what you heard; after; the funeral*) and by silence. Hollis has thought about this every day for three years. He's afraid of saying anything he isn't sure of.

The truck:

**HOLLIS:** It had its brights on. A pickup, dark. Ford or Chevrolet, I couldn't tell you, and I've tried. It came around that bend like it owned both sides of the road.

The car:

**HOLLIS:** You went right. I saw your right wheels drop off the edge. There's no shoulder there, just the clay. You came back hard. That's what anybody does. That's what I'd have done.

**ELLIS:** How fast was I going?

**HOLLIS:** I was doing forty-five. I was a hundred and fifty yards behind you, and you weren't pulling away from me. I told the deputy that. I'll tell anybody that.

Wayne, in his chair, looks at the mountains.

What he heard:

**HOLLIS:** I got to the car. The doors wouldn't open. You were saying you were sorry. Over and over. I'm sorry, I'm sorry.

He stops. He looks at Ellis to see if he should go on. The player can hold silence, and Hollis will go on. Or the player can ask, and he'll go on.

**HOLLIS:** And she said— she told you to stop saying sorry. Just like that. Like a sister. "Stop saying sorry."

**HOLLIS:** I ran to the nearest house. The Hensley place. Theirs was the only porch light on the road. The lady there let me use the phone in the hall. Then she got a blanket and came back down the road with me, in her housecoat, in the rain.

**HOLLIS:** When we got back she was talking. Your sister. Low. To you. I couldn't make it out. I'm sorry. I've thought about it for three years. I can't tell you what she said.

**ELLIS:** You couldn't hear any of it.

**HOLLIS:** No, son. The rain. And I was trying to get your door.

**HOLLIS:** I tried to pull you out the window, because of the gasoline. You fought me like a cat. You said you had to stay where she could see you.

**HOLLIS:** She passed before Pettigrew got there. I was holding the flashlight, and the lady from the house, she had her hand. That's all I know. That's everything I know.

The funeral (only if the player asks):

**HOLLIS:** I went. I stood in the back. There was a woman back there too. Dark-headed. Pretty. She left before the dinner.

Wayne makes a sound, very small. He knows who she was.

### Wayne and Hollis

At the end, when there's nothing left to ask, Wayne speaks. He hasn't said a word since they sat down.

**WAYNE:** Pruitt told me what you said. The next day. I said you were wrong.

**HOLLIS:** I know. He told me that too.

A long pause.

**WAYNE:** I'm sorry.

He says it to Hollis Beam, a seed salesman he's never met, on a porch in North Carolina, before he's ever said it to his son. Ellis hears it.

**HOLLIS:** Well.

That's all Hollis says. He means it kindly.

At the truck, Hollis shakes Ellis's hand again, and holds it again.

**HOLLIS:** Are you all right, son?

The medicated Ellis gives the honest answer; the options are all versions of it:

**ELLIS:** I'm on some medicine.

**HOLLIS:** Well. Good.

### The tailgate

On the highway south of Sylva, a gravel pull-off with a view of the Balsams, and a roadside stand: a folding table, a propane burner, a steel pot, a hand-lettered sign (BOILED P-NUTS · 50¢), and an old man in a lawn chair.

Wayne pulls off without asking and buys two paper sacks, wet through at the bottom. He drops the tailgate. They sit on it, side by side, with the mountains in front of them, and eat boiled peanuts, cracking them with their thumbs, sucking the brine, dropping the shells in the gravel.

Then:

**WAYNE:** Grace hated these.

**ELLIS:** She ate them anyway.

**WAYNE:** Every one.

Ellis cracks another. He tastes it, seriously.

**ELLIS:** Second.

**WAYNE:** Out of what?

**ELLIS:** All of them. Every stand between the two cities. And now this one.

**WAYNE:** You rank them?

**ELLIS:** Somebody has to.

Wayne eats one. He considers it.

**WAYNE:** Hm. Second.

The stand man in his lawn chair has heard every word and looks enormously pleased.

**WAYNE:** I'm sorry.

- **"Okay."**
- **"Me too."** Wayne looks at him. He doesn't know what Ellis means, yet.
- **Silence.** Ellis cracks a peanut. Wayne cracks one.

It doesn't fix anything. Wayne says it anyway.

**WAYNE:** These are too salty.

**ELLIS:** That's why they're second.

## MISSION 6 — THE ARGUMENT

**Playable:** Ellis (at sixteen) → Ellis → Riley · **Where:** a 1968 Chevrolet Impala on Tolliver Road, Thursday April 12, 1973 (memory); Knob House kitchen; the dock · **When:** Tuesday June 29, 2:40 – 3:50 a.m. · **Length:** ~20 min

Ten days after the report. Three after Sylva. The medication usually puts Ellis under by eleven and keeps him there until seven. Tonight he wakes up at 2:40 a.m. and can't get back.

Knob House, dark. Everyone asleep. Tater across the door. The player walks Ellis downstairs in his socks to the kitchen for a glass of water. He sits at the long pine table with it. It starts to rain on the roof.

**ELLIS:** No.

**ELLIS:** It's a bar, Grace. Daddy'd kill us both.

Silence.

Knob House. 2:51 a.m. The water glass is on its side on the pine table, and water is running off the edge onto the floor. The rain is heavy on the roof.

### Riley

## MISSION 7 — FLUSH

**Playable:** Ellis · **Where:** Knob House; the great room; the Stony Knob fire tower · **When:** Thursday July 1 – Sunday July 4 (the Bicentennial) · **Length:** ~25 min

### Day one

Thursday. 8:00 a.m. The kitchen sink. Riley at the stove with the first burnt pancake. Dean reading the sports page aloud to Theo, who doesn't care. The jelly glass. The white bottle.

Ellis shakes a small blue tablet into his palm.

There's one prompt on the screen, and it isn't *Take it.* It's:

*Palm it.*

No timer. Nobody's watching; Riley is flipping a pancake. When the player presses it, Ellis closes his hand, turns on the tap, drinks the water, and puts his closed hand in his jeans pocket. It's a mechanic's move, quick and easy, the way you'd pocket a bolt.

Upstairs, in his room, he puts the tablet in an empty Sucrets tin at the back of the dresser drawer.

> **Design note — why.** Ellis stops the medication because he remembered what he said to his sister, and the medicine keeps that at a distance, like weather behind a mountain, and he can't stand it being at a distance. And because Clara is the only Grace he has. He said *I'm not coming back for you* and she looked out the window, and then she was gone, and then Clara was there. He took a pill that sent Clara away too. He can't do it twice. The prompt names the act exactly (*Palm it*), like *Make her stop* in Chapter VIII.

### Day two

Friday, 3:05 a.m. Awake. The player walks the dark house: the gallery over the great room, the truck's red standby light through the kitchen window, the chimney, the lake through the west windows with the moon on it. Everyone asleep. Tater asleep across Ellis's door; the player has to step over him.

The Observe cue comes back, faint, after nearly three months. The line is still short: *Moon on the lake like a dropped plate.* It's longer than *Lake. Still.*

### Day three

Saturday afternoon. A session in the great room. The band in the square on the rugs. Frank in the truck.

The Room system is different today, and the player can feel it immediately: the sound has more presence, more air, a little more high end, like somebody took a blanket off the speakers. Ellis's hands are quicker. When the player uses *reach* ("Stay with me"), Ellis leaves the arrangement of "Knob House" and goes somewhere nobody planned, and the band follows him, all three at once, and it's the old thing, the thing that made them. Frank's voice on the talkback, from the truck: "Well, *hello*."

It's thrilling. Cal looks at Ellis across the square. Riley looks at Ellis across the square. Both of them notice he's brighter. Riley also notices he didn't come down for breakfast because he never went to bed.

### Day four

Sunday, July 4, 1976. The Bicentennial. Two hundred years.

Evening. The whole house goes down the gravel lane a quarter mile to the Stony Knob overlook in the van and Tully's truck, with beer and a sack of sparklers Dean bought at a fireworks stand on the state line. Frank stays in the truck, asleep with his headphones on.

The overlook. The guardrail. The long view both ways.

At 9:30 p.m. it starts:

- **West**, forty miles off, over the Laurel Gap, on the horizon: Laurel City's fireworks, small, silent, gold and red chrysanthemums opening on the rim of the world.
- **East**, forty-five miles off, over the Tanner Valley: Tannersville's, the same, a different rhythm, blue and white.
- **Below**, in the valley, close and loud: Hollow Ridge's own, launched from the high school football field by the volunteer fire department, three at a time, the thumps coming up the mountain a second late, echoing off the knob.

Both cities and the town between them, all going at once, and from here you can see them all.

Dean writes THE BLAKES in the air with a sparkler, backward, so it reads right from the other side. Theo and Cal sit on the hood of the van with their shoulders touching. Tully stands a little apart, arms folded, flinching slightly at each Hollow Ridge thump, and Dean, without saying anything, goes and stands next to him. Riley has her head back, glasses on, watching the east.

Ellis walks away from them up the path to the fire tower.

### The fire tower

The Stony Knob fire tower: a steel frame, sixty feet high, with switchback stairs up to a cab on top. Locked at the cab. The stairs are open.

On the first landing, sitting on a step with her boots on the rail, in the jean jacket, lit gold and blue and red by fireworks on two horizons at once, is Clara.

She's brighter than she's ever been. Sharper. More there.

**CLARA:** Hey.

**ELLIS:** Hey.

**CLARA:** You left me.

**ELLIS:** I know.

**CLARA:** Two months.

**ELLIS:** Twelve weeks. Since the eighth of April.

**CLARA:** You counted?

**ELLIS:** Cal counts. I just know what day it is.

She almost smiles.

**CLARA:** Don't do it again. Now sit down. You're blocking Tannersville.

He sits down on the step below her.

**ELLIS:** I remembered the car.

She looks at him.

**CLARA:** I know.

She does. She knows now what he knows.

**ELLIS:** Did she forgive me?

Clara doesn't answer right away. The fireworks in the west: three gold ones at once.

**CLARA:** Ask me something I know.

A long time. The Hollow Ridge fireworks thumping up the mountain.

**ELLIS:** Who are you?

Clara turns and looks at him, with Grace's eyes, and their mother's face, and says it right back, the same words, not a trick:

**CLARA:** Who are you?

He takes out memo book 71 and a pencil. He writes, by the light of the fireworks, on the fire-tower stairs, the first lines of the last song:

> *Who are you, in the jacket that was hers,*
> *on the stairs at the top of the world?*
> *I made a room in the house for you to stay in.*
> *I never asked who'd be living there.*

Clara reads it over his shoulder.

**CLARA:** That's good.

**ELLIS:** It's a start.

He writes WHO ARE YOU? at the top of the page, and underlines it twice.

Down at the overlook, Dean is yelling his name. The grand finale is starting in all three places at once. The player can go down, or stay on the stairs with her. Either way, when the last Hollow Ridge shell goes off and the thump comes up the mountain, the chapter's first half is over.

> **Setup.** "Who Are You?" is begun here, on the Fourth of July, unmedicated, on a fire tower, with Clara. Its final verse is never written. In Chapter X he improvises it in front of 150,000 people, and it's the last thing he ever sings.
> **Where the phrase comes from.** Dean said it first, in a getaway car in Chapter I, to a stranger named Tully. Clara says it here. Grace says it last, on a cassette the player hasn't heard yet.

## MISSION 8 — BOTTOM

### The bathroom

**DEAN:** I think I need help.

**CAL:** Okay.

Behind him, Theo is already sitting up and reaching for his shirt.

### Three nights

Stutter, in the doorway. The switch goes to Cal.

### Le Guin

Night two. Dean's room. Dean in bed, shaking a little, sweating, miserable, awake. Cal in a chair beside the bed with a paperback: Ursula K. Le Guin, *The Dispossessed*, the one he's been reading since Chapter I, now bent at the spine and swollen from being read in a bathtub.

He reads it aloud. He reads it the way Cal reads everything, in the voice he uses for wiring diagrams.

**DEAN:** Do the voices.

**CAL:** There are no voices. It's a book.

**DEAN:** Do Shevek.

**CAL:** I'm doing Shevek.

**DEAN:** That's how you do everybody.

**CAL:** That's how everybody sounds.

> **Design note.** The scene turns into something else in the middle of a sentence, and the book doesn't stop, and the voice doesn't change. It's the move the band makes onstage: somebody loses it, and the others keep playing so there's something to come back to.

### Night three

Dean sleeps. Cal is asleep in the chair with the book open on his chest, glasses crooked. The player can look at them both. Ellis left it there on the second night. Nobody mentions it. Theo takes the glasses off Cal's face and folds them and puts them on the nightstand next to a glass of water, and turns off the lamp.

### The step

Friday, July 16. Six days.

Tully drives Dean down the mountain in the crew truck (not the Corvette) to the Pine Knot trailer court behind the Starlite, at four in the afternoon, and parks, and stays in the truck.

**DEAN:** I'm six days.

**DEAN:** I'm not asking to come in. I just wanted you to know it was six.

**LYNETTE:** Sit on the step.

## MISSION 9 — THE TABERNACLE

**Playable:** all four (rapid switching) · **Where:** the Tabernacle, downtown Tannersville · **When:** Saturday July 17, 4:00 p.m. – 12:30 a.m. · **Length:** ~45 min

### The building

The Tabernacle was built in 1892 by a riverboat captain who got saved at a tent revival and wanted the preacher to have a roof. Red brick, tall arched windows with amber and green glass, curved wooden pews that seat 2,300, a gallery around three sides on iron columns, and a stage at the front where the *Tanner Valley Jubilee* broadcast every Saturday night on 50,000 watts from 1943 until March 1974, when the Jubilee moved to a new theater at a theme park in the suburbs with air conditioning and parking.

Since then the Tabernacle has been empty. The city council votes in September on whether to tear it down for a parking deck. A preservation group of eleven people, mostly retired schoolteachers, is holding a benefit to save it. Eddie Farris offered them the Blakes for nothing.

It sold out in fifty-five minutes. Tannersville, the city that didn't want them, the city three of them live in, the city whose music business said *that kind of thing*, bought every seat.

The player arrives as Cal, at four, with the crew, and gets to walk it empty:
- The pews, worn pale where two generations sat.
- The stage, the boards scarred by eighty years of boots, a circle of darker wood in the middle where the Jubilee's microphone stood.
- The gallery. A plaque on the rail about a benefactor.
- The acoustics. Cal stands on the stage and says "Check," once, not loud, and the whole building says it back, warm, clear, from every direction, and dies away in exactly the right amount of time. Theo, at a folding table in the center aisle where he'll mix, puts his hands flat on the table and says "Oh," and nothing else for a while.

Frank Dalton has called it the best room in the South for years.

### The class

Ellis's idea, three weeks ago on the telephone: bring a singing.

The Sacred Harp singings his grandmother took him to as a child were at country churches in the North highlands and over the line into Alabama, all-day singings with dinner on the grounds. The man who led them then still leads them now: **Elmer Tidwell**, eighty-one, a retired mail carrier with a voice like a barn door. Ellis called him from the party line at Knob House and asked if the class would come sing in the Tabernacle in Tannersville with a rock-and-roll band. Elmer said, "Is it loud?" Ellis said, "Yes sir." Elmer said, "Good."

Forty-one singers come in two church buses and a line of cars: farmers, schoolteachers, a veterinarian, three generations of one family, a boy of twelve who sings bass already. They carry their oblong maroon books. They arrange four rows of chairs on the stage in a hollow square: tenors on one side, facing the altos; trebles facing the basses. The leader stands in the middle and beats time with his hand. Nobody faces the audience. Nobody ever has.

And in the alto row, in a navy dress, with her own book, is **Joan Riley**.

She's been driving to singings in Alabama since Thanksgiving. Once a month. She didn't tell Riley until last week.

The player, as Cal, sees Riley see her mother take her seat in the hollow square, and sees Riley's face.

### The crowd

The doors open at seven. The player can switch freely now among the four as they get ready, and each of them looks out from the wings and sees someone:

- **Cal** sees his parents, in the third row. Walt Mercer has never been to one of their shows. He's in his church suit. Dot is waving. Walt isn't, and doesn't need to.
- **Dean** sees his parents and Patty, in the sixth row. Richard Holloway in a blazer, looking at the Tabernacle's ceiling the way he looks at a well-built balance sheet. Dean is seven days sober. He hasn't told them. He'll tell them tomorrow at six o'clock.
- **Riley** sees her father, next to the aisle, in his tie with the atoms on it, and Professor Landry and his wife, halfway back, Landry looking deeply suspicious of the whole proceeding.
- **Ellis** sees Clara, in the wings beside him, radiant, watching him pick at the tape on his set list. "Quit that," she says. "You'll have nothing to read." And then, if the player lets him look up at the gallery, he sees, in the second row of the gallery, at the rail, a man in a hat. Wayne came. Roy Cagle is beside him in a sport coat that doesn't fit.

### "Wondrous Love"

It opens with the class, alone.

The house lights go down. The stage lights come up on forty-one people in a hollow square, facing each other, and an eighty-one-year-old man in the middle with his hand raised. There is no band on the stage.

Elmer Tidwell calls the page number in a voice that reaches the back of the gallery without a microphone. They sing the shapes first, *fa sol la mi*, at full voice, in that building, and two thousand three hundred people who came for a rock band sit absolutely still.

Then the words:

*What wondrous love is this, O my soul, O my soul…*

The player is Ellis, in the wings, in the dark. He's been hearing this music since he was six. It's the music his grandmother sang. It's the sound under "Shape Note," under the drones, under every open fifth the Blakes have ever played.

With *attention*, the player can look up at the gallery during the second verse.

Wayne Blake is standing up at the rail. He's singing. The bass line, from memory, a line his mother taught him forty years ago in a country church. The player can hear him, faintly, when they look: the mix lets one voice come through out of two thousand three hundred. A big rough bass, half a beat behind, sure of every note. He's forty-seven, and he sings like somebody who used to be loud and funny and stopped.

When the player looks back at the wings, Clara is gone.

### The set

On the last verse of "Wondrous Love," the band walks on in the dark and takes the fourth side of the square, in the tenor row, and comes in under the class, soft, with the drone, and the class sings on over it, and "Wondrous Love" becomes "Shape Note," live, the way Ellis heard it in his head in a dark chapel a year ago.

The performance is the richest the Room system gets before Chapter X. The switching is rapid now, on the band's cues and on the player's choice: the four of them as "different organs of one body," each with their own verbs, the switch happening on a look, a headstock, a nod, a stick click. The crowd model is the Tabernacle's: two thousand three hundred people in curved pews in the best room in the South, and they respond to everything, instantly, the way the Lantern did, but bigger and more careful.

- **"Low Water."** The class stays on stage in the square and listens. Two of the old men tap their books on their knees.
- **"Still Here."** The crowd sings *Nobody here knows you* and it goes around the whole horseshoe of the gallery and comes back.
- **"Sunday Clothes."** Riley sings it. Her song, in her voice, in her key, the way nobody has heard it since the radio took it. The crowd knows it in Ellis's voice from the single, and the player, as Riley, can hear them hear the difference. In the hollow square behind her, in the alto row, her mother is singing the harmony under her own song, from memory. The player can use Riley's *attention* to turn and look at her. Joan is looking at her book, which is closed on her lap.
- **"Tomorrow Problem."** Dean, seven days sober, plays it better than he ever has, slower, with space in it, and the crowd shouts the title back, and a twelve-year-old bass singer from Alabama shouts it too, not knowing what it means.
- **"New Skin."** Its first public performance. And it's where the night cracks.

### The lost verse

"New Skin" was written on the medication, in daylight, at a kitchen table. Ellis has been off the medication for seventeen days. The player is Ellis. The first verse comes; it's the part he wrote before the pills.

The second verse doesn't come.

The player can feel it happen. The camera tightens. The sound narrows (not a switch; the other thing, a room going away). Ellis is at the mic, and the band is going around into the second verse, and he doesn't have it. Not the words. Not the shape of it. The song was written by a man he can't reach from here. He opens his mouth and nothing is in it.

Clara is at his shoulder, singing something, but it's the wrong song. It's "Still Here." It's her song.

Switch, on a stutter the player didn't choose: the camera goes to Cal.

The player is Cal. Cal has one second to decide, and the game gives the player the verb they've used as Cal since Chapter I, *follow*, turned the other way around. Instead of catching Ellis's deviation and turning it into a change, Cal can go up the neck of the bass, high, where he never plays, and play the vocal melody of the second verse, note for note, loud enough in the monitors for Ellis to hear it.

If the player does it, Ellis hears it. He finds the melody under the words, and the words come back behind it, a line late, and he sings the verse a line late with Cal playing it under him like a man walking a horse across a creek.

If the player doesn't, Riley sings the verse from her mic (she's heard him sing it forty times at the lodge), and Ellis comes in on the chorus. It works. It's not as good.

Either way, the crowd thinks it's an arrangement. Nobody in the pews knows. Frank, in the back of the house next to Theo, knows. Joan, in the square, knows. Wayne, in the gallery, knows.

### Holy Manna

The encore. Ellis at the mic:

**ELLIS:** There's somebody up here who can lead one. Mrs. Riley.

Joan looks up from the alto row like she's been shot.

Riley, at her mic, turns around and looks at her mother.

Elmer Tidwell steps out of the middle of the square and holds his hand out to Joan, and after a long moment, she stands up, and puts her book down on her chair, and walks into the middle of the hollow square, where the leader stands, and raises her hand.

The player is Riley, with no verb. She watches her mother call out a page number in the Tannersville Tabernacle in front of two thousand three hundred people, and beat the time, and bring in forty-one singers and a rock band and, by the second verse, the whole building, on "Holy Manna":

*Brethren, we have met to worship*
*and adore the Lord our God…*

It's the hymn from the Linwood living room at Thanksgiving. Joan Riley is leading it. The organist who gave up Oberlin in 1949 and played Bach alone on Saturday mornings for twenty-six years is standing in the middle of the loudest music in the South with her hand in the air, and everyone is singing to her, because that's how the square works: everyone faces the leader.

Up in the gallery, Tom Riley is standing on a pew. Landry has his hand over his mouth.

> **Design note.** The set piece of Chapter IX: a building the country-music establishment abandoned, a sound older than country music, and one hymn led by a woman who never got to lead anything. Riley has no line.

### After

The alley behind the Tabernacle, midnight. The class loading their buses. Elmer Tidwell shakes Ellis's hand and says "Your grandmama would've hollered," which is the highest praise in Sacred Harp.

Brief things, each one playable for a moment as whoever is nearest:
- **Walt Mercer** finds Cal at the stage door, in his church suit, and shakes his hand, which he has never done. Then he shakes Theo's, who is standing beside Cal. He doesn't say anything. Dot hugs both of them and talks for four minutes without stopping.
- **Richard Holloway** finds Dean by the buses. Dean is shaking a little; seven days. Richard looks at him for a long time.
  **RICHARD:** Dinner's at six.
  **DEAN:** I know.
  **RICHARD:** Tomorrow.
  **DEAN:** I'll be there.
  Richard nods, once, and goes to find the car. Patty, behind him, punches Dean in the arm, hard, and then hugs him, harder.
- **Landry** finds Riley. "I hated most of it," he says. "The part with your mother I didn't hate."
- **Wayne** doesn't come backstage. The player, as Ellis, can go out front and look for him; the F-100 is already pulling away down Front Street, with Roy's arm out the passenger window, waving at nobody.

## MISSION 10 — COUNT

**Playable:** Riley · **Where:** Knob House, Ellis's room; the kitchen; the gravel lane · **When:** Monday July 19, 9:00 a.m. – 12:30 p.m. · **Length:** ~15 min

### What she's seen

Since the Fourth, Riley has watched Ellis get brighter. He plays like he used to. He writes. He laughs on time. He doesn't sleep. She's heard him on the dock at three in the morning, talking, quietly, in the pauses of a conversation with someone who doesn't answer out loud. She watched him lose a verse at the Tabernacle.

Monday morning. Everyone's at the lake. Ellis is in the truck with Frank. The player, as Riley, has the house.

### The count

Ellis's room at the east end, over the kitchen. The bed made badly. Loretta leaned in the corner. The Jazzmaster case. The green notebook on the nightstand, which Riley gave him ten months ago. It isn't blank anymore; the player can see ink on the edges of the pages, but Riley doesn't open it. (She won't, until the epilogue.)

The white pharmacy bottle is on the dresser.

The player can pick it up and read the label. *Trifluoperazine 2 mg. Take one tablet by mouth twice daily. Qty: 60. Filled 6/24/76.*

Twice a day since the twenty-fourth of June is fifty tablets. There should be ten left.

The player can count them. It's a real count: Riley pours them into her palm and pushes them across the dresser top with one finger, two at a time, and the game counts with her, out loud, in her head. Riley can count faster than the player, and she does it again.

Forty.

She checks the date on the label. She counts again. Forty. He hasn't taken one since July first.

The player can search the drawer. The Sucrets tin at the back. Six more inside.

### The kitchen

She waits in the kitchen with the bottle and the tin on the pine table in front of her. The player can sit, or stand at the sink, or go out on the back porch. It's a long wait. Ellis comes in from the truck at 11:40 for coffee, and stops in the door.

He sees the bottle. He sees her face.

**RILEY:** Since the first.

**ELLIS:** Yeah.

**RILEY:** Why?

He doesn't answer right away. The player, as Riley, can wait him out.

**ELLIS:** I couldn't.

**RILEY:** Couldn't what?

**ELLIS:** Lose her twice.

He says it simply. It's true, and she knows it, and it doesn't help.

**ELLIS:** I can hear again, Riley. I heard a fish jump this morning from my bed, with the window shut. I wrote "Who Are You?" on the fire tower. I'm better.

**RILEY:** Define "better."

**ELLIS:** I'm playing like— you heard the Tabernacle.

**RILEY:** I heard you lose a verse in front of my mother.

**ELLIS:** Cal got me. *(If Riley sang the verse: "You got me.")*

**RILEY:** Cal got you. *(Or: "I got you.")*

The player chooses how Riley says the thing she's learned this year. Every option says it; the choice is how hard.

- *"It doesn't show you anything, Ellis. It just turns everything up. I spent two years thinking it showed me things. It showed me my own hand."*
- *"You're not hearing better. You're hearing more. Those aren't the same."*
- *"I did the same thing. With a different bottle. It lied to me too."*

He doesn't argue. He doesn't agree either.

### I can't watch

**RILEY:** Are you going to take them?

**ELLIS:** I don't know.

That's honest too.

Riley pushes her glasses up with a knuckle. She stands up.

**RILEY:** I can't watch you choose her.

She doesn't say anything after it. She goes upstairs.

### The lane

She packs in twenty minutes. The Martin. The Rickenbacker. Gerald, who came up to the lodge in May in a coffee can. The scrapbook. Her notebooks. Her father's peacoat, which she didn't need all summer.

The player carries it all out to the Datsun, one trip at a time, past the kitchen, where Ellis is still sitting at the pine table with the bottle.

On the last trip, Cal is standing by the Datsun. He's come up from the lake. He knows. Everyone at Knob House knows how to read a car being packed.

**CAL:** Session's Wednesday.

**RILEY:** I'll be here Wednesday. I'm not leaving the band. I'm leaving the house.

**CAL:** Okay.

**RILEY:** I'll be at my parents'. Mama can have me for August.

**CAL:** Tell her I've never heard anything like Saturday.

**RILEY:** I'll tell her.

She gets in the car. The player drives her down the gravel lane, past the chain, past the fire tower, past the overlook, down the switchbacks, past the pull-off (the ditch where the Corvette was still has tire marks in it), out of the North highlands and through the Laurel Gap to Linwood. It's forty-five minutes. Riley doesn't cry until the Laurel Gap, and then she does, and drives the rest of the way that way, carefully, at the speed limit.

> **Design note — Riley's choice.** The bible said Riley was wrong about three things: that altered perception reveals truth, that understanding someone gives you a say in what happens to them, and that she had special access to Ellis. This chapter takes all three away from her. What's left is the thing she's right about: she can't make him choose, and she won't stand in a kitchen and watch him not. She leaves the house and keeps the band. Nobody makes her.

## MISSION 11 — EXCERPTS

**Playable:** Ellis · **Where:** the Hollow Ridge Carnegie library; Main Street · **When:** Thursday July 29, 2:00 – 3:30 p.m. · **Length:** ~15 min

### The library

The Carnegie library on Main Street: one room, tall windows, a radiator, oak tables, a card catalog, and **Mrs. Odom**, who has been sliding books across the desk at Ellis without comment since he was eleven.

Ellis comes in to return Elizabeth Bishop. He's been off the medication four weeks. He's been writing constantly. Clara is with him, on the radiator, with her boots on the windowsill.

Mrs. Odom takes the Bishop. She doesn't slide anything back. Instead she reaches under the desk and takes out a magazine and puts it on the counter in front of him, face up, and folds her hands.

*Rave*, September 1976. On the cover, a detail of the Bowery photograph (the player's version), cropped to Ellis's face. The cover line:

> **THE NOTEBOOKS OF ELLIS BLAKE**
> **Twelve Pages from the Western Auto**
> **Introduction by Dex Lundgren**

### How

The player learns it from Dex's introduction, which is honest about everything except whether it should exist:

> *In January, Monarch Records' publicity department, preparing a press kit, photocopied several of Blake's pocket notebooks, the numbered Western Auto memo books he's kept since he was fourteen, from the band's van, where he kept the carton. Monarch provided these pages to Rave. One more page came into my possession on the band's tour bus that month, in a way I'm not proud of. I have held it for six months. I'm printing it now because I think it's the best thing any American has written this year and I think he'll never publish it himself.*

The player can read the pages. They're laid out like facsimiles, Ellis's carpenter's-pencil handwriting reproduced on the magazine page, with the lines typed beside it.

### The lines

This is where the hidden-poet system pays off. The lines *Rave* prints are drawn from the Observe lines the player collected across the whole game, from the memo books Monarch copied (numbers 1 through 67; memo book 68 was in Ellis's jacket that week). The game chooses the best of what the player gathered. If the player gathered little, an authored fallback set fills in. A typical page looks like this:

> *The only thing awake in Room 12 is the ice machine.* (IV)
> *A plate for whoever comes. Somebody always does.* (VII)
> *They say my name like they're giving something back.* (VII)
> *An ice bucket in the middle of the hall like somebody set down a question and ran.* (VII)
> *Everybody I've got, asleep behind me at fifty-five.* (VII)
> *The trees on Stony Knob hold the signal the way you'd hold a cup you didn't want to spill.* (III)
> *She held the light so still I could hear the rain hit it.* (VII)

And, on its own page, set in larger type: the torn page from memo book 68. The first half of "New Skin."

> *I gave you a birthday every year you didn't get one.*
> *I gave you a jacket that was already yours…*

(It's the bus version. *Didn't get one. Was already.* The song on the album, recorded in June, says *didn't have one*, *already was*.)

> **Design note — the hidden poet.** Since Chapter I, the Observe button has looked like a collectible journal. It was always this. Every line the player let Ellis write down is a line the country can now read without his permission.

### Mrs. Pardue

A voice from the reference table by the window.

**MRS. PARDUE:** I told you so, Ellis.

Mrs. Pardue, in a cardigan in July, with the same magazine open in front of her. She taught English at Hollow Ridge High for thirty years. The player met her in Chapter I, with a Rambler that honked like a goose.

**MRS. PARDUE:** In 1973. In the eleventh grade. I told you you wrote better than anybody I'd taught in twenty years.

**ELLIS:** Yes ma'am.

**MRS. PARDUE:** You didn't believe me.

**ELLIS:** No ma'am.

**MRS. PARDUE:** Do you now?

The player chooses: *"No ma'am."* / *"Some."* / silence. Whatever he says, she closes the magazine.

**MRS. PARDUE:** They shouldn't have taken it. It's yours.

She goes back to her reading.

### Clara

**CLARA:** That one's mine.

**CLARA:** Same thing. Hush. You're in a library.

**CLARA:** Now everybody's got me.

## MISSION 12 — BENCH

**Playable:** Ellis → Cal · **Where:** Mercer Radio & TV, east Tannersville · **When:** Tuesday August 3, 4:00 – 6:00 p.m. · **Length:** ~20 min

### The shop

The player, as Ellis, has driven the Valiant to east Tannersville, to a street by the rail yard, to a shop the player has been inside many times as Cal and never as Ellis.

Mercer Radio & TV. The smell of solder and dust burning off tubes. Radios on every shelf, televisions with their backs off, a bench under a lamp. Walt Mercer at the bench with his loupe.

**WALT:** He's at Vale's till five.

**ELLIS:** I know. I came to see if I could use your bench.

Walt looks at him through the loupe, then over it.

**WALT:** You know how to solder?

**ELLIS:** No sir.

**WALT:** Take your time.

He goes and sits in the chair by the front window with the newspaper, where he can see.

### Bad at it

This is a repair minigame, and Ellis is bad at it. The iron's too hot or not hot enough. The solder balls up. He uses too much. He burns his finger and says a word Walt pretends not to hear. The joint is ugly.

He's a mechanic. He can hear a bad lifter across a parking lot. He's never done this. It's Cal's world, and everything in it is small.

On the shelf over the bench, the player can see it: an old Philco cathedral radio, 1930s, walnut, in perfect condition, not for sale. The only radio in the shop without a job ticket.

If the player looks at it, Ellis looks at it for a long time.

### Cal

At 5:20, Cal comes in the front door from Vale's. Walt, from the window, without lowering the paper:

**WALT:** Your friend's here.

Stutter, at the door. The camera goes to Cal.

The player, as Cal, sees Ellis Blake at his father's bench, hunched over a clock radio with an iron, with a burned finger in his mouth and a solder joint that looks like a wad of gum.

**CAL:** You're using too much solder.

**ELLIS:** Allegedly.

### The apology

Ellis, not looking up from the joint:

**ELLIS:** What I said on the bus.

**ELLIS:** It was the worst thing I ever said to anybody.

A pause.

**ELLIS:** Except one.

Then Cal says the thing he's carried for a year.

**CAL:** I've known since August. Last August.

**ELLIS:** Known what?

**CAL:** That she was in the car. The night you were driving to Marlon's. Your first Thursday.

Ellis puts the iron down in its stand.

**CAL:** Somebody told me. I didn't tell you I knew.

**ELLIS:** Who?

**CAL:** Doesn't matter.

**ELLIS:** Why didn't you tell me?

**CAL:** I promised somebody.

Ellis looks at him for a long time. The player can see him decide not to push.

**ELLIS:** Okay.

A pause.

**ELLIS:** Did it change anything?

**CAL:** I counted more.

## Chapter IX — design summary

### What happened
The band made a second album in a lodge on a mountain above the house where Ellis grew up. On the medication, Ellis slept and swam and finished a song about his sister in daylight, and charmed the country on television, and Riley said *that's not him*, and Cal said *that's him too*. His father opened a report that had been in a box for three years. A seed salesman on a porch in North Carolina said *you weren't pulling away from me*, and *she told you to stop saying sorry*, and *I couldn't make it out*. On a tailgate Wayne apologized, and it didn't fix anything. Ellis remembered the car and looked at his sister, with the player's hands, and a truck came around a bend. He stopped the pills, because he couldn't lose her twice, and on the Fourth of July he found Clara on a fire tower and asked her a question she couldn't answer. Dean put a Corvette in a ditch and poured it out and walked up a mountain and knocked on a door. Joan Riley led a hymn in the Tabernacle. Cal walked Ellis back into a verse on the bass. Riley counted to forty and left the house and kept the band. A magazine printed the lines the player collected, and called them genius. Ellis was terrible at soldering and Cal forgave him. Ellis found every dollar of his rent in a coffee can, and went into Grace's room, and met her alive for one afternoon in 1972, and closed the door on a fourteen-year-old who couldn't be her. He asked his father to come to New York, and his father said *Roy's got a week coming*. He wrote *Dad — Sunday?*

### The reveals, and what's held back
- **Revealed:** the report (a truck over the line; *we were arguing and I looked at her*). Wayne knew the next day. Hollis Beam: *you weren't pulling away from me*; *she told you to stop saying sorry*; the Hensley porch light and the lady with the blanket; *she was talking and I couldn't make it out*; *you had to stay where she could see you*. The argument, in full: *When I go, I'm not coming back for you.* Grace alive: bossy, silly, *later*. WGRC. *You're my brother. Unfortunately.* The coffee can.
- **Held back, for Chapter X:** what Grace said after the white. "Stop saying sorry" is known; "Liar," "It's okay" and "Go on" are not. The player has everything except the last three things she said.

### The medication, honestly
The chapter was built so that nothing good is reserved for being off the pills and nothing bad is blamed on being on them. On: sleep, swimming, the pie (VIII), Riley's laugh, "New Skin" finished, a calm he's never had, and a television appearance so fluent Riley didn't know him. Off: Clara, the reach, the Room thrilling again, "Who Are You?" begun, and 3 a.m., talking to air, a lost verse in front of 2,300 people, and Riley leaving the house. Neither list wins. The chapter shows why he chose, and what the choice cost the people watching.

### Riley
She watched her mother lead a hymn in the middle of the loudest music in the South. She counted pills, twice. She said *I can't watch you choose her.* She left the house and kept the band, and cried in the Laurel Gap at the speed limit. She played him her song on a dock, and it wasn't about him, and he asked her to stand where he'd turn.

### Cal
Fished and caught nothing, on purpose now. Told the story of his grandfather and the Philco. Said *He slept eight hours. That's him too.* Brought Theo up the stairs as himself. Walked Ellis back into a verse with the bass high up the neck. Read Le Guin in one voice for three nights. Taught Ellis to solder. Said *I counted more*, and forgave him with one word, and kept Marlon's name out of it.

### Dean
Sat in a lot four times watching a woman refill ketchups. Put a Corvette in a ditch at the pull-off where Wayne used to listen. Poured it out. Walked two miles in the dark with nothing in his hands. *I think I need help.* Three nights, one voice for everyone, and Theo doing a Cajun Shevek until the laugh turned into something else. Woke up with the Starlite Polaroid on his nightstand. Six days, on a step, with a glass of tea and a five-year-old playing drums. Played "Tomorrow Problem" sober in the Tabernacle with space in it. Said *I'll be there* to his father, and went, at six.

### Wayne
Watched his son be nice about Grace on television and called a deputy. Opened the envelope with his own knife and handed his son the knife. *I couldn't have it be nobody's.* Drove to North Carolina with every clipping about the band in the glovebox. Apologized to a stranger and then to his son, both times to the mountains. Stood up in the gallery of the Tabernacle and sang the bass line his mother taught him. Kept every dollar of the rent. *Go on in.* *You want me there?*
