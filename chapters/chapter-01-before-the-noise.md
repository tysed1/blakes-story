# CHAPTER I — BEFORE THE NOISE

> **Hollow Ridge · Tannersville · Thursday, October 10 – Saturday, October 19, 1974**
> Main-story runtime: ~4.5 hours · Playable: Ellis Blake, Riley, Dean Holloway, Cal Mercer

## Chapter thesis

The player should finish this chapter believing they watched the accidental beginning of a rock band. They have actually met four people shortly before their lives became inseparable, and a family arranged around a door nobody opens. Nothing here should feel like setup. It should feel like autumn in 1974 and like life. On a first playthrough, Chapter I is funny, warm, a little sore, and very much a place. On a second playthrough it should be hard to sit through.

## World state at open

- Hollow Ridge: autumn, maples turning on the ridges, woodsmoke in the mornings, a freight every forty minutes. The railroad is down to sixty men in town and everyone knows the number is still falling.
- Nobody outside Marlon's Thursday regulars knows Ellis plays. Riley, Cal and Dean have never heard of Hollow Ridge or each other, except that everybody at Tannersville College has heard of Dean.
- Money is scarce. Prices are 1974 prices. Every purchase costs another purchase.
- Period texture on screen this week: WIN buttons (Ford's speech was Tuesday), talk of the Ali–Foreman fight at the end of the month, the pardon still making men at Marlon's angry, "Sweet Home Alabama" on every jukebox.

## COLD OPEN

Black screen.

A guitar string, plucked. It's flat. Someone turns a tuning peg a quarter turn and plucks it again. Closer.

Under it, the room assembles itself out of sound before we see it: a pool ball cracking into another and one of them dropping; a man laughing a beat too long at his own joke; ice in a glass; the last bars of "Sweet Home Alabama" from a jukebox and the mechanical *chunk* of the tonearm lifting; somewhere far off down the valley, a freight horn, two longs, a short, a long, for a crossing nobody here can see.

The string again. In tune.

A pull-chain clicks and a single amber bulb comes on over a stage eight feet wide made of plywood laid across pallets.

**Marlon's Tavern. Hollow Ridge, Georgia. Thursday.**

There are sixteen people in the room. Most of them aren't here for the kid on the stool.

The kid on the stool is Ellis Blake: eighteen, thin, dark hair a month past a haircut, work boots still gray with the day's garage floor, a white scar through his left eyebrow. The guitar across his knee is a cheap black Sears Silvertone whose case, open behind him on the stage, is also its amplifier. He's bent over it listening to the low string like it might tell him something.

Behind the bar, Marlon Pettit (late fifties, reading glasses on a cord, sleeves rolled past forearms like hams) is drying a glass that's been dry for a while.

**MARLON:** You tuning that thing or negotiating with it?

Ellis doesn't look up.

**ELLIS:** We're close.

**A MAN AT THE POOL TABLE:** Play the damn thing.

**ELLIS:** See, now he's jealous.

Two people laugh. Ellis grins for half a second, the kind of grin that's gone before you're sure you saw it.

The game hands the player control without announcing it. There is no tutorial card and no title. The HUD fades up so faintly that most players won't notice it: in the top corner, small, the day of the week. **THURSDAY.**

## MISSION 1 — THE GIG

**Playable:** Ellis · **Where:** Marlon's Tavern → Hollow Ridge → the Blake house · **When:** Thursday Oct 10, 1974, 9:40 p.m. – 12:40 a.m. · **Length:** ~40 min

### The room

The player can stand up before playing. If they do:

**MARLON:** Where you going?

**ELLIS:** Building suspense.

**MARLON:** Sit down.

Marlon's is a 1930s brick bar with a 1950s cinderblock addition that never quite agreed with it. The player can walk the whole room and nobody will stop them. Interactables, each with a small behavior and, where noted, an **Observe** line (see *The notebook*, below):

- **The chalkboard by the door.** In Marlon's block capitals: THURSDAY — ELLIS BLAKE / FRIDAY — THE COUNTY LINE BOYS / SAT — 8-BALL TOURNEY $2. Somebody has drawn a very small, very anatomical addition next to "8-BALL." Marlon has half-erased it.
- **The handwritten set list** taped to the Silvertone's case: nine songs in carpenter's pencil, three crossed out, one with a question mark.
- **The cigarette machine.** Winstons are fifty cents. Ellis has a dollar and change. Buying a pack is possible and costs him.
- **The pool table.** Two railroad men, Earl Pike and a younger one named Dwayne, are in the middle of a game and a bad mood. Earl works on Wayne Blake's section crew. If Ellis gets close: *"Your daddy know you're down here?" / "Probably." / "Section's down to four men, you know that? Four. On eleven miles."* Ellis: *"I heard."* That's all.
- **The *Fireball* pinball machine.** Playable. Ellis is good at it. (Remember this when he claims to be good at pool.)
- **The jukebox.** Twenty-five cents a play, three for fifty. The selection is mostly country with a rock row: Skynyrd, CCR, the Allmans, Bad Company, a Jim Croce, a Dionne Warwick and the Spinners somebody's wife asked for.
- **The newspaper** on the bar: the Tannersville *Banner*, Wednesday's. FORD ASKS NATION TO "WHIP INFLATION NOW." A red WIN button is pinned to the cash register. It's hard to tell if Marlon is being sincere.
- **The back exit.** A steel door propped open with a brick. Beyond it the alley and, beyond that, the rail line.
- **The bar telephone.** A black rotary set. Picking it up gets a dial tone. There's nobody Ellis would call.
- **Loretta's case.** Inside the lid, in green felt-tip, faded, a name: *LORETTA*. The Observe prompt does not appear here.

The overheard conversations overlap and never pause for the player:

- A farmer at the end of the bar on diesel prices: *"Forty-one cents. For diesel. My daddy'd have shot somebody."*
- Two men on Ali and Foreman, three weeks out: *"Foreman's gonna kill him." / "Ali's too pretty to die." / "Nobody's too pretty to die, Virgil."*
- Someone on the pardon: *"Ford can pardon whoever he wants. I didn't."*
- Someone saying Tannersville is getting too big and somebody else saying they said that about Tannersville in 1950.
- An older woman at the corner of the bar, Mrs. Opal Hensley, who has been stirring the same Coca-Cola and bourbon for an hour and who comes every Thursday. She says nothing to anybody. Nobody thinks anything of it.

Nobody in this room knows history is happening. Most of them never will.

> **Design note — Opal Hensley.** In Chapter IX the witness to the crash says he "ran to the Hensley house to call." Opal's was the telephone. She has come to Marlon's on Thursdays since Ellis started playing. She never explains herself and the game never points at her.

### The Room (performance system introduction)

When the player sits back on the stool, the lights settle and the first song begins. There is no rhythm-game track and no colored notes. The song plays. The player shapes it.

Ellis's verbs in Chapter I (introduced through play, labeled only on the first use):
- **Intensity:** how hard he plays and sings. Too hard in a room this size and people turn away; too soft and nobody hears you over the pool table.
- **Phrasing:** sit back on the beat, sit on it, or push. Different phrasing lands differently on different lines.
- **Attention:** where Ellis looks. At his hands (safe; nobody feels addressed); at the room (riskier; people either meet it or look away); at one person (the heckler, Marlon, the woman at the bar).
- **Reach:** stay on a section longer, improvise a turnaround, take the risky high note. Reach is where the good moments and the bad moments both live.

Mistakes aren't failures. A chord can ring ugly, a lyric can come in late, a string can go sharp in the heat of the lamp. The room reacts. Sometimes a mistake makes the song better.

**The crowd model** is visible behavior, never a meter. In this room: the pool game pauses or doesn't; the Ali–Foreman argument gets quieter or louder; Marlon keeps drying glasses or doesn't; Mrs. Hensley's spoon keeps going or stops.

### Song one — a cover

A period cover everyone knows: **Jim Croce's "I Got a Name"** (1973). *(Licensing target. Alternates: "Operator," "Me and Bobby McGee." The scene works with any song this room would know by heart.)* Ellis starts nervous. Half the room keeps talking. That's fine. When it ends, the farmer says, to nobody, *"Croce. Rest his soul,"* and goes back to diesel.

> **Replay layer.** A song called "I Got a Name," by a singer who died young in a crash the year before, is the first thing Ellis plays in the game. Nobody remarks on it and nobody should.

### Song two — a request

Dwayne from the pool table: *"Play something with a beat, Blake. It's Thursday, not a funeral."*

The player chooses by action, not by menu:
- **Ignore him** and play the next song on the list. Dwayne sulks. Marlon is quietly pleased.
- **Answer him** off-mic: *"You got a quarter, there's a jukebox."* A laugh. Dwayne laughs too, eventually.
- **Take him up on it.** Ellis turns the next song into a shuffle and plays it straight at the pool table until Dwayne misses his shot and throws up his hands. The room likes this best. It costs Ellis nothing but a little nerve.

Whichever way it goes, Ellis loosens. The player can feel it in the controls: phrasing gets more responsive, reach gets cheaper.

### Song three — "No Name"

An original. E minor. He doesn't introduce it.

> *There's a light in the kitchen that stays on all night*
> *for somebody who swore they'd be back by nine.*
> *Daddy says it's the wiring, says it's cheaper to leave it,*
> *so I leave it. I leave it all right.*
>
> *And the freight comes through and it don't stop.*
> *It just slows down enough to make you think it might.*

The room doesn't transform. That would be sentimental. Attention changes slowly and the player has to be paying attention to see it: Mrs. Hensley's spoon stops. Earl lines up a shot and doesn't take it. Marlon puts the glass down. The Ali–Foreman argument by the door just stops, both men looking at the stage as if someone had said their names.

Ellis doesn't notice at first, because he's watching his left hand. Then, on the last chorus, he looks up.

People are listening. It scares him a little more than being ignored.

He finishes. There's a full second of nothing. Then applause, small and real. Ellis looks down at the guitar as if he doesn't know where to put it.

**MRS. HENSLEY** *(to her glass, not to him):* That was pretty, hon.

It's the only thing she says to him in the entire game.

### Getting paid

Marlon counts bills onto the bar. Ellis counts them again.

**ELLIS:** That's twenty-six dollars.

**MARLON:** Good. They taught counting at Hollow Ridge High after all.

**ELLIS:** You said thirty.

**MARLON:** You had two beers and you broke a glass.

**ELLIS:** I didn't break a glass.

**MARLON:** Somebody broke a glass.

**ELLIS:** So charge somebody.

**MARLON** *(looking around the room at the sixteen people):* You want to go find him?

Ellis pockets the twenty-six. Without looking at him, Marlon slides four more dollars across the bar.

**ELLIS:** Thought the glass —

**MARLON:** Get out of my bar.

Ellis smiles. He also takes a hamburger bun off the pass-through, which Marlon sees and lets go.

### The alley

The steel door, the brick. Cool night. Insects that should be gone by October. A freight horn somewhere down the valley. The rail line is thirty feet away behind a chain-link fence with a hole in it.

Ellis sets Loretta's case against the wall and lights a Winston, cupping it backward in his palm the way he does at the garage. After the song, everything is quiet.

**CLARA** *(o.s.):* Second one was better.

He doesn't startle. The player turns the camera.

A young woman is sitting on an upturned Pepsi crate against the brick. Twenty, twenty-one. Dark hair to her shoulders. A faded jean jacket with a small embroidered patch on the breast pocket (the camera doesn't linger; it's a horse, and it's sewn on crooked). A red flannel shirt under it. Boots up on a second crate. Nothing about her is strange. She looks like she's been sitting there a while.

**ELLIS:** Hello to you too.

**CLARA:** First one you were trying too hard.

**ELLIS:** Nobody asked.

**CLARA:** You keep playing in public. That's basically asking.

**ELLIS:** You sound like Marlon.

**CLARA:** Marlon's smarter than you.

**ELLIS:** Everybody's smarter than me tonight.

She smiles and stands. He picks up the case. They walk.

> **Directing note — Clara.** There is no horror language anywhere near Clara. No whispering score, no camera glitch, no chromatic aberration, no strange reflections, no cold light. The game treats her exactly the way Ellis's mind treats her: as a person. Players should like her immediately. Everything depends on that.

### The walk to the car

The Valiant is parked on Depot Street under the one streetlight. It's a 1963 Plymouth, sea-foam green gone chalky, one hubcap missing.

**CLARA:** He come?

Ellis knows exactly who she means.

**ELLIS:** No.

**CLARA:** You thought he might.

**ELLIS:** Didn't say that.

**CLARA:** Didn't have to.

The player will assume she means his father. The player is right.

He unlocks the car. She gets in on the passenger side, from Ellis's point of view.

**CLARA:** You going straight home?

**ELLIS:** Eventually.

### The night drive (driving introduction)

Hollow Ridge at night, and the driving model: the Valiant is a slow, honest car with a slant-six, drum brakes that pull slightly left, and an AM radio that picks up two country stations and, faintly, the Jubilee rerun out of Tannersville. The right rear tire is bald. On wet pavement (there's none tonight) it would matter.

The player can drive straight home up Cold Branch Road, or wander. Main Street is a block and a half: the dark hardware store, the pharmacy with its soda-fountain stools upside down on the counter, the Methodist church on the hill with the cemetery behind it, the depot, a Gulf station closing, dark houses climbing the hills, Hollow Creek beside the road catching the one streetlight.

Clara talks about what they pass.

- At the Gulf station: *"You hungry?" / "Can't afford hungry." / "You got thirty dollars." / "I got thirty dollars for the rest of the week."*
- At the new brick ranch houses going up on the Cold Branch Estates lot: *"You'd live there?" / "I'd rather get shot."*
- On US 19 at the west edge of town, where the sign says LAUREL CITY 38: *"Keep going." / "Where?" / "Does it matter?"* A pause long enough that the player might actually keep going. *"I work tomorrow." / "Tragic."*
- At the Methodist church: nothing. She looks the other way.

If the player drives south toward the South Fork and reaches the turnoff for **Tolliver Road**, a county sawhorse leans across it (ROAD WORK — LOCAL TRAFFIC). Clara says, the way you'd give directions, *"Not that way,"* and Ellis turns the car around. If the player tries again later in the chapter, Ellis stops at the turnoff without being told and says, *"Nah,"* and turns around on his own.

> **Design note — Tolliver Road.** Ellis will not drive this road until Chapter VIII. From Chapter II onward the sawhorse is gone, and the refusal is his: he brakes and turns the car around with a single line ("Nah." / "Not tonight." / "Other way's quicker."). This is the only place on the map Ellis won't go. Players who notice will assume a quirk. On replay it is the most important road on the map.

The player has already learned something about Ellis without being told: part of him wants to disappear, and he keeps not doing it.

### The Blake house

The mood changes before anyone speaks.

The Blake house sits halfway up Cold Branch Road toward Stony Knob: a four-room frame house from the forties with a later addition, a porch that needs work, one bulb over the door. It's maintained just enough, and no more. Wayne's '67 Ford truck sits in the gravel. Through the front window a television is flickering gray.

A fat, elderly dog heaves himself up off the porch and waddles out to meet the car. This is **Tater**: part beagle, part something long, eleven years old and built like a footstool. Ellis gets out, crouches, and gives him the hamburger bun from Marlon's. The player can pet him. Tater leans his whole weight against Ellis's shins.

Clara stays in the car. Tater doesn't look at the car.

**CLARA:** I'll see you.

**ELLIS:** You coming in?

She looks at the house. A small pause.

**CLARA:** Nah.

Completely believable. He goes in alone.

> **Objective shot.** As Ellis crosses the yard, the camera holds on a wide shot of the house and the Valiant under the porch light. The passenger side is in the shadow of the truck. You can't see who's in it. This is the first of dozens of objective shots framed so that Clara's absence can't be noticed.

### Wayne

Wayne Blake is sitting in the living room in the dark. The television is showing the test pattern; the Tannersville station signed off an hour ago with the anthem and he never changed it. There's a glass on the side table. He isn't drunk. He isn't asleep.

He's waiting.

Ellis sees this and immediately knows it's worse.

Wayne doesn't turn his head.

**WAYNE:** What time is it?

**ELLIS:** Not late.

**WAYNE:** Didn't ask if it was late.

Ellis looks at the kitchen clock.

**ELLIS:** Twelve-forty.

**WAYNE:** You're at Roy's at seven.

**ELLIS:** I know where I'm at.

**WAYNE:** Hm.

Ellis heads for the hallway. Wayne's eyes go to the guitar case.

**WAYNE:** Thirty dollars.

**ELLIS:** Thirty dollars.

**WAYNE:** For four hours.

**ELLIS:** Three.

**WAYNE:** Roy pays you better than that.

**ELLIS:** Roy doesn't let me sing.

**WAYNE:** Roy's got sense.

Ellis keeps walking. Wayne looks past him through the front window at the Valiant under the porch bulb.

**WAYNE:** Right rear's bald.

**ELLIS:** I'll deal with it.

**WAYNE:** Yeah.

A beat. Then, quietly, almost to the television:

**WAYNE:** You're real good at dealing with cars after the fact.

Ellis stops.

The player has no context for this. What they have is Ellis's back, gone completely still, and Wayne's face, which shows he knew the second it left his mouth that it was too far. He does not take it back.

Ellis turns around slowly.

**ELLIS:** Fuck you.

Wayne stands up.

**WAYNE:** What'd you say?

**ELLIS:** You heard me.

Wayne takes one step. For a moment the player might expect him to swing. Instead he points down the hall.

**WAYNE:** Go to bed.

**ELLIS:** Gladly.

Ellis goes. A door slams.

The camera stays in the living room. Wayne stands there. Then he goes to the front window and looks out at the Valiant, specifically at the right rear tire, for a long time. Then he sits back down in front of the test pattern.

### Ellis's room

A small back bedroom in the addition. A single bed. The Silvertone's case under the window. A poster of the Allman Brothers' *Eat a Peach* thumbtacked over a water stain. A Snap-On calendar from the garage (a red Corvette, October). A shelf of pocket memo books, numbered in marker on the spines, **1** through **43**; number **44** is in his back pocket. About forty records in milk crates. A plastic horse on the dresser, a child's toy, palomino, one ear chewed.

A framed photograph on the dresser is lying face-down.

The player can pick it up. Ellis turns it over and we see, for a few seconds: an Olan Mills studio portrait from the mid-sixties against a mottled blue backdrop. A younger Wayne, dark-haired, almost smiling, in a suit that doesn't fit. A woman beside him with a beehive and a gap between her front teeth, laughing at something off-camera. A boy of about eight in a clip-on tie. On the woman's lap, a little girl of four or five with her hair in two pigtails, holding a plastic horse.

Ellis turns it face-down again. No explanation.

> **Replay layer.** Everything the player will need is in this photograph: Grace, the horse, and their mother's face at thirty, including the gap in her teeth. Clara has that gap. Nobody will notice on a first playthrough. Some players will notice on a second one and stop the game.

The plastic horse can't be picked up. The prompt simply doesn't appear.

### The notebook (Observe introduction)

On the bed, Ellis takes number 44 out of his back pocket. The player gets their first look at the **notebook**: a Western Auto memo book, spiral at the top, pages in carpenter's pencil. There are a dozen earlier entries in this book: short lines, dated, about the garage, the town, the weather. They read like overheard sentences and descriptions. There are no poems, or nothing that calls itself one.

The Observe system is introduced here with a single prompt. The camera finds the window: the porch light throwing the shadow of the screen onto the yard, Tater asleep in the gravel. Pressing Observe, Ellis looks, then writes:

> *Oct 10. Porch light on the dog. The screen makes a net on him. He doesn't know he's caught.*

A small line in the corner: **Ellis wrote something down.** That's all the tutorial says.

> **Design note — Observe.** Throughout the game, quiet moments offer the Observe prompt: a place, a person, a light, a sound. Ellis stops, looks and writes a line. The notebook is readable at any time. It looks like a collectible journal. It is the hidden-poet system. Lines the player collects will reappear in Chapter IX as "Ellis Blake's poems," leaked and published without consent. The player is helping write the poems the world will mythologize.

He puts the book on the nightstand and lies down with his boots still on. Down the hall, Wayne moves around: a glass set in the sink, the TV clicking off, a door, the toilet, a door. Then silence.

Fade.

### Title

Morning. A truck engine that doesn't want to catch, catching. Dogs somewhere up the road. Someone hammering, a long way off.

## MISSION 2 — WHAT THINGS COST

**Playable:** Ellis · **Where:** the Blake kitchen → Hollow Ridge Auto & Service → the Tanner Valley corridor → Tannersville College · **When:** Friday Oct 11, 6:15 a.m. – 2:30 p.m. · **Length:** ~45 min

### The kitchen

A wind-up Big Ben alarm clock. Ellis hits it. It goes off again. He hits it again.

**WAYNE** *(o.s., from the kitchen):* If you're late, don't come crying to me when Roy runs you off.

**ELLIS** *(into the pillow):* Morning to you too.

No answer.

The kitchen is linoleum, a chrome-legged table, a percolator on the stove, a calendar from the Tannersville Feed & Seed. Wayne is at the table in his work khakis with coffee and the *Banner*. There's almost nothing theatrical about this. They don't acknowledge last night. That is how this house survives.

A pile of bills sits by the salt shaker: Georgia Power, Southern Bell, a feed-store statement, and a small stapled booklet of payment coupons. If the player looks at it, the top coupon reads:

> HOLLOW RIDGE MONUMENT & VAULT CO.
> Coupon 19 of 48 · Amount due $11.50 · Due Oct 15, 1974

Ellis moves the pile to set down his cup. The booklet ends up on top. He turns it face-down. That's all.

Wayne slides a piece of paper across the table without looking up from the paper. It's a receipt pad slip from Carson's Tire & Recap in Wayne's square print: *FIRESTONE 6.50-13 USED GOOD TREAD $8 MOUNTED.*

**WAYNE:** Carson's has one coming that'll fit.

**ELLIS:** I said I'll handle it.

**WAYNE:** You said that about the muffler.

**ELLIS:** And I handled it.

**WAYNE:** With a coat hanger.

**ELLIS:** It's holding.

**WAYNE:** Hm.

That's the whole fight. It's almost friendly, by this house's standards.

Ellis gets his keys from the hook by the door. Next to them, on the counter, is a sandwich wrapped in wax paper: bologna, from the look of it, and an apple. Ellis looks at it, then at Wayne. Wayne is reading about the Tannersville city council.

Ellis takes the sandwich. Neither of them says anything.

**WAYNE** *(to the newspaper)*: It's gonna rain.

There isn't a cloud over Stony Knob.

Outside, Tater's bowl is already full. The player can pour more in anyway. Ellis does, out of habit.

> **Running detail — Tater.** Both men feed the dog every morning and every night, and neither knows the other does it. That is why Tater is shaped like a footstool. The game never explains this. Characters will comment on the dog's weight in later chapters, and the player will eventually work it out.

### Hollow Ridge Auto & Service

East edge of town, where the road starts down into the Tanner Valley. Two bays, two gas pumps under a tin canopy (regular 53.9, ethyl 57.9, printed on flip cards), a parts counter, a Coke machine, a tow truck. A radio on the workbench, AM only unless you stand it in the window.

Roy Cagle (late fifties, big, slow-moving, reading glasses pushed up on a bald head) looks at the Pepsi clock over the office door.

**ROY:** You're four minutes late.

**ELLIS:** That's practically early.

**ROY:** Clock says otherwise.

**ELLIS:** Clock's got an attitude.

Roy hands him a pair of work gloves.

**ROY:** That Clara girl find you? You said she might come by.

**ELLIS:** She found me.

**ROY:** Buick needs an oil change. Mrs. Pardue's Rambler is making a noise she describes as "a goose."

**ELLIS:** A goose.

**ROY:** She was very specific about the goose.

### Garage gameplay

This mission establishes physical work: the lift, draining oil, pulling parts off the shelf, pumping gas (full service, 1974: pump, check the oil, wipe the windshield, make change from a coin changer on Ellis's belt), and diagnosing by sound. It's tactile, quick and human, and never a simulator. The point is that Ellis is good at this. He understands machines. He simply hates that this is supposed to be his whole life.

**Mrs. Pardue's goose** is the diagnostic tutorial. Ellis starts the Rambler, listens, walks around it. The player moves the camera toward the sound (the rear, then underneath) and finds a loose heat shield on the exhaust that honks when the engine revs. Ellis fixes it with a hose clamp.

Mrs. Pardue (sixties, cardigan, taught English at Hollow Ridge High for thirty years) watches from the office doorway.

**MRS. PARDUE:** Are you writing, Ellis?

**ELLIS:** No ma'am.

**MRS. PARDUE:** Hm.

She pays and leaves. Roy looks at Ellis over his glasses and goes back to the Buick.

> **Replay layer.** Mrs. Pardue is the teacher who told eleventh-grade Ellis he wrote better than anybody she'd taught in twenty years. Ellis has never told anyone. He just lied to her face.

### Small human moments

These happen during the loop, in whatever order the player's work brings them:

- **A young mother** with a toddler on her hip and a Ford Falcon that needs a water pump and a fan belt. She can pay for one. Roy, from under the Buick, without coming out: *"Write it up as one hour labor. We didn't do the belt. Did we."* Ellis: *"No sir."* The belt gets done.
- **Wesley Tate**, eleven, from the hardware-store family, buying a Coke from the machine with a sack of pennies. He looks at Ellis a long time. *"You were playing at Marlon's last night." / "Allegedly." / "I heard you from the tracks." / "You're not supposed to be on the tracks." / "You got a band?" / "No." / "Sounded like you should."* Ellis doesn't answer. Wesley leaves with his Coke. The line stays somewhere.
- **Roy's office.** On a shelf above the parts catalogs: a framed photograph of a nineteen-year-old in Army fatigues squinting into the sun, and next to it a folded flag in a triangular wooden case. The player can look. Ellis looks. Roy, from the doorway: *"Hand me that three-eighths ratchet."* Nothing else. (See Chapter VII.)

### Payday (money system introduction)

Friday is payday. Roy pays in cash from a gray lockbox, with a slip.

> 44 hrs @ $2.00 = $88.00 · less taxes · **$76.40**

With last night's thirty, Ellis has **$106.40** and a list of needs the player can see in the notebook's back page:

| Need | Cost |
|---|---|
| The tire | Carson's: on order, in Friday (unavailable) |
| Gas (the Valiant is on a quarter tank) | ~$7.00 to fill |
| Guitar strings (Black Diamond, a set) | $3.50 |
| Cigarettes | $0.50 a pack |
| Food for the week | varies |
| Money to Wayne (he's never asked; this changes in Ch II) | — |
| A notebook (Western Auto, 29¢) | $0.29 |

There is no morality meter. The game simply doesn't let you buy everything at once and have it matter. The tire can't be bought this week at any price, so it stays bald, and more people in Hollow Ridge will mention it. The live choice is strings or gas: if Ellis buys strings, the Valiant runs on fumes; if he fills the tank, his strings sound dead at Marlon's next week, and Cal will hear it. If he eats at the Starlite, Wayne will smell it on him and say something. The first thing the system teaches is Ellis's foundational reality: **every choice costs another choice.**

### The Tannersville delivery

Roy rebuilds alternators for the maintenance shop at Tannersville College, among other people. He points at a crate.

**ROY:** Two alternators, a box of belts, a bag of hardware, and an invoice. College maintenance, behind the science building. Take the truck. Bring the truck back.

**ELLIS:** Was fixing to sell it.

**ROY:** You wouldn't get much.

**ELLIS:** Then we're safe.

Roy throws him the keys anyway.

### The Tanner Valley corridor

This is the player's first real look beyond Hollow Ridge, and it's a road the game will use for fifty hours.

East out of town the road drops off the ridge into the Tanner Valley: switchbacks, a creek that follows the road for a while and then loses interest, a closed apple stand, a Church of God of Prophecy with a marquee (JESUS IS COMING — LOOK BUSY, and under it, smaller, REVIVAL OCT 20–27), pastures, a new brick subdivision with a sign advertising $24,900 homes, a boiled-peanut stand with a hand-painted sign (open; Ellis can stop; he rates them), and then the land flattening and widening toward the river.

The radio clears. On Roy's truck the AM picks up Tannersville stations one after another: country, country, a preacher, the farm report, a Top 40 station playing "You Haven't Done Nothin'," country.

Then Tannersville comes up out of the river haze: the bend of the Tanner River, bridges, the rail yard's long fan of tracks and boxcars, church spires, a few office towers, and the Row (two avenues of old houses on the west side with publishers' signs on the lawns: TREE LINE MUSIC, CEDAR HOLLOW PUBLISHING, DEMOS $40 AN HOUR). Tannersville feels like another planet: students, long hair, veterans in field jackets, black students, foreign students, preppy kids in Weejuns, a hippie with a dog on a rope, a billboard for a new country-music theme park out in the suburbs (THE JUBILEE'S NEW HOME!), a SAVE THE TABERNACLE banner hung on the old brick tabernacle downtown, a WIN button on a meter maid.

Ellis takes all of it in while pretending not to. The Observe prompt is available at the top of the river bridge. If the player takes it:

> *Oct 11. From the bridge the whole city is facing the water, like it's waiting for somebody to come up the river.*

### Campus

Tannersville College: brick quads, a bell tower, oaks, a lot of people who seem to know where they're going. Ellis backs Roy's truck up behind the science building. A maintenance man signs his invoice with a pencil from behind his ear.

A student with an armful of books stops by the truck.

**STUDENT:** Do you know where Garrison Hall is?

Ellis looks down at his coveralls, which say ROY'S over the pocket.

**ELLIS:** I look like admissions?

**STUDENT** *(laughing):* Worth a shot.

**ELLIS:** It's the building with all the people who know where they're going.

**STUDENT:** That narrows it down.

She points him out of existence and goes. Ellis smiles despite himself.

### First Riley sighting

Ellis carries the empty crate back across a walkway to the truck.

Ahead, under an oak: a young woman sitting barefoot in the grass with two other students, books spread around them, an acoustic guitar case beside her. She's in the middle of an argument and winning it.

Ellis walks straight across the foreground. He never looks at her.

The camera follows Ellis. Then it catches: the faintest frame-stutter, like film jumping in a projector gate. The ambient noise of the quad narrows to the conversation under the tree. The depth of field slides off Ellis's back and settles on her. Ellis keeps walking. The camera doesn't.

**FRIEND:** You're impossible.

**RILEY:** That's not an argument.

The HUD fades. Control transfers.

> **Design note — the switch.** This is the player's introduction to the switch. Don't label it. Don't show a name. The game has simply decided: this is the person you need to understand now.

## MISSION 3 — THE LONG WAY AROUND

**Playable:** Riley · **Where:** Tannersville College · **When:** Friday Oct 11, 2:30 – 7:00 p.m. · **Length:** ~35 min

Riley moves differently. Ellis inhabits space like he's always on the way somewhere he'd rather not be going. Riley wanders. She stops, changes direction, touches the bark of the tree, talks to whoever's there. She's comfortable here, and her walk says so.

### The argument under the oak

Two friends: Pam (earnest, psychology) and Russell (bored, pre-law). The argument is about B. F. Skinner, because it's 1974.

**PAM:** He's saying we're basically what the environment makes us. Reinforcement. You get rewarded for something, you do it more.

**RILEY:** Then nobody's responsible for anything.

**PAM:** That's not what he says.

**RILEY:** It's where it goes.

**RUSSELL:** It's where everything goes with you.

**RILEY:** Skinner says a lot of things for a man who raised his daughter in a box.

**PAM:** That's a myth. It was a crib. It had a window.

**RILEY:** A crib with a window is a box.

**RUSSELL:** You argue with people you agree with more than anybody I've ever met.

**RILEY:** Those are the useful arguments.

This matters for her. Riley is warm, and she's intellectually serious. She isn't written as a stoner flower child and never will be.

### Campus

The player crosses campus freely. Riley knows everybody. Greetings follow her around: *"Riley!"* *"You coming to Hannah's thing?"* *"Landry wants your paper."* *"Did you finish the crossword?"* This life existed before Ellis, and it will go on trying to claim her after him.

Optional interactions, each a small scene:
- **WTCR**, the student station, in the basement of the student union: a picture window onto a tiny booth where a serious young man with a beard (Martin Keller, station manager) is cueing a record with the concentration of a surgeon. Riley knocks on the glass. He waves without looking up.
- **The record shop** on the edge of campus: Joni Mitchell's *Court and Spark* in the window. Riley already has it. She flips through the import bin anyway.
- **The women's caucus table**, a card table with a hand-lettered sign: THE ERA IS COMING TO THE GEORGIA SENATE. WRITE YOUR SENATOR. Riley signs up to phone-bank, again.
- **A poetry reading poster** (Thursday, Garrison Hall), a **theater rehearsal** through an open door, a **frisbee game** that the player can join (Riley is bad at frisbee and good at trash talk), a **veteran** in a field jacket smoking alone on the library steps who nods at her and nobody else.
- **Professor Landry** eating a sandwich on a bench, reading. *"Ms. Riley." / "Professor." / "Your paper." / "Monday." / "It was due Wednesday." / "It's better on Monday."* He almost smiles and turns the page.

### The seminar

A small room with a long table: "Mind and Self," Professor Alan Landry, twelve students, a radiator that knocks.

**LANDRY:** Locke. The prince and the cobbler. The prince's soul, with every memory the prince has, wakes up one morning in the cobbler's body. Who is he?

**STUDENT:** The prince.

**STUDENT 2:** The cobbler. Look at him.

**LANDRY:** Ms. Riley?

The player gets three tonal options (earnest, joking, contrarian), and all three get to the same canonical place.

**RILEY:** A cobbler with a very bad week ahead of him.

Laughter.

**LANDRY:** And if, after a year, he forgets he was ever the prince?

**RILEY:** Then he's a cobbler. And the prince is dead.

**LANDRY:** Is he?

Riley opens her mouth and closes it.

**RILEY:** I don't know.

**LANDRY:** Good. Read Parfit for Tuesday.

It's ordinary college material. It should feel ordinary. Much later, players will quote it.

### Riley's room

A dorm double in a brick women's residence hall: plants on every flat surface (a spider plant, Gerald, has pride of place), stacked books, records in an orange crate, a Joni Mitchell poster, a batik tapestry, a half-finished essay in the typewriter, a stick of incense going on the windowsill because the RA can smell weed from forty yards.

Hannah Greer (Riley's roommate: education major, from Macon, practical, dry) is reading on her bed. Riley sits on the floor with her class notes and rolls a joint while she reads them. It's routine, with no rebellious fanfare.

**HANNAH:** You realize you're doing homework while smoking weed.

**RILEY:** Efficiency.

**HANNAH:** That's not what efficiency means.

**RILEY:** Sounds like something a sober person would say.

### Riley's guitar (her mechanic)

Riley puts on a record (the player can choose from her crate: Joni, Laura Nyro, Nick Drake, a Jobim, the Band) and picks up her guitar, a 1968 Martin D-18 with her mother's initials scratched inside the soundhole.

She plays differently from Ellis. Ellis finds songs emotionally, by feel. Riley understands why chords work together. Her mechanic is **voicing**: the same chord built different ways, each with its own color, and **leading**: moving each note of one chord to the nearest note of the next. The player experiments with a progression she's working on; some voicings sound flat, some open up, one is wrong in a way that's better than right. When the player finds it, Riley stops and writes it down carefully in a notebook with a list of words she likes on the inside cover.

She's already a songwriter. She isn't "the girl who joins Ellis's band." She has songs of her own, and one of them (it has no title yet) is about her mother.

### The hall phone

A girl pounds on the door: *"Riley! Phone! It's your mother, she says it's not an emergency, she says it twice."*

The hall phone is a black wall set by the stairs with a pencil on a string. Riley's mother, Joan, is warm, funny and a little over-involved.

**JOAN:** Maggie.

**RILEY:** Mama.

**JOAN:** Your father mailed the application.

**RILEY:** I didn't ask him to.

**JOAN:** You asked him where the stamps were.

**RILEY:** That isn't the same thing.

**JOAN:** To your father it is.

They laugh. Joan asks about classes, meals, sleep, whether she's wearing a coat, whether she's been to church, which she knows the answer to. The application is for a summer program in philosophy that Landry recommended her for, in Scotland.

**JOAN:** You still thinking about Edinburgh?

**RILEY:** Maybe.

**JOAN:** You're very lucky, Margaret Anne.

**RILEY:** I know.

She does know. That's why she sometimes feels guilty for wanting something else, even before she knows what it is.

> **Name note.** The player hears "Maggie" and "Margaret" here. Nobody explains. Riley goes by her last name; everyone she's chosen calls her Riley.

### Weekend plans

Back in the room, Hannah mentions somebody on the third floor is getting mushrooms for the weekend. Riley is interested, not breathlessly.

**HANNAH:** You ever done them?

**RILEY:** Once.

**HANNAH:** And?

**RILEY:** Spent forty minutes convinced a tree understood me.

Hannah laughs.

**RILEY:** It did.

That gets a bigger laugh. There's no sinister music and no danger marker. It's just part of Riley's world.

### Dean's first appearance

Riley and Hannah walk out toward the library at dusk. A car comes around the campus loop too fast: a black 1971 Chevelle SS with the windows down and Bad Company blasting. A guy is hanging halfway out of the passenger window yelling at a girl on the sidewalk, who yells back, delighted.

The driver brakes late at the crosswalk. A campus security officer in a brown uniform jumps back and shouts. The driver waves an apology over the roof without slowing down.

**RILEY:** Idiot.

**HANNAH:** Dean Holloway.

**RILEY:** I know who Dean Holloway is.

**HANNAH:** Everybody does.

The Chevelle parks across two spaces in a faculty lot. Dean gets out and pulls half a drum kit out of the trunk: a floor tom, a bag of stands.

**SOMEONE ACROSS THE QUAD:** Dean!

**DEAN:** What?

**SOMEONE:** Party tonight?

**DEAN:** There'd better be.

Riley shakes her head and keeps walking. Dean crosses behind her with his arms full of cymbal stands, and the camera catches: stutter, focus shift. Riley goes up the library steps. The camera stays.

Dean drops a cymbal stand. It clangs on the brick.

**DEAN:** *Goddammit.*

Control transfers.

## MISSION 4 — GOOD FAMILY

**Playable:** Dean · **Where:** Tannersville College → Belle Grove → a farmhouse in the Tanner Valley → Vale Music · **When:** Friday Oct 11, 6:30 p.m. – 12:10 a.m. · **Length:** ~45 min

Dean's first thirty seconds tell you everything: he's completely cool until the exact moment he isn't.

He swears at the cymbal stand. He picks it up. The wing nut falls off and rolls under a parked Oldsmobile. He lies down on the brick in his satin jacket to get it.

A girl passing by: *"Need help?"*

**DEAN** *(from under the car):* Emotionally or physically?

She keeps walking.

**DEAN:** Playing hard to get. Respect.

Dean moves fast and loose. His walk has a bounce the controls exaggerate slightly; he's quicker to sprint, slower to stop. He drums on things he passes: a railing, a trash can, a parked car's fender. The player can do this deliberately (a button taps whatever surface he's next to), and it's the first hint of his mechanic: Dean hears everything as something to play.

### Belle Grove

Dean drives home. The shift from Ellis's life should be almost shocking, and the player gets to drive it: across the river, up the long boulevard into Belle Grove, where the streets curve for no reason, the lawns are the size of Hollow Ridge's football field and the mailboxes have names on them in brass. The Holloway house is a white Colonial with black shutters and a pool, covered for the season, out back. Richard Holloway's Lincoln is in the drive. The kitchen lights are warm.

There is no hidden horror here. Nobody hits anybody. There is no terrible secret. Dean comes from a loving family. That's what makes his rebellion interesting.

**Patty** (fifteen, braces, a sarcasm she's clearly learned at her brother's knee) is at the kitchen table doing algebra homework and losing. Dean looks over her shoulder on his way to the refrigerator.

**PATTY:** Don't.

**DEAN:** x is seven.

**PATTY:** How did you—

**DEAN:** Guessed.

He takes a Coke and leaves her staring at the page. The player can check: x is seven.

> **Seed — Dean's secret.** Dean can do arithmetic in his head faster than anyone in the game. He's spent eight years pretending he learned nothing at his father's elbow. This is the first of a handful of moments that show it and don't explain it (see Chapter VII, "Ledger").

### Dinner

A dining room with a chandelier. Mrs. Bea, the housekeeper, brings pot roast and goes home. Richard Holloway (fifty, tortoiseshell glasses, a CPA's patience and a CPA's anxiety), Carol Holloway (forty-seven, warm, wry, sees everything and chooses her battles), Patty, Dean.

**RICHARD:** Caldwell said the internship's still open.

**DEAN:** He's persistent.

**RICHARD:** He's trying to help you.

**DEAN:** I know.

**RICHARD:** Then why do you say it like that?

Dean eats.

**RICHARD:** Accounting isn't exciting when you're twenty.

**DEAN:** Glad you warned me.

Carol suppresses a smile. Patty doesn't bother.

**RICHARD:** You think everything's a joke.

**DEAN:** Not everything. Funerals are pretty serious.

Richard sets down his fork. Carol looks at Dean over the rim of her glass.

Dean knows.

**DEAN:** Sorry.

He means it. And then Richard says something perfectly reasonable.

**RICHARD:** At some point you've got to decide what you're doing with your life, son.

Dean's face changes, a small tightening at the jaw. To him that sentence sounds like: *pick your cage.*

### Downstairs

Dean's real life is in the finished basement: a drum kit (a Ludwig in black oyster pearl, the ride cymbal cracked), a stereo, records in a stack that has fallen over, concert posters, Polaroids stuck to the paneling, phone numbers written on the paneling itself, a bottle of Jim Beam behind the water heater and, in a Sucrets tin in a desk drawer, a small amount of cocaine.

This is 1974, not the cliché of the 1980s. For Dean cocaine is glamorous, adult, social and useful. He uses some before going out. The game offers no stat bonus and no glowing screen edges. What changes is the sound: music in the house gets sharper, more separate. Dean gets quicker. His dialogue speeds up. The world feels closer and more his. The player should understand why he likes it. If the game is going to be honest about what happens later, it has to be honest about this first.

If the player looks closely at the basement's walls, there's a line of pencil on a strip of exposed insulation behind the stereo: *R.H. 6/67 — 2 layers.* Richard soundproofed this room himself the summer he bought his son a drum kit. He has never mentioned it. Dean has never noticed.

### His mother at the door

Dean goes up the basement stairs and out through the laundry room to leave quietly. The back door opens before he reaches it. Carol is standing there holding his jacket.

**CAROL:** Use the front door like a normal person.

**DEAN:** Where's the adventure in that?

She hands him the jacket.

**CAROL:** Your father thinks you're studying.

**DEAN:** I'm studying people.

**CAROL:** Terrifying.

He kisses her on the cheek.

**DEAN:** Love you.

**CAROL:** Drive carefully.

**DEAN:** Always.

He goes out the back door anyway. At the end of the drive, the Chevelle's tires chirp.

### The party

A rented farmhouse out on the Tanner Valley corridor where four seniors live: cars parked on the lawn and in the ditch, a bonfire in a burn barrel, a keg in a washtub, a record collection being abused, forty people, then sixty. Somebody has strung Christmas lights on the porch in October. Somebody's girlfriend is crying in a Volkswagen. Somebody is explaining the Carter candidacy to a cow over the fence (Carter hasn't announced yet; the guy is very ahead of his time).

Dean comes alive. This section needs to be genuinely fun. Optional activities, each small and quick:
- **Dance** (Dean is a good, shameless dancer).
- **Flirt** (Dean is charming and never predatory; he takes no for an answer instantly and cheerfully).
- **Play quarters** at the kitchen table.
- **Take a stupid bet**: that he can't jump the burn barrel, that he can't finish a beer standing on his head, that he can't get to the Gulf station on Route 60 and back in eleven minutes.
- **Help a drunk kid** throwing up off the porch get water and sit down. Dean is good at this; he's done it a lot.
- **Escalate or defuse an argument** between two guys over a girl who isn't interested in either of them. Dean can make it funny.
- **The drums.**

Dean genuinely likes people, and he's the guy everyone wants at the party because something happens when he's around.

### The drums

A party band in the living room: a guitarist who's decent, a bassist who isn't, and a drummer who's too drunk to keep going. The drummer gets up, weaving. Dean sits down.

What follows should be the first moment players realize: *oh, he's actually good.*

Dean's mechanic, introduced: **feel** (push or hold the tempo), **fills** (when, and how big), **dynamics** (drop out, come back like a door kicked in) and **read the room**. As Dean, the player can see crowd energy the way no other character can: a faint shimmer of movement at the edges of the screen where people are starting to dance, a stillness where they're drifting. Nobody else in the game gets this view.

He's smiling. He throws fills that shouldn't work and do. He answers the guitarist's phrases like it's a conversation. He plays too hard, beautifully. Everything reckless about Dean becomes useful behind a drum kit: the chaos turns into rhythm. People crowd in. Someone is standing on the couch.

### The raid

A neighbor finally calls the county. Blue lights come up the gravel drive. Panic, mostly cheerful.

**OWNER** *(from the porch):* Everybody out the back! Out the back!

**DEAN:** My drums!

**FRIEND:** Leave them!

Dean looks at him like he suggested leaving a baby on the side of the road.

**DEAN:** Go to hell.

Gameplay becomes frantic comedy: dragging a floor tom and a bag of stands out the back door, across a pasture, through a gap in a barbed-wire fence (the stand snags; the player has to free it), cymbals held against Dean's chest like a shield. Someone runs past with a case of beer. A deputy's flashlight sweeps the yard. Dean cuts around the barn to where the Chevelle is parked in the tree line, throws the drums in the trunk on top of something that's already there (he doesn't look), and dives behind the wheel.

The passenger door opens. A big man he's never seen before (mid-twenties, a field jacket, a face that looks older than the rest of him) drops into the seat and closes the door.

**DEAN:** Who are you?

**STRANGER:** Drive.

A beat.

**DEAN:** Good enough.

### The escape

A short chase, not GTA carnage: Dean is avoiding a ticket, not a manhunt. A gravel road, dust in the headlights, a deputy's cruiser a quarter mile back. The player can kill the headlights (a button; the road goes almost black; the moon helps a little) and turn into an old quarry service road. The cruiser passes the turnoff. Its lights move away through the trees.

Dean and the stranger sit completely silent in the dark with the engine ticking.

Then Dean starts laughing. The stranger starts laughing: a short, rusty sound, like he doesn't do it much.

**STRANGER:** My truck's still at that house.

**DEAN:** That's a tomorrow problem.

**DEAN:** I'm Dean.

**STRANGER:** Tully.

**DEAN:** Tully what?

**TULLY:** Just drive, man.

Dean drops him at a crossroads store on Route 60. Tully gets out, taps the roof twice, says "Obliged," and walks off into the dark without looking back.

**DEAN** *(to nobody):* Weird guy.

> **Replay layer.** "Who are you?" is first spoken here, as a joke, by Dean, to a stranger in a getaway car. The answer is "Drive." Tully Voss is a Tanner Valley boy home from Vietnam three years and drifting. He'll be back.

### The amplifier

At the Route 60 Gulf station, Dean opens the trunk to arrange his drums properly and finds what he threw them on top of: a small PA head, the party band's amplifier, the faceplate cracked and one knob gone. Someone must have thrown it in during the stampede. It smells like hot dust. It has a rental sticker: VALE MUSIC — TANNERSVILLE.

He calls the farmhouse from the pay phone. The owner (panicked, whispering) says it's a rental from Vale's, the deposit's forty dollars, he doesn't have forty dollars, Vale's gonna kill him, and "Mercer" at Vale's can fix anything and is always there late.

**DEAN:** Who's Mercer?

**OWNER** *(on the phone):* Cal Mercer. At Vale's. He's always there.

**DEAN:** At midnight?

**OWNER:** He's probably still there.

**DEAN:** Already hate him.

### Vale Music

Vale Music is at the edge of the Row, a two-story brick storefront with guitars in the window and the owner's apartment upstairs. The front is dark. Around back, a workshop light is on behind a steel door.

Dean bangs on it. Nothing. He bangs again.

**VOICE** *(through the door):* We're closed.

**DEAN:** Fantastic. I'm not buying anything.

The door opens. **Cal Mercer**: twenty-one, tall, short hair, sleeves rolled to the elbow, a pen in his shirt pocket, a soldering iron in his hand, an expression like a closed door that happens to be open.

Dean holds up the amplifier.

Cal looks at it.

**CAL:** No.

**DEAN:** You don't even know what I want.

**CAL:** You want me to fix whatever you did to that.

**DEAN:** Maybe.

**CAL:** No.

**DEAN:** Money.

Cal opens the door wider.

### Switch

Mr. Harlan Vale (sixty-four, cardigan over pajamas, reading glasses, woken up) comes down the back stairs, sees Dean and sighs like a man who's seen a hundred Deans. He takes Dean into the office to argue about the rental deposit. Cal takes the amplifier to his bench.

The camera follows the amplifier, not Dean. It catches on Cal's hands setting it down under the bench lamp. Stutter. Focus locks on his fingers loosening the chassis screws.

Control transfers.

## MISSION 5 — EVERYTHING HAS A REASON

**Playable:** Cal · **Where:** Vale Music → Mercer Radio & TV → Gaslight Alley → Vale Music · **When:** Saturday Oct 12, 12:10 a.m. → Monday Oct 14 · **Length:** ~35 min

### The bench

Cal's gameplay is observation and precision. He doesn't guess. He isolates.

The repair is a short, clean diagnostic puzzle, the mechanical version of Cal's worldview: the fuse (intact); power at the transformer (yes); the tubes (one 6L6 cracked at the base, from the fall); a cold solder joint on the output jack (from long before tonight; this amp has been dying for a year). The player probes with a meter, reads values, swaps the tube, reflows the joint. There's a satisfying hum when it comes back to life, then a cleaner silence.

The workshop is Cal's space: pegboard with outlined tools, labeled drawers, a coffee can of resistors sorted by value, a paperback science-fiction novel face-down on a stool (Le Guin, *The Dispossessed*), a cassette recorder, a Timex on the bench because he takes it off to solder.

### Mr. Vale

Mr. Vale comes back from the office with the deposit settled.

**VALE:** Go home, Calvin.

**CAL:** Holloway wants it tonight.

**VALE:** Then Holloway can discover patience.

**CAL:** He doesn't strike me as a man who's interested.

**VALE:** You met him ten minutes ago.

**CAL:** I've got instincts.

Vale laughs and goes back upstairs.

Dean comes out and waits. He drums on a glass display case of harmonicas.

**CAL:** Stop.

Dean stops. Five seconds. Tap. Tap. Cal looks at him. Dean sits on his hands.

The amp is done in forty minutes. Dean pays with a twenty and says keep the change. Cal counts out his change exactly and holds it out.

**DEAN:** I said keep it.

**CAL:** I heard you.

Dean stares at him, then takes the change, delighted in spite of himself.

**DEAN:** You're a strange man, Mercer.

**CAL:** Good night.

Cal doesn't absorb other people's failures. It turns out he doesn't absorb their generosity either. He'll learn the difference late.

### Cal

What the player should gather over this mission, without being told: Cal is twenty-one. He's not rich and he's not poor in Ellis's way; he's working. Disciplined. Dry. Musically experienced: he has spent five years playing weddings, lounges, school dances, publishers' demo sessions on the Row at union scale, and backing singers who aren't as good as they think they are. He's the most technically experienced musician in the future Blakes, and the least romantic about being one. Or so he'd tell you.

### Mercer Radio & TV

Saturday morning. East Tannersville, by the rail yard: rows of shotgun houses, a laundromat, a Baptist church with a sign about tithing, and a narrow storefront with a hand-painted window, MERCER RADIO & TV — TUBES TESTED FREE. Inside: shelves of televisions with their backs off, a smell of solder and dust burning off tubes, a radio on the bench tuned to whatever station the customer left it on, as a matter of principle.

Walt Mercer (fifty-four, reading glasses, a Signal Corps tattoo gone blue on one forearm) is working on a console TV. Cal comes in with two coffees. They talk while facing the chassis. Neither looks at the other much.

**WALT:** Gig tonight?

**CAL:** Supposed to.

**WALT:** Pays?

**CAL:** Thirty-five.

**WALT:** Then it's work.

That says almost everything about how this family shows love: through competence. Walt glances at the bass case by the door.

**WALT:** Still buzzing?

**CAL:** Only unplugged.

**WALT:** Then it's still buzzing.

**CAL:** Nobody hears it.

**WALT:** You do.

A tiny seed of Cal's whole psychology: a problem stays a problem even if nobody else notices it.

Dot Mercer comes through from the back with a sack of sandwiches, talking before she's through the door: about June's boys, about the woman at the phone company who got her hair done wrong, about Cal's shirt, about whether Cal's eating, about the Baptist preacher's new car. Walt and Cal both nod at the chassis. Cal takes a sandwich. Dot kisses the top of his head on her way out, which he permits.

### Gaslight Alley

Saturday night. Downtown Tannersville, an alley of lounges and supper clubs with neon script signs, where the tourists who used to come for the Jubilee show still wander in looking for something. The Blue Moon Supper Club. Cal is sitting in with **Jimmy Ray Tolbert & the Nightcaps**: the singer is drunk, the guitarist is late, the drummer has not learned the songs.

The player plays bass. Cal's mechanic:
- **Lock:** sit with the drummer, or sit slightly behind him and drag him back.
- **Follow:** catch somebody else's deviation (an early entrance, a missed change) and turn it into something that sounds planned.
- **Floor:** hold one note under everything when everyone else is lost.

When the drummer drifts, Cal anchors him. When Jimmy Ray comes in a bar early, Cal moves the whole band under him so it sounds like an arrangement. When the guitarist forgets a turnaround, Cal leaves space so the silence sounds intentional. The group sounds better because Cal is there. The audience barely knows he exists. That is Cal's musical role, and eventually his emotional role in The Blakes.

At the bar, a man in his late twenties in a good shirt watches Cal through the whole second set. When Cal glances up once, the man nods. Cal nods back. Nothing else happens. Nobody else notices.

### Pay

After, in the kitchen hallway, Jimmy Ray hands Cal a twenty.

**CAL:** Thirty-five.

**JIMMY RAY:** Bad crowd.

**CAL:** I wasn't paid by the head.

**JIMMY RAY:** Twenty's what I've got.

**CAL:** Then find fifteen.

No shouting. No threat. Cal simply stands there. Jimmy Ray finds fifteen in the tip jar. That principle (I will not absorb your failure) is going to make Ellis furious one day.

### Monday

Vale Music, Monday afternoon. Mr. Vale hangs up the phone.

## MISSION 8 — ONE MORE SONG

### The late drive

He can't sleep. He takes his keys. Clara goes with him. The Valiant's starter wakes Wayne (the camera stays with Wayne for three seconds, in bed, eyes open, listening), and he doesn't get up.

Ellis drives up Cold Branch Road to the top of **Stony Knob**, the overlook: a gravel pull-off, a guardrail, the fire tower black against stars, and Hollow Ridge below. A dozen streetlights, the depot, the steeple, the dark line of the rail cut, the ridges going blue into more ridges, and, far off east, the glow of Tannersville on the clouds.

Clara sits on the hood beside him. The engine ticks.

**CLARA:** You ever think about leaving and never coming back?

**ELLIS:** All the time.

**CLARA:** Why don't you?

**ELLIS:** Money.

**CLARA:** That's it?

**ELLIS:** No.

She waits. He never explains.

**CLARA:** You gonna play Friday?

**ELLIS:** Might could.

**CLARA:** Might could.

**ELLIS:** Yeah.

**CLARA:** You sounded happy tonight.

He looks at her. That almost bothers him.

**ELLIS:** Did I?

**CLARA:** For a little while.

He looks back at the town.

> **Observe** (available here): *Oct 19. From up here the town is twelve lights and a church. You could cover it with your hand. I've tried.*

### The deputy

Headlights come up the road. Ellis turns his face away from them, not dramatically, a reflex, a slight squint and a turn of the shoulder, the way anybody might.

A county sheriff's cruiser pulls into the overlook and stops beside the Valiant. The window rolls down. **Deputy Lyle Pruitt**: forties, a toothpick, a face that has known this boy since he was in diapers.

**PRUITT:** Little late for the view, Blake.

**ELLIS:** Sir.

**PRUITT:** Everything all right?

**ELLIS:** Yes sir.

Pruitt looks at the car. The blocking is completely natural: from where he sits, he's looking past Ellis at the hood and the town, and he never acknowledges anyone else. He doesn't need to. Nobody would think anything of it.

**PRUITT:** Wayne know you're out?

**ELLIS:** I'm eighteen.

**PRUITT:** That wasn't what I asked.

**ELLIS:** Then no.

**PRUITT:** Go home before he calls me looking for you.

**ELLIS:** He won't.

That answer tells Pruitt more than Ellis meant it to. Pruitt looks at him a second longer. Then at the right rear tire.

**PRUITT:** That tire's bald, son.

**ELLIS:** So I hear.

Pruitt rolls the window up and pulls out. His taillights go down the mountain.

**CLARA:** Friendly town.

**ELLIS:** Everybody knows everything.

Clara looks at him.

**CLARA:** Not everything.

She watches the cruiser's taillights to the bottom of the mountain.

**CLARA:** He's gonna write you up for that tire.

> **Replay layer.** Pruitt worked Grace's crash. He's the one who got Ellis out of the car. The whole town watches this boy's tires. The flinch at the headlights is the first one; there will be more, and nobody will ever mention them.

### Before dawn

Ellis comes home near six. It's still black; the ridge won't gray for an hour and a half (Georgia is on year-round daylight time this year). Tater is asleep on the porch and thumps his tail without getting up.

Inside, Wayne has fallen asleep at the kitchen table, head on his forearm, the overhead light still on. Under his other hand is a snapshot: a girl of about twelve sitting bareback on an old chestnut mare in a pasture, squinting and grinning at whoever's holding the camera. Wayne's hand covers most of her face. The player can see the horse, and her knees, and a green felt-tip date on the white border: *June 1971*.

Ellis stands there and watches his father sleep. He looks at the green date on the border for a long time.

He takes the afghan off the back of the couch. For a moment it seems he'll put it over Wayne's shoulders.

He doesn't. He folds it and sets it on the table beside Wayne's arm, where he'll see it when he wakes up.

### The notebook

In his room, at the desk (a card table), Ellis opens number 44 to a clean page and writes, in carpenter's pencil:

> FRIDAY — MARLON'S
> Riley — guitar / vocals
> Cal — bass

He pauses, then:

> Dean — drums

Then:

> Ellis — guitar / vocals

He looks at the four lines. For a moment he smiles.

Below them, he writes:

> THE BLAKES

He stares at it. He crosses it out, hard, three lines through it. Closes the book.

A beat.

He opens it again and looks at the crossed-out words. He doesn't tear the page out.

He never tears a page out.

### Final audio

Black.

Dean's voice from the first set:

**DEAN:** One—

**CAL:** Wait.

Silence.

**CAL:** Now.

**DEAN:** One, two, three, four—

All four instruments hit together. Cut.

## Chapter I — design summary

### What the player believes after Chapter I
- Ellis is a talented, broke kid with a harsh father and some family tragedy involving a car and a little girl.
- Clara is someone important to him: an older friend, a cousin, a former neighbor, a girl he used to date. She's funny and kind and she doesn't like his father.
- Riley is a college girl slumming, maybe serious about music, maybe not.
- Dean is an entertaining disaster who thinks they're destined for greatness.
- Cal is the irritatingly competent bass player who'll fight with Ellis forever.
- The Blakes formed by accident.

### What actually happened
- Wayne has told his surviving child, in two sentences, that Grace died and he didn't.
- The closed room, the horse sticker, the plastic horse, the girl on the chestnut mare and the little girl in the portrait are all Grace.
- The coupon book is for her headstone.
- Clara never interacts with Wayne, never enters the house while Wayne is awake, never touches an object anyone else could verify, never appears in the objective wide shots and is never looked at by the dog or the deputy.
- When Ellis talks about leaving, Clara always understands why before he explains.
- "Hush. Eat one of his biscuits." is Ellis looking after himself in the only voice he'd take orders from, and it's Grace's old habit with Wayne.

### Wayne in Chapter I
Two blows ("cars after the fact," "you got to keep yours"), one wordless kindness (the wax-paper lunch), one sign of fear ("Don't," at the door), one sign of shame (and, if the player holds silence, a half-said name that lands on nothing), and one secret: he looks at the bald tire through the window for a long time.
