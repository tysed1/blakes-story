# CHAPTER VIII — FEEDBACK

> **New York · the winter Midwest · Hollow Ridge · Laurel City · Tannersville · Monday, January 12 – Friday, April 30, 1976**
> Main-story runtime: ~7 hours · Playable: Cal, Riley, Dean, Ellis, in a rotation that breaks down as the chapter does

## Chapter thesis

Feedback is a signal re-entering itself until it screams. The Blakes built their sound on it: a guitar held close to an amplifier, the note coming back around, and a band that knows exactly how long to let it grow before it takes over the room. In this chapter the same loop runs through the record business, the press, the drugs and Ellis's mind, and nobody can find the volume knob.

The last two clauses of the van promise break. The player learns what Clara is made of. Ellis takes a pill that makes her quiet, and her help goes quiet with her.

## World state at open

- "Still Here" is at #38 on the national chart and falling slowly. Monarch Records is about to release "Sunday Clothes" as the second single.
- The Bowery photograph (the player's version) runs on *Rave*'s February cover. By March it's a poster in college bookstores.
- Carter is campaigning in Iowa. "Convoy" is on every truck-stop jukebox, and the CB radio is on in every cab. *One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest* is in theaters.
- The band: a manager, a label that has sold its distribution to a bigger one, twenty-one arena dates booked on a Tuesday by one of them, and a promise made in a van in May that already has two clauses broken.

## COLD OPEN — THE POSTER

A cinderblock dorm room at a state university in Ohio, January. Radiator clanking. A poster of Farrah Fawcett on one side, bare wall on the other.

The camera belongs to nobody. The player has only the view.

A girl of eighteen in a Kent State sweatshirt stands on her bed with a roll of masking tape, pressing the corners of a poster to the cinderblock. It's the Bowery photograph, the one the player made in Chapter VII: four young people against a brick wall in the snow, Dean smiling, Ellis doing whatever the player had him do.

Her roommate, at the desk with a chemistry book, looks up.

**ROOMMATE:** Who's that?

**GIRL:** The Blakes.

**ROOMMATE:** Which one's Ellis?

**GIRL:** The one who isn't smiling.

(Dean is smiling in every version, so this works in every version.)

**ROOMMATE:** Is he the one whose sister died?

**GIRL:** Yeah.

**ROOMMATE:** That's so sad.

**GIRL:** I know.

She smooths the last corner down. On the girl's desk, a clock radio is playing "Still Here," low, on a Cleveland station.

The camera moves in on the poster until Ellis's face fills the frame, printed in halftone, a little blurred, eighteen inches tall.

Cut to: the same photograph, framed, eight feet wide, on the lobby wall of Monarch Records in New York. Cal Mercer, in a pressed shirt and a borrowed tie, is looking at it.

**CHAPTER VIII**
**FEEDBACK**

> **Design note.** The myth has left the South. The player's own photograph is on a stranger's wall, next to the one fact about Ellis the whole country knows.

## MISSION 1 — KEY MAN

**Playable:** Cal · **Where:** Monarch Records, 1650 Broadway, New York · **When:** Monday January 12, 10:00 a.m. – 1:00 p.m. · **Length:** ~25 min

### The conference room

Over Christmas, Southern Star's owner sold the national distribution to a New York major. Monarch now distributes *Borrowed Stone* and will pay for the second album, the tour and the radio. Monarch has a rider.

Twentieth floor. Coffee in a silver service, pastries nobody touches but Dean. The band and Eddie Farris on one side; Gil Tarver and two Monarch lawyers on the other. Harold Vance at the end of the table, looking like a man at a wedding he didn't approve of.

Cal has the rider in front of him, eleven pages, and his pen marks what he marks:

- $40,000 tour support against royalties, for a bus, a crew, lights.
- A $25,000 advance and a $40,000 recording fund for the second album.
- Options on three more albums, at Monarch's election.
- **Clause 14. Key member.**

Cal reads clause 14 twice, flat, the way he'd read a wiring diagram:

> *14(a). Should Ellis Blake cease to perform as a member of the Group for any reason, Monarch may, at its sole election, terminate this Agreement with respect to the remaining members of the Group, and shall retain an irrevocable option on the exclusive recording services of Ellis Blake as an individual artist on the terms set forth herein.*

Across the table, Gil is smiling. He isn't hiding anything. It's all right there on page nine.

### Richard

Cal calls Tannersville collect from an empty office.

**RICHARD HOLLOWAY** *(on the phone)*: Read it to me.

Cal reads it. The player can hear Richard's pencil.

**CAL:** Can we strike it?

**RICHARD:** You can ask. They'll say no. If Ellis goes, they keep Ellis and they can drop you. If you go, nothing happens at all. They're not paying forty thousand dollars for a bass player. How's Dean?

Through the glass, Dean is on his third Danish, telling a lawyer a story with his hands.

**CAL:** Fine.

**RICHARD:** Mm.

Richard knows "fine" is a word Cal uses about machines.

### The vote

The band votes. Cal asks the question and counts.

- **Dean:** "Yes. Yes. It's a *bus*, Dad."
- **Riley:** "Yes. With one condition." ("Sunday Clothes" stays hers on every piece of paper. Cal writes it down.)
- **Ellis**, looking out the window at Broadway: "Yeah."
- **Cal:** the player chooses. Yes makes it unanimous, and Cal hates it. No makes it three to one, and Cal knew it would be.

**CAL:** I'll sign.

### Signed under protest

Dean signs with a flourish and draws a drum. Riley signs *Margaret A. Riley*. Ellis signs without reading, which Cal watches him do. Cal signs last, and in the margin of page nine, next to clause 14, in his neat Parker Jotter print, he writes:

> SIGNED UNDER PROTEST — C.M.

**LAWYER:** That doesn't have any legal effect.

**CAL:** I know.

Gil reads it and smiles.

**GIL:** Noted.

He means it kindly, which is worse.

> **Object.** In 1996, when Riley, Cal and Dean fight Monarch to release *Who Are You?* as they meant it, their lawyer puts page nine in front of a judge. It still has no legal effect. The judge reads it twice.

### The Lincoln

On Broadway, a long black Lincoln at the curb, and a driver in a cap holding a sign that says THE BLAKES.

**GIL:** For the week. You'll want it in this weather.

Dean is inside it before anyone else has moved.

**RILEY:** Dean. "No limousines."

**DEAN** *(already lying across the back seat)*: It's a Lincoln.

**RILEY:** It's a limousine.

**DEAN:** It's a Lincoln with ambitions.

Ellis stands on the sidewalk a second longer, looking back at the eight-foot photograph in the lobby window. Then he gets in.

> **The van promise.** Dean's clause (*no limousines*) is broken by Dean, gleefully, in the first hour with the new label.

## MISSION 2 — SINGLE VERSION

**Playable:** Riley · **Where:** Monarch Studio B, West 54th Street · **When:** Tuesday January 13, 11:00 a.m. – 7:00 p.m. · **Length:** ~25 min

### The ask

Gil told them at dinner, like it was good news. Monarch wants "Sunday Clothes" as the second single, re-cut in New York with Ellis singing lead, because radio knows his voice now, and a new voice on a second single is, in Gil's phrase, "starting over." Riley said, "Let me think about it."

8 a.m., Riley's hotel room. A knock. It's Ellis.

**ELLIS:** We don't have to.

**RILEY:** I know.

**ELLIS:** I'll tell him no.

**RILEY:** You'd sing it better than me on the radio.

**ELLIS:** That's not true.

**RILEY:** It's a little true.

He doesn't argue. That's the problem with Ellis: he doesn't lie to her about music.

The player chooses what Riley says. The options are the same decision, said differently: *"Do it."* / *"If it gets played, do it."* / *"Sing it right."* If she says the last, Ellis nods, like she's given him a set of instructions, which she has.

> **Design note.** Riley decides her song is worth more on the radio in someone else's voice than in a drawer in hers. She wanted her mother to hear it in the car.

### Studio B

Monarch's Studio B: a sloped glass ceiling, carpet on the walls, a 24-track machine, and a staff producer named **Lenny Sayre** (forty, gold chain, excellent ears, zero patience) who calls everybody "chief."

The band cuts the track in four hours. Lenny tightens the tempo and cuts the second verse in half. The half he cuts is the one with her mother's hands in it, the line she crossed out on a Kant syllabus a year ago and put back in the summer. Cal argues about the tempo and loses.

### Her song in his mouth

Riley is on the leather couch in the control room for the playback of Ellis's first take:

*She plays for the Lord at a quarter to eleven*
*and for nobody at all on Saturday…*

He sings it well, the way he sings everything, like he's telling you something he shouldn't. On the line about the Saturday organ he does a thing with the phrasing she never thought of, and it's better, and she hears that it's better, and that's the worst part of the day.

The player can:
- **Watch Ellis through the glass,** singing with his eyes shut.
- **Watch Lenny** punch the talkback: "That's the one, chief."
- **Look at the lyric sheet** on the console, in her handwriting. She wrote it out for him this morning so he'd have the words right.
- **Leave the room.** In the hallway by the elevator she adds a word to the list inside her notebook cover: *usufruct*. Nobody comes after her. After a minute she goes back in.

### The harmony

**LENNY** *(talkback)*: Riley, sweetheart, let's get you on the harmony.

Headphones. Her own song in her ears, in his voice, and her job is to sing *under* it. The Room's harmony verb, in a studio, alone: **above**, **below**, **unison**, or **silence**.

- **Below** is what Lenny wants. It's beautiful: her, holding up his voice from underneath.
- **Above** is the high part, the one she'd sing if the song were hers onstage. "Too much, sweetheart, it's his record now." She can keep singing it. He takes it and mixes it low.
- **Unison** doubles his voice. Nobody will ever hear her in it.
- **Silence.** She doesn't sing, three takes running, and Lenny doubles Ellis onto the harmony track instead. The single goes out with no Riley on it at all.

Tracked: the one she picks is the one the country hears on the radio all spring.

### Label copy

At seven, Cal puts the label-copy sheet in front of her without saying anything:

> SUNDAY CLOTHES (Single Version)
> (M. Riley)
> Stony Knob Music, BMI
> THE BLAKES

**CAL:** That's what goes on the label. Every copy.

**RILEY:** Thank you.

**CAL:** It's correct.

She looks at it for a long time. *(M. Riley).* Small type.

> **Setup.** The single reaches #14 in April. Every DJ in America introduces it as "Ellis Blake's 'Sunday Clothes.'"

## MISSION 3 — THE OPENER

**Playable:** Dean · **Where:** Cobo Arena, Detroit · **When:** Wednesday January 14, 4:00 p.m. – 1:00 a.m. · **Length:** ~30 min

### Arena

Detroit in January, snow gone black at the curbs. Cobo Arena, twelve thousand seats, sold out for Sawtooth: two gold records, a laser rig, a lead singer in a white jumpsuit. The Blakes get eighteen feet of the front of the stage, no soundcheck, house lights at half, thirty-five minutes.

The player is Dean, and Dean is in paradise. He stands in the middle of twelve thousand empty chairs and yells "HELLO DETROIT," and it comes back from four directions. He looks at Sawtooth's gong the way other people look at churches.

### Rusty's vial

In the tunnel under the stands, Sawtooth's drummer, **Rusty Kowalczyk** from Hamtramck, big and bearded and friendly, says the drum part on "Low Water" is "a mean thing, man, a mean thing, I mean that nice." After dinner he offers Dean a glass vial with a tiny spoon on a chain.

The player isn't asked. Dean takes it the way you'd take a stick of gum. The camera doesn't linger. Rusty says "Welcome to the big leagues, Georgia," and Dean says "Tannersville," and Rusty says "Same thing," and Dean lets it go.

### Thirty-five minutes

8:05 p.m. Half the crowd is in their seats. At arena scale the crowd model is big and slow. With *read the room*, Dean sees a dark field, cold almost everywhere, with a few bright points: college kids in the second row who know "Still Here," a man in section 112 who stopped on the stairs with two beers. The room answers in two seconds, three, or not at all.

> LOW WATER · SUNDAY CLOTHES · STILL HERE · TOMORROW PROBLEM · STONY KNOB · SHAPE NOTE (5)

The task is Dean's whole job: find the warm points and play to them until they spread. "Low Water": nobody. "Still Here": a patch of the floor stands up, maybe four hundred people. "Tomorrow Problem" is Dean's. The player can push the tempo, go big on the fills, throw a stick, and if he reads the room right the patch spreads to the lower bowl and the beer lines turn around, a few thousand people shouting *THAT'S A TOMORROW PROBLEM* at an opening act they'd never heard of an hour ago. "Shape Note": five minutes of drone to an arena waiting for lasers. Some people boo. More go quiet, and stay quiet.

The applause isn't big, and it's real.

### Paradise

The tunnel after, Sawtooth's lasers overhead. Dean against the cinderblock, soaked, grinning, jaw tight, heart going too fast, and he doesn't notice his heart because everything is going too fast.

Rusty passes and slaps his shoulder.

**RUSTY:** You got 'em on the joke song, man.

**DEAN:** It's not a joke.

He says it before he thinks. Rusty's already gone up the ramp into the light.

On the loading dock, in the snow, Tully is coiling cables. Steam comes off Dean's shoulders.

**TULLY:** You're shaking.

**DEAN:** It's cold.

**TULLY:** Uh huh.

## MISSION 4 — THE BUS

**Playable:** Ellis · **Where:** a 1974 Eagle coach converted for bands, somewhere between Toledo and Cleveland on I-75 and I-80 · **When:** Monday January 19, 11:00 p.m. – Tuesday January 20, 9:00 a.m. · **Length:** ~25 min

### The bus

Monarch's tour support bought a bus: a forty-foot Eagle coach with a Greyhound past, converted by a shop in Tannersville that builds coaches for Row acts. Twelve bunks with curtains, a front lounge, a back lounge with a television bolted to the wall, and a bathroom nobody is supposed to use for anything serious. A driver named **Earl** who has driven for Porter Wagoner and Conway Twitty and has never, in thirty years, been on a bus that smelled like this one.

Between tour missions the bus is the chapter's social hub, and everyone is somewhere:
- **Dean** in the front lounge, playing gin rummy with Earl at red lights, losing, paying in quarters.
- **Riley** in her bunk with the curtain half-open and a paperback, or at the table doing the crossword.
- **Cal** at the table with the ledger and a Panasonic recorder, listening back to last night on headphones.
- **Tully** asleep, always, anywhere.
- **Theo** rides in the crew truck behind and comes onto the bus at gas stops. He and Cal are careful.
- **Dex Lundgren**, for four days, in the back lounge.

### Dex

*Rave* sent Dex out for a cover story: four days with the band on the bus. He has his Sony, his notebook, his film canister, a velvet jacket too thin for Ohio in January, and the good manners of a man who knows he's a guest in somebody's house and plans to go through the medicine cabinet.

He doesn't interview anybody. He knows every band on earth, plays gin better than Earl, and never asks Ellis a direct question, which is how Ellis ends up talking to him more than anybody.

### Liar

11:40 p.m. Somewhere east of Toledo on the Turnpike. The front lounge. Dean and Earl at cards. Riley asleep. Ellis at the table with a memo book (number 68) and a pencil.

He's been doing what Dean does before shows for a week now. By the second week of the tour it's what the tour does at six-thirty, in a bathroom, off the back of a hand. It happens off-camera; the player sees the aftermath. He talks faster, laughs more, and can't sleep. He hasn't slept since Saturday.

Clara is on the couch across the table from him with her boots up on the cushion, in the jean jacket, eating nothing.

**CLARA:** He dresses like a sofa.

She means Dex. Ellis snorts.

**DEAN** *(from the cards)*: What?

**ELLIS:** Nothing. Your hand.

**DEAN:** What about my hand?

**ELLIS:** It's bad.

**DEAN:** You can't see my hand.

**ELLIS:** I can see your face.

Dean throws down a card. Earl picks it up and wins.

Under the table Ellis's knee is going like a sewing machine.

**CLARA:** Quit that.

It stops. After a while it starts again.

Clara leans across the table, chin on her fist.

**CLARA:** You sleeping?

**ELLIS** *(barely)*: I'm fine.

**CLARA:** Liar.

She says it the way she said it in October 1974, on the floor under his window, and in December on the windowsill: light and fond, a big sister catching him.

> **Replay layer.** "Liar," three times, always when Ellis says he's fine: I, V, VIII. This is the last. Chapter X says who she got it from.

### The first catch

Ellis gets up to go to the bathroom. The player walks him down the narrow aisle at walking pace, and nothing is happening: the engine and the cards. Clara is at the edge of the couch, and he has to pass her.

As he passes her, the switch begins, the grammar of eight chapters: the ambient sound narrows, the image catches on the faint frame-stutter, the controller gives the soft double pulse that comes with every switch, the focus starts to pull off Ellis, and the HUD begins to fade.

The focus lands on Clara. For six frames she's sharp, seen from exactly where Ellis stands: boots on the cushion, chin on her fist, the bus soft around her.

A second stutter.

Then the snap-back: the focus on Ellis, the HUD back all at once, the bus engine too loud, and one hard single pulse in the controller.

Ellis doesn't notice. He goes into the bathroom.

> **Design note — the failed catch.** For eight chapters the switch has meant *you are about to become someone else*, and it only goes to real people. Tonight it tries to go to Clara and can't, because there's nobody there to become. The six frames are Ellis's own eyes (§11.4), so Rule 3 holds. The sequence is fixed and must be identical every time it happens, pulse for pulse, so it reads as grammar, not a glitch. No text, no music cue. It happens once more, on television.

### New skin

1:30 a.m. Back at the table. The bus quiet. Dean asleep sitting up in the corner of the couch with cards in his hand. Dex across the table, apparently asleep, sunglasses on.

Ellis writes in memo book 68: the beginning of a song, the first thing he's written since New York.

> *I gave you a birthday every year you didn't get one.*
> *I gave you a jacket that was already yours.*
> *I gave you a voice that could carry the high part*
> *and a seat by the window, and never a door.*

He stops. He reads it. He writes, at the top of the page, NEW SKIN, and draws a line under it, and then can't find the next line, and puts his head down on his arms on the table.

**CLARA:** Look at the lines going under. Write that down.

The player can Observe from here (the window, the dark, the white lines going under the bus), or not.

### The page

A half-sleep state: the image dim and slightly doubled, the sound of the engine rising and falling. Ellis's eyes are nearly closed, his cheek on his arm. From this angle he can see the table, the memo book open by his elbow, and across it, Dex's hand.

Dex's hand moves to the memo book, slowly, the way you'd reach for a match in the dark. It turns the book toward him. It pauses.

Clara's voice, close, from beside Ellis's head:

**CLARA:** He's taking me.

The player can't move. There's no prompt. Ellis's eyes close and open. The sound of a page coming away from a stitched binding, very quiet under the engine.

When the image clears, the memo book is where it was. Dex is asleep across the table with his sunglasses on and his hands in his lap.

### Morning

9 a.m. Cleveland in the windows. Everyone waking up. Dean asking who ate his crackers.

Clara is sitting on the arm of the couch.

**CLARA:** Sit up. You've got the table printed on your face.

He sits up. He opens memo book 68. The player opens it with him.

The page is gone. There's a narrow stub where it was torn out, close to the stitching, careful. In sixty-eight notebooks since he was fourteen, Ellis has never torn out a page.

He looks across the lounge at Dex, who is drinking coffee and reading *Billboard* and says "Morning" without looking up.

The player gets a prompt, and it's small: *Ask him.* If Ellis asks ("You take something out of this?"), Dex looks at the notebook, and then at him, with perfect friendliness, and says "Out of your notebook? Man, I'd never," and goes back to *Billboard*. If Ellis doesn't ask, he closes the notebook and puts it inside his jacket, and from then on he keeps it there.

**CLARA:** He took me.

**ELLIS** *(under his breath)*: He took a song.

**CLARA:** Same thing.

> **Setup.** The torn page is the first half of "New Skin." It appears in *Rave* in Chapter IX, printed as a poem, months before the song is finished.

## MISSION 5 — CLEVELAND

**Playable:** Dean → Cal · **Where:** the Coliseum, Richfield, Ohio; the emergency room at Akron City Hospital · **When:** Saturday January 24, 8:00 p.m. – 6:00 a.m. · **Length:** ~25 min

### The best show of the tour

The Coliseum in Richfield: eighteen thousand seats, and Cleveland radio has been playing "Still Here" for a month. The player is Dean. The warm field is big from the first song, "Tomorrow Problem" takes the lower bowl, and the band follows him anywhere.

He did three lines before it and one in the middle of it, behind the riser during "Stony Knob."

The last note of "Shape Note." Dean comes down the riser stairs, and halfway down he stops.

### The tunnel

The player has control, and the control is wrong. The camera is breathing. Dean puts a hand on the stair rail. His heartbeat is in the audio, too fast and not even: a fast beat, a skip, two fast, a long one.

He sits down on the bottom step. He's grinning, because Dean grins, and he's gray.

**DEAN:** Huh.

Tully is there first, the way he always is.

**TULLY:** What.

**DEAN:** My heart's doing a thing.

**TULLY:** What kind of thing?

**DEAN:** A jazz thing.

Tully puts two fingers on Dean's neck. He was a squad leader in a rifle company. He counts. His face doesn't change.

**TULLY** *(over his shoulder, not loud)*: Cal. Get the truck.

### The ride

The crew truck on I-77 in snow, Tully driving, Dean against the passenger door with his coat open, sweating in the cold. Dean talks the whole way: Rusty's gong, Cobo, the man in section 112, Bobby, who'll be five in February.

**CAL:** Stop talking.

**DEAN:** I can't. If I stop talking I'll notice.

### The ER

Stutter, at the emergency-room doors: the camera leaves Dean on a gurney and settles on Cal in the hallway with Dean's satin jacket over his arm. Akron City Hospital, 11:20 p.m. Green tile, plastic chairs, a man with a bloody towel on his hand, a Coke machine.

A waiting mission:
- The Coke machine takes Cal's quarter and gives nothing. He opens the panel with his pocketknife (it's a Vendo; he knows the latch) and frees the can. The man with the towel watches with deep respect.
- The ledger on his knee: *1/24/76 — Akron City Hosp. — D.H. —* and he stops, because he doesn't know yet what to put.
- Riley calls the hall phone. "Do you want me to come?" "No. Keep the others there." "Cal." "I've got him."

### Lynette

A doctor comes out at 1:40 a.m.: a resident in his twenties, very tired, not unkind.

**DOCTOR:** He's in atrial fibrillation. Irregular heartbeat. It's the cocaine. He admitted to cocaine.

**CAL:** Is he going to—

**DOCTOR:** He's twenty-one. It'll probably convert on its own in a few hours. If it doesn't, we'll help it. He's asking for a dime.

Cal gives him a roll of dimes out of his shirt pocket. He carries one on the road for exactly this.

A nurse wheels Dean to the hall pay phone with the monitor on a cart beside him. He dials the Starlite. Lynette's on shift. Cal, ten feet away, can only hear Dean's side.

**DEAN:** Hey. It's me. ... No, I'm okay. ... Ohio. ... It's a hospital. ... My heart's doing a thing. ... No, it's a little thing. ... Yeah. ... Yeah, I was.

A long pause. Dean shuts his eyes.

**DEAN:** I know. ... I know. ... Will you— can you just stay on? You don't have to say anything.

He holds the receiver with both hands. Faintly, through it: the Starlite's jukebox, a woman's voice saying "I'll be right there, hon" to somebody else, and then an open line, six hundred miles long, and Dean breathing into it.

After a while he holds the receiver out to Cal without looking at him.

**DEAN:** She wants you.

**LYNETTE** *(on the phone)*: Is he lying to me?

**CAL:** About what?

**LYNETTE:** Anything.

Cal looks at Dean in the wheelchair, gray, with the monitor wires coming out of his shirt, still trying to grin.

**CAL:** Some.

**LYNETTE:** Okay. Thank you.

A pause.

**LYNETTE:** Keep him alive till Tuesday. I'll yell at him Tuesday.

She hangs up.

### 5:40 a.m.

Dean's heart converts on its own at 5:12. The nurse finds Cal asleep in the plastic chair, glasses crooked, the ledger slipping off his knee. At 5:40 they discharge Dean with a sheet of instructions.

**DEAN** *(reading)*: "Avoid stimulants including caffeine, nicotine and cocaine."

He folds it and puts it in his pocket.

**DEAN:** That's a tomorrow problem.

Cal doesn't laugh. Dean hears himself, and doesn't laugh either.

Outside it's snowing on the parking lot, and it's already tomorrow. In the truck, Cal finishes the line:

> *1/24/76 — Akron City Hosp. — D.H. — ER, 1 night — $86.40. Paid from fund.*

He looks at it. Then he adds, smaller:

> *Not to be paid back.*

## MISSION 6 — HOTEL BAR

**Playable:** Ellis · **Where:** the bar of a big old hotel on Michigan Avenue, Chicago · **When:** Thursday January 29, 12:50 – 1:40 a.m. · **Length:** ~15 min

### Upstairs

After the first of two nights at the International Amphitheatre, Riley walks Ellis to his door on the ninth floor. They haven't been alone for more than a minute in weeks.

**RILEY:** You said after New York.

**ELLIS:** I know what I said.

**RILEY:** We were in New York. Then we were in Detroit.

**ELLIS:** After the tour.

**RILEY:** That's February fifteenth.

**ELLIS:** I know what day it is.

She pushes her glasses up with a knuckle and doesn't say any of the things she could say.

**RILEY:** Okay. February fifteenth.

She goes to her room. Clara is at the elevator end of the hall, sitting on the radiator.

**CLARA:** Don't you let some doctor poke at you.

He doesn't go into his room. He goes back down in the elevator.

### The bar

A long dark bar, brass rails, a mirror behind the bottles that the camera never looks into, a piano player finishing "Feelings" for four people. In a booth at the far end, in a velvet jacket, with sunglasses on at one in the morning: Dex Lundgren.

Ellis orders a beer. Clara is at the other end of the bar with her boots on the rung of the next stool, watching the piano player like he's the funniest thing she's ever seen.

**CLARA:** Tip that man. He's trying so hard.

### Elaine

A young woman sits down next to him: twenty-four, a white rabbit-fur coat she keeps on, a glass of white wine she brought from a table, smart, a little drunk, pleased with herself in a way that's almost likable. She puts a copy of *Rave* on the bar, folded open to the January article.

**CLARA:** Look at the pen marks on that. Watch yourself.

**ELAINE:** I'm not going to ask for an autograph.

**ELLIS:** Okay.

**ELAINE:** I'm going to ask you something else.

Every branch of the conversation goes the same place. She's read the article four times, and she talks about it like a seminar paper: the mother who left, the sister, the stone on the cover, "Old friend."

**ELAINE:** Clara's your sister. The one who died. You changed her name for the song.

**ELLIS:** My sister's name was Grace.

It's out before he knows it.

**ELAINE:** Then who's Clara?

Ellis doesn't answer. At the far end of the bar, Clara has stopped watching the piano player and is watching them.

**ELAINE:** You keep looking down there.

**ELLIS:** Bartender's slow.

**ELAINE:** No. You look at the end of the bar like somebody's sitting there.

She finds a paragraph she has underlined in ballpoint. If Ellis gets up to leave, she reads it to his back, and he turns around. If he stays, she reads it to his face.

**ELAINE** *(reading)*: "'Old friend,' he says, and he says it the way men say the names of women who are married or dead."

She closes the magazine on her finger.

**ELAINE:** Which one is she?

### The kiss

The prompt comes up on the question. It's the only thing on the screen:

*Make her stop.*

No timer. Elaine keeps talking: the song, the stone, a thing her professor said about grief and doubles. The piano player starts "Misty." The prompt stays.

When the player presses it, Ellis kisses her, quick and hard. She's surprised, and then she isn't, and her hand is on his face. He pulls away, puts two dollars on the bar and walks out, without looking at the end of the bar where Clara was, or at the booth where Dex's pen is already moving.

### The room

The ninth floor. He turns on the television, and at 1:40 a.m. in Chicago the only thing on is a test pattern: the Indian-head card, the tone. He sits on the floor at the foot of the bed in the gray light of it. Clara isn't in the room. The player can look for her. She isn't anywhere.

## MISSION 7 — ELEVEN TIMES

**CAL:** Why are you telling me?

Eleven marks.

### The fight

Cal has decided. The player chooses how, never whether.

**CAL:** Dex is going to print the girl.

**ELLIS:** Let him.

**CAL:** Riley's going to read it in a magazine.

**ELLIS:** Then I'll tell her.

**CAL:** When?

Ellis doesn't answer that.

**DEAN:** Cal.

**CAL:** The tunnel in Toledo. The hallway in Indianapolis. Twice at soundcheck in Chicago. The bar, before the girl sat down; Dex saw that too. Tuesday, here, at this table.

**ELLIS:** You counted.

**CAL:** I count everything.

The player picks an option, Ellis answers, the options narrow, and the player picks again:

- **"You need a doctor."** → *"I'll take it under advisement."*
- **"Who is she?"** → *"A friend."* / Cal: *"Be specific."* → silence.
- **"Riley's scared to death."** → Ellis looks at the closed curtain. *"Riley can talk for herself."*

When the options run out there's one left, and it's the one Cal came here to say:

**CAL:** She isn't real, Ellis.

The bus hits an expansion joint. The cards on the table jump.

Ellis looks at Cal for a long time. It's the face Wayne had at the kitchen door in Chapter I, reaching for the sentence that would hurt most.

**ELLIS** *(quiet)*: At least mine isn't somebody I keep in a hotel room.

Nobody moves.

**DEAN:** What?

Ellis doesn't take it back. He doesn't look away from Cal. He knows the instant it's out, and he leaves it there on the table between them like a bill.

Tully's boots come down off the couch. He sits up. He isn't asleep and hasn't been.

**TULLY:** That's enough.

### Kenosha

Cal closes the ledger. The player has no dialogue left, only movement. He walks to the front of the bus, past Riley's curtain, which has opened two inches.

**CAL:** Earl. Next exit.

**EARL:** Kenosha?

**CAL:** Wherever.

**CAL:** You don't have to.

**TULLY:** I'll ride with the truck.

The crew truck pulls into the lot behind the bus. Theo is in the passenger seat. He rolls down the window.

Dean is at a window. Riley is at a window. Ellis isn't, and then, when the player turns the camera up at the bus, he is.

**THEO:** What happened?

**CAL:** I'm going home for two days.

**THEO:** I'll come.

**CAL:** No. You mix. Make them sound good without me.

**THEO:** That's impossible.

**CAL:** I know.

Theo gets out of the truck, into the snow, in front of the bus and the crew and a trucker at the fuel island, and puts his hand on Cal's arm, and leaves it there. Cal doesn't move it.

### Switch

## MISSION 8 — NO FLOOR

**Playable:** Ellis → Cal · **Where:** the Milwaukee Arena; the Dane County Coliseum, Madison; Mercer Radio & TV, Tannersville; a hotel in Bloomington, Minnesota · **When:** Sunday February 1 – Wednesday February 4 · **Length:** ~30 min

### Phil

Monarch flies in a session bassist: **Phil Treadway**, turtleneck, reading glasses on a cord, a little embarrassed to be there. He asks for charts. The band has never had charts. Riley writes them on Pfister Hotel stationery in an hour.

### Milwaukee

The player is Ellis, and the performance system shows what Cal was. When Ellis *reaches* ("Stay with me," off-mic), Dean follows, Riley follows a beat late, and Phil keeps playing the chart, because the chart says eight bars. The turn doesn't happen. Underneath everything, there's no **floor**. "Shape Note" without the drone is thin, and they cut it to three minutes. Phil plays every note correctly all night.

In the space where the floor was, Clara is louder: at Ellis's shoulder, on the monitor wedge, at the lip of the stage. She sings the high part into his ear during "Still Here," the words, not humming, and with the bass gone there's nothing under her. Her voice sits exactly where Grace's would.

Between songs, while Phil turns a page:

**CLARA:** Quit glaring at him. He's reading as fast as he can. Give him his eight bars.

The crowd doesn't know anything's wrong.

### Madison

Monday, worse. Ellis loses the second verse of "Low Water" and stands at the mic with his mouth open while the band vamps. Riley sings the verse for him from her mic, without being asked.

### The line

After Madison, 2:10 a.m., a hotel room on the Beltline. Ellis on the floor against the heater with memo book 68. If the player opens the notebook now, there's a new line in pencil, after two lines about the snow on the parking lot:

> *If a train came through right now that'd be all right.*

No prompt. No music. Nobody sees it.

> **Design note.** Ellis thinks about death here the way people in pain do: passively, as relief, without a plan. He isn't trying to die. In the epilogue Riley finds the line and decides, alone, that nobody else will ever read it.

### Walt's bench

Stutter across two states, with no carrier: a clock radio warming up in a Madison hotel, a radio warming up on a repair bench in east Tannersville.

Monday evening, Mercer Radio & TV. Cal has been home since Friday night. His mother has fed him and hasn't asked. The player can fix a Zenith Trans-Oceanic with a dead oscillator, and Cal fixes it in twenty minutes, and it works. Then he takes the back off again and starts over. Walt watches him do it.

**WALT:** The drummer called. Four times.

**CAL:** He's not my drummer.

**WALT:** Four times.

Walt solders something on his own side of the bench.

**WALT:** You fixed that radio twice.

Cal looks at the Zenith. He did.

**WALT:** Airport's forty minutes. I'll drive you in the morning.

**CAL:** I didn't say I was going.

Walt turns off his iron and goes upstairs. In the morning the station wagon is warming up in the alley at 5:30, with a sack from his mother on the passenger seat: two biscuits and a thermos.

### Bloomington

Tuesday afternoon, a hotel near the Met Center. A band meeting in Dean's room: Dean, Riley, Ellis, Tully, Eddie, and Theo by the window, who has mixed two shows without a bass player and looks like it. Phil has gone home with Monarch's check and Riley's charts, which he asked to keep.

The door opens. Cal, with his bass case, in his father's old overcoat. Nobody says anything. Dean half stands. There's no dialogue wheel. Cal sets the case down and looks at Ellis.

**CAL:** Theo's mine.

He says it to Ellis, and then he looks at each of them: Dean, Riley, Tully, Eddie.

**CAL:** That's real. You don't get to use it.

Theo, at the window, looks at the floor. He's smiling, and trying not to.

**ELLIS:** Cal.

**CAL:** I know.

He doesn't want the apology in this room. He picks up the bass case.

**CAL:** Soundcheck's at four. I want to run "Shape Note" with the full drone. Theo, how'd it sound?

**THEO:** Like a room with no floor.

**CAL:** Yeah.

He stops at the door.

**CAL:** Riley. Thanks for the charts. He said they were good.

**RILEY:** They were terrible.

**CAL:** He said they were good.

### Coffee

Wednesday, 8:15 a.m. A knock on Cal's door: Dean, with two paper cups from the lobby, one with the lid already off because he spilled it in the elevator. He hands Cal the good one.

**DEAN:** For the record, I figured you were just boring.

**CAL:** I am boring.

**DEAN:** Exactly. It's great.

**CAL:** He mixes it correctly.

**DEAN:** He mixes it too loud and I love him for it. I'm going to tell him at breakfast. In front of everybody. Loudly.

## INTERLUDE — SNOW DAY

**Playable:** Dean → Cal → Riley → Ellis → Dean · **Where:** Kessel, Wisconsin, on State Highway 29: the Cenex, the Red Pine Supper Club, the Pla-Mor Lanes next door · **When:** Friday February 6, 4:40 a.m. – Saturday February 7, 5:10 a.m. · **Length:** ~25 min

### Kessel

The storm comes across Minnesota behind the bus and catches it on Highway 29 in the dark. At 4:40 a.m. a trooper in a fur hat waves Earl off the road with a flashlight. There's a milk truck on its side a mile ahead, and nothing is going east until the plows come through, and the plows aren't coming through until tomorrow.

Earl takes the only exit with lights. Kessel, Wisconsin: a water tower, a grain elevator, a Catholic church, a Cenex, and at the edge of town, sharing one plowed lot, the Red Pine Supper Club and the Pla-Mor Lanes. Snow goes sideways through the Cenex sign.

The player is Dean, woken by the engine note changing. The bus is dark and warm, and everyone else is asleep, Ellis too, face down in his bunk with his boots on.

Earl comes back from the pay phone at the Cenex with snow on his shoulders.

**EARL:** Green Bay's off.

**DEAN:** Says who?

**EARL:** Says Wisconsin.

Dean sits down across the front table from him and takes a napkin out of his jacket. It's from a Howard Johnson's in Toledo, soft as a handkerchief by now, ruled into two columns, DEAN and EARL, with three weeks of gin down both sides. At the bottom, circled, is what Dean owes: $41.75.

**DEAN:** We got a day.

**EARL:** We got a day and a night.

Dean shuffles.

### The Seeburg

The Red Pine opens at eleven because Vern Schultz can't see the point of not. Knotty pine, red leather booths, a fieldstone fireplace, a muskie mounted over the back bar, and by the register a Packers schedule for a season everyone in Kessel is trying to forget. The room fills with truckers off the closed highway.

The jukebox is a Seeburg by the cigarette machine. Whatever you press, it plays "Convoy."

The player is Cal. He has heard "Convoy" nine times since he sat down.

**VERN:** Since Christmas. My brother-in-law says it's the tubes.

**CAL:** It doesn't have tubes. Which selections?

**VERN:** All of 'em.

**CAL:** Be specific.

**VERN:** All of 'em is specific.

Cal takes the back off with the screwdriver on his pocketknife. Theo holds a flashlight for him without being asked. The player follows the light down a rack of relays, pressing a selection and listening, until one relay doesn't click: a contact has welded itself shut, and every selection falls through to the same record. Cal borrows an emery board from Marlys Schultz's purse, files the contact clean and puts the back on.

He presses F-7. The Seeburg thinks about it, and plays Freddy Fender.

A trucker at the bar turns on his stool and claps three times.

Theo puts in a quarter and presses the button for "Convoy." It plays "Convoy."

**CAL:** That one works too.

Marlys won't take money for the repair, and she won't take it for lunch either. She brings Cal a bowl of booyah from the pot on the back of the stove, a sleeve of saltines and a piece of sour cream raisin pie, and stands by the booth until he has eaten some of each.

**MARLYS:** You fixed the Seeburg.

**CAL:** It was one contact.

**MARLYS:** There's more booyah.

### Pla-Mor Lanes

Eight lanes, the pinsetters banging behind the wall, league shirts on hooks, and over the bar a Hamm's sign with a waterfall that moves. League night is cancelled. Riley and Tully have the place to themselves.

The player is Riley, in rented shoes with a 9 on the heel, holding a maroon house ball. She has questions.

Tully shows her once. Four steps, the ball goes out on the fourth, and you look at the arrows painted on the lane, not at the pins. He rolls it over the second arrow from the right, and the pins go down with a noise like a drawer of silverware hitting the floor.

**RILEY:** Why would I look at the arrows? The pins are the objective.

**TULLY:** Arrows are closer.

**RILEY:** You aim at what you want to hit. Name one sport where you aim at something you don't want to hit. I'll wait.

**TULLY:** Pool.

A long pause.

**RILEY:** Fair.

The player controls her approach, her release and where she looks. If she looks at the pins, the ball finds the gutter. She looks at the pins. Gutter. She throws it harder. Gutter, faster.

**RILEY:** It should curve. The friction on a wooden surface—

**TULLY:** Lane's oiled.

**RILEY:** Define "oiled."

**TULLY:** Oiled.

Gutter.

In the sixth frame she looks at the second arrow, mostly to prove him wrong, and throws a strike. Then another. Then a third. The man behind the counter reaches under it for a cowbell he keeps for this and rings it. Two farmers at the bar turn around.

**RILEY:** I understand it now. It's the angle of entry into the pocket, relative to the—

Ninth frame. Gutter.

**TULLY:** Uh huh.

She finishes with a 94. Tully bowls a 181 without taking his coat off, tears the score sheet off the pad and hands it to her. She puts it in her pocket.

### Fish fry

Friday, 6:30 p.m. Every table in the Red Pine is full, and the windows are white. The band, the crew and Earl have the long table under the muskie. Bev, who has waited tables here on Fridays since Truman, puts down a relish tray without being asked: carrot and celery sticks, radishes, green onions standing up in a glass, pickled beets, and a crock of port wine cheese with breadsticks.

Then the drinks.

**DEAN:** A brandy old-fashioned.

**BEV:** Sweet or sour?

**DEAN:** Yes.

Riley orders one sweet and says the *-ed*. Bev writes down "brandy old fashion sweet" and says it back without one. Riley tries it Bev's way and can't. By the end of the round the whole table is saying it, in five accents, none of them from Wisconsin, and Bev says "Close" to each one. Cal has a Coke. Earl has a Point Special.

The player is Ellis. In front of him is a brandy old fashion sweet with an orange slice and two cherries on a plastic sword.

Clara is sitting on the radiator under the window with her boots on the rung of an empty chair.

**CLARA:** You're drinking a fruit cup.

The fish comes on oval platters: beer-battered cod, all you can eat for $2.95, with coleslaw and rye bread and a choice of potato pancakes, German potato salad or fries. Dean asks what the difference is. Bev says, "Potatoes."

Ellis moves a piece of cod around his plate.

**CLARA:** Eat something.

He eats a pickled beet. She waits. He eats the cod.

Dean eats four platters. On the fourth, Marlys comes out of the kitchen to watch. Earl eats perch with his napkin tucked into his collar, one fillet at a time.

At seven the Arnie Zastrow Dutchmen set up by the fireplace: a concertina, a tuba, a trumpet and a drum kit, four men with about two hundred and seventy years between them. The drummer came in on a snowmobile with his snare in a feed sack. Dean stands up to look at his kit and doesn't sit down again for a while.

They open with "In Heaven There Is No Beer," and the floor fills.

A woman in a lavender pantsuit and snow boots stops at the end of the table. Irene Pfaff, seventy-eight, a dairy farmer's widow, who has danced at the Red Pine every Friday since it opened. She points at Ellis.

**MRS. PFAFF:** You. Can you polka?

**ELLIS:** I might could.

He can. Nobody at the table knew. The player steers and the feet take care of themselves: hop, step, step, counterclockwise around the floor with everybody else, Mrs. Pfaff leading and pretending not to. The truckers at the bar watch the way you'd watch a dog walk on its hind legs.

**MRS. PFAFF:** You've done this before.

**ELLIS:** Allegedly.

The song ends. He starts back toward the table.

**ELLIS:** I'm fixing to sit down, ma'am.

**MRS. PFAFF:** No, you're not.

The Dutchmen go into "Beer Barrel Polka," and the whole Red Pine sings the chorus, Earl included. Mrs. Pfaff keeps Ellis for that one and for the waltz after it.

When he gets back to the table his fish is cold and Riley has eaten his coleslaw.

**RILEY:** Strong polka.

**CLARA:** Quit showing off.

He eats the cold fish.

### Gin

1:15 a.m. The bus in the Red Pine's lot, idling, snow packed white on the windows on one side. Everyone in the bunks. Dean and Earl at the front table under the one reading light, with the napkin between them.

The player is Dean. It's a penny a point, and he's down $61.50, and his per diem went into the Red Pine's cigarette machine and a great many brandy old fashion sweets.

**DEAN:** Double or nothing.

**EARL:** On what?

Dean looks around the lounge. He looks at the ceiling.

**DEAN:** The bus.

**EARL:** It ain't yours.

**DEAN:** It's Monarch's. Monarch bought it against our royalties. A quarter of the royalties are mine, and I'm putting up the whole bus, because I'm generous.

Earl deals.

The hand is played for real, and Earl plays gin better than the player and much better than Dean. Around the eighth draw he lays his cards down.

**EARL:** Gin.

Dean writes it at the bottom of the napkin, under the circle, in capitals: IOU ONE (1) BUS, EAGLE, 40 FT. D.H. Earl reads it, folds the napkin and puts it in his wallet.

**EARL:** So I own the bus.

**DEAN:** You'd still have to drive it.

**EARL:** I drive it anyhow.

**DEAN:** Exactly. So nothing's changed. Deal.

He puts up the crew truck.

**TULLY** *(from a bunk, not asleep)*: No.

Dean deals anyway.

At 5:10 a.m. a county plow goes by on 29 with its blade down and its light going around on the snow. Earl puts his cards face down, starts the bus and goes out to scrape the windshield.

> **Design note.** The chapter's breath: a day nobody planned, where nothing goes wrong. Nothing here is tracked, and nothing here is a clue.

## MISSION 9 — FIVE

**LYNETTE:** Leave the drums.

## MISSION 10 — NIGHT STAGE

**Playable:** Ellis · **Where:** Studio 4, a television building on West 57th Street, New York · **When:** Saturday March 13, 2:00 – 6:30 p.m. (taped for broadcast Friday March 19, 11:30 p.m.) · **Length:** ~20 min

### Night Stage

*Night Stage* is a late-night network music show, taped "live-to-tape" a week ahead, which means nothing gets fixed afterward. A black floor, three pedestal cameras and a crane, and a hundred and fifty people who were handed tickets on the street. Behind the riser, twenty feet wide on a painted scrim, is the Bowery photograph, the player's version: Ellis's face behind Ellis, three stories high.

### The dressing room

Ellis in the makeup chair with a paper bib around his neck. Rosalie powders his forehead and tells him he has good bones. **CLARA:** You look like a cake.

**ELLIS** *(to Rosalie)*: Is that enough?

**ROSALIE:** Honey, the lights'll eat you alive.

**CLARA:** Hush. Let her work.

### The floor

5:10 p.m. "Low Water" in one take, and it's good. Then "Still Here." The floor manager counts them in on his fingers.

**CLARA** *(from somewhere behind camera two)*: Sing to the one with the red light on it.

*Nobody here—*

### Forty seconds

The player has one input on the screen: **Continue.** Until they press it, the silence plays in real time, and the band fills it:

- **Cal** drops to the root and holds it, the floor.
- **Dean** plays a pulse on the floor tom with mallets, soft, a heartbeat.
- **Riley** lets one open string on the twelve ring, then another, and holds a drone.

Ellis looks at the floor between the cameras. Clara looks back at him. She doesn't say anything. In the control room the director says *what's he doing* into his headset, and the producer behind him, with a cigarette, says *stay on him, don't cut, don't you dare cut.*

- If the player presses **Continue**, Ellis sings the next line (*"And I'm still here"*), the band comes up under him as if they'd planned it, and the song finishes.
- If not, at ninety seconds Riley steps to her own mic and sings the line herself, quietly, and on the second phrase Ellis comes in under her.

The studio audience doesn't applaud right away. They don't know if it's over. Then they do.

> **Design note — the second failed catch.** The same sequence as the bus, frame for frame and pulse for pulse. On broadcast, the country sees a young man stop in the middle of a line and stare at an empty studio floor for somewhere between forty seconds and a minute and a half (the player decides how long) while his band holds a drone under him. *Night Stage* reruns it twice.

### After

The dressing-room hallway. The producer finds them, glowing.

**PRODUCER:** That was the most electric thing I've seen on that floor in three years. The silence. My God. Did you plan that?

Nobody answers him. He goes away happy.

Riley finds Ellis in the dressing room, alone, with the makeup still on and the paper bib still around his neck.

**RILEY:** What happened?

**ELLIS:** I lost the line.

**RILEY:** You didn't lose it.

He doesn't say anything. She takes the paper bib off him and folds it and puts it on the counter.

**RILEY:** She was there.

A long pause.

**ELLIS:** She walked out on the floor.

Riley sits down on the counter next to where Clara was, and after a while she takes a tissue and a jar of cold cream from Rosalie's station and starts taking the makeup off his face, and he lets her.

## MISSION 11 — RAVE

**Playable:** Riley · **Where:** a hotel lobby on Central Park South; Riley's room; a loft on Greene Street · **When:** Monday March 15, 11:00 a.m. – Tuesday March 16, 6:30 a.m. · **Length:** ~30 min

### The rack

Monday morning in New York. On the way to get coffee, Riley passes the newsstand by the elevators, and stops. Two things on the same wire rack, side by side.

*Rave*, April 1976. A much more famous man on the cover. Down the side, in orange:

> **ON THE BUS WITH THE BLAKES**
> **Dex Lundgren: Four Days in the Loud Part**

And *Metro*, a New York weekly on newsprint, on the bottom shelf. The cover is a photograph from the Bowery session that nobody used: Riley alone, turned half away from the camera, with her glasses on. The headline is one word:

> **RILEY**

The player chooses which she reads first. Both are readable in full, standing at the rack, the way you do.

### Metro

Nina Sorensen's piece is four columns long.

> *The best musician in the Blakes isn't the one they're named after. Listen to what happens under Ellis Blake's voice on "Borrowed Stone": a twelve-string tuned to an open chord, one string ringing against the others like a bell nobody's ringing on purpose. That's Riley. Listen to "Sunday Clothes," the best song on the record, which she wrote about her mother, a church organist in Georgia who plays Bach on Saturday mornings to an empty sanctuary. Riley plays like somebody raised in a choir loft who got out with the hymnal.*

And then, depending on what she did in Studio B:

- *(below)* *…which is now a hit single, sung by Ellis Blake, with its author holding up his voice from underneath. It is a theft committed by a friend, and the victim sings backup.*
- *(above)* *…with its author audible, just, on a high harmony somebody tried hard to bury.*
- *(unison)* *…with its author doubling his voice so closely you'd need a lab to find her.*
- *(silence)* *…with its author nowhere on it. I asked her why. She said, "I didn't have anything to add." I don't believe her, and I don't blame her.*

Riley reads the last paragraph twice.

### Rave

Dex's piece is eight pages, with photographs from the bus, and it's brilliant; that's the problem with Dex. On the sixth page:

> *At 1:30 in the morning in the bar of a big old hotel on Michigan Avenue, a girl in a white rabbit coat read Ellis Blake a sentence of mine out of this magazine and asked him which one Clara was, married or dead. I was there. I watched him hear it. And then he kissed her, like she'd said a password, and put two dollars on the bar, and left. I don't know who Clara is. I'm not sure he does.*

On the letters page, under a headline in *Rave*'s ugliest typeface (SPACE DEATH, INDEED), a letter from Richmond:

> *I SAW ELLIS BLAKE at the Monroe Hotel at 2 in the morning in November having a fight with NOBODY. He was yelling at the end of the hall and there was nobody there. I love the Blakes but I think somebody should check on him. — Denise A., Richmond, Va.*

*Rave*'s reply: *Denise, we check on him every month. See page 42.*

None of the choices at the rack is wrong: **buy both** ($1.25 and 50¢); **buy *Metro* only**; **put *Rave* back** facing the wall, the way she did in December; or **buy every copy of *Metro*** (six). "Big fan?" "I'm in it." "Congratulations."

### The telephone

Her room. The player can paste *Metro* into the scrapbook in her suitcase (she does; the prompt is the only one), and the *Rave* page or not.

The phone on the nightstand rings: a call from Linwood, Georgia.

**JOAN** *(on the phone)*: Maggie.

**RILEY:** Mama. Is everything—

**JOAN:** Everything's fine. I heard it on the radio. In the car. On the way to choir.

A pause.

**JOAN:** That boy sang your song.

**RILEY:** It's my song, Mama.

**JOAN:** I know whose song it is.

Another pause. Behind her, the Linwood kitchen: a clock, the dishwasher.

**JOAN:** He sings it too fast.

Riley laughs. It surprises her. It goes on a little too long, and turns into something else at the end, and she puts her hand over the receiver.

**JOAN:** Is he all right? That boy?

The player chooses: *"He's fine."* / *"No."* / silence. If *"He's fine,"* Riley tucks her hair behind her ear, the thing she does when she lies, and Joan, who can't see her, says "Maggie" the way she said it when Riley was nine, and waits, and Riley says, "No." If *"No,"* Joan is quiet. If silence, Joan waits, and says, "I see."

**JOAN:** Are you?

**RILEY:** I'm—

She stops.

**JOAN:** Come home for a Saturday. I'll play for you.

**RILEY:** I will.

### Greene Street

Nina left a message at the desk: a party tonight, a loft on Greene Street, *come if you want, don't if you don't*. Riley goes alone and doesn't tell anyone.

The loft is up a freight elevator the player runs themselves (a lever, a gate): a white room with columns, paintings leaned against the walls, jug wine, forty people, and *Horses* on a record player, with somebody's *Frampton Comes Alive!* hidden face down under the crate so nobody can put it on. It's the kind of room she thought she'd be in when she was nineteen.

Nina introduces Riley to people as "the best thing on that record," every time, until Riley asks her to stop, and she doesn't. At the tall window, over the cobblestones in the rain:

**NINA:** When do you make yours?

**RILEY:** My what?

**NINA:** Your record. Just you.

Riley laughs, like it's a joke. Nina doesn't. Riley thinks about the third verse of "Occasionally Astonishing," which won't come, and doesn't mention it.

Around midnight someone offers her a small square of blotter paper on the tip of a finger.

The player isn't given a choice. Riley takes it. She has always believed, the way William James believed about nitrous oxide, that this is a way of seeing more. And tonight she wants, very badly, to be anywhere but inside her own head.

### The trip

It starts beautifully, and it's played straight: the white walls breathe, and *Horses* becomes a building she can walk around inside.

Then the bathroom: a clawfoot tub, a bare bulb, a mug of tea somebody gave her. She looks down at her hands. It's the chipped enamel mug from the farmhouse in the South Fork in July.

The camera leaves Riley's body and shows the pasture party from Chapter VI from a few yards away: the porch, the fireflies, the light-show wheel on the barn wall. Riley, eight months younger, in her peasant blouse, crossing the porch with the mug, holding it out to Ellis.

The player can move the camera around this scene. They can't change it. If they try to make the Riley in the memory stop (the prompt is *Don't*), her hand keeps moving, and the scene loops back three seconds and plays again: the porch, the mug, her hand, his hand taking it. The harder the player tries, the more often it loops. The screen door. Her own voice, eight months ago, saying something about how it doesn't put things in your head.

It releases only when the player stops pressing anything. She's on the bathroom floor with her back against the tub, holding a mug of cold tea.

### The stairwell

Four in the morning. The loft's back stairwell, one bulb, rain on a skylight. Riley on a step with her knees up. Nina finds her there, sits two steps below, and doesn't say anything for a long time.

**RILEY:** I gave it to him.

**NINA:** Gave what to who?

**RILEY:** Something he didn't need.

If Riley explains (the farmhouse, the tea, the field, Milledgeville), Nina listens to all of it and says, "Okay." If she doesn't, Nina doesn't ask.

Dawn in the skylight, gray.

**RILEY:** I'm not doing that again.

**NINA:** The acid?

**RILEY:** Any of it.

**NINA:** Okay.

Nina walks her down four flights and hails her a cab on Houston.

**NINA:** I meant what I wrote.

**RILEY:** I know. I read it twice.

The cab pulls away. Riley watches Nina get smaller in the back window, standing in the rain in a man's jacket with her hands in the pockets.

> **Design note — Riley's belief, broken.** Riley has always believed altered perception shows you what's true. Tonight it shows her one true thing (she handed him the cup) and loops it until she stops fighting. She stops that night, for good, on her own.

## MISSION 12 — KITCHEN, COLD BRANCH ROAD

**Playable:** Riley · **Where:** the Blake house, Cold Branch Road · **When:** Thursday March 18, 2:30 – 4:30 p.m. · **Length:** ~20 min

### The drive

Thursday, a gray afternoon. Riley drives the Datsun an hour from Tannersville to Cold Branch Road without telling anybody. On the way out of town she passes the college. Spring registration was in January. She didn't go to that one either.

Ellis is in Tannersville today at Dalton Sound, helping Frank mix a live tape from the tour. She checked.

The Blake house. The one bulb over the porch, off in the daytime. Wayne's F-100 in the yard. Tater comes down off the porch and barks once, a real bark, at a real person, and then waddles over to smell her hand and leans his whole weight against her shin.

### The door

Wayne opens it in his undershirt and work khakis, in his socks. He's home on a Thursday afternoon because the railroad consolidated his section in January and put him on the extra board, and he has been offered a yard job in Tannersville or early retirement, and has taken neither.

He looks at her. He knows who she is.

**WAYNE:** He's not here.

**RILEY:** I know. I came to see you.

**WAYNE:** Hm.

He stands there a while. Then he steps back and holds the door.

### The kitchen

Through Riley's eyes the kitchen is smaller than she expected, and cleaner. The oilcloth on the table. The percolator. The bills pile by the salt shaker, and on top of it, the coupon book: *Coupon 36 of 48.* Four chairs around the table: two with the seats worn shiny, one piled with newspapers, one pushed all the way in.

Wayne makes coffee without asking if she wants any. He sets it in front of her in a Hollow Ridge Feed & Seed mug.

The player can look around while he does it: the pantry with its shelf of canned goods and a Maxwell House can at the end; the kitchen radio (AM, the farm report); and, down the hall, a closed door with a small horse sticker on it at the height of a six-year-old.

### What he knows

The conversation is Riley's to open, and the player chooses how. However she starts, Wayne gets there first.

**RILEY:** He sees someone.

**WAYNE:** I know.

**RILEY:** Since when do you—

**WAYNE:** August. In that funeral home in Tannersville. He told me what she looks like. With y'all standing right there.

**RILEY:** I was there.

**WAYNE:** Then you heard it.

**RILEY:** I didn't know what it meant.

Wayne drinks his coffee. He looks at the window over the sink.

**WAYNE:** Clara was my mama's name.

Riley doesn't move.

**WAYNE:** Grace sang the high part. On my records. Her and him, on the Louvin Brothers, in the front room. Acted like they hated it.

**RILEY:** He says she hums.

**WAYNE:** Who does?

**RILEY:** Clara. He says she hums the high part.

Wayne sets his mug down. He gets up and goes to the sink and runs the water over nothing, over his hands, for a while. He turns it off.

**WAYNE:** There's a box.

**RILEY:** What box?

**WAYNE:** Not for you.

He doesn't say anything else about it.

### What she came for

She came for one thing.

**RILEY:** He needs to see a doctor.

**WAYNE:** He won't go.

**RILEY:** He'll go if you take him.

**WAYNE:** What makes you think that?

Riley has an argument, because Riley always has an argument. The player chooses the form:

- **"Name one time you asked him for something and he said no."** Wayne thinks about it. *"...Rent."* A pause. *"He pays it."*
- **"You're the only one he'll let drive."** Wayne looks at her like she's said something in another language.
- **"Because I can't."** The plain one. Wayne looks at her for a long time.

Whichever she says, Wayne doesn't agree. He asks a question instead.

**WAYNE:** Is he sleeping?

**RILEY:** No.

**WAYNE:** Hm.

A long pause.

**WAYNE:** Raymond didn't sleep.

Riley knows who Raymond is. Ellis told her in a pasture in July.

Tater comes in from the porch and puts his head on Riley's knee. The player can scratch his ears. Wayne watches that.

### The door, going

At the door, she has her keys out. Wayne stands on the porch in his socks.

**WAYNE:** Drive careful. It's gonna rain.

It isn't going to rain. The sky is flat and dry. He says it every time.

She drives off. In the mirror he's still on the porch, and Tater, and the closed door behind them.

> **Design note.** Wayne has known for seven months and done nothing with it but drive the roads at night. It takes a girl from Linwood at his kitchen table to make him say his mother's name out loud. Two days later he goes to his closet.

## MISSION 13 — THE BOX

**Playable:** Ellis · **Where:** the Blake kitchen · **When:** Saturday March 20, 6:10 – 6:40 p.m. · **Length:** ~15 min

### Home

Ellis comes home from Tannersville on Saturday evening in the Valiant. The kitchen light is on. Wayne's at the table, still in his hat, which he never wears in the house.

In front of him on the oilcloth is a Buster Brown shoebox, the lid beside it.

Clara is with Ellis coming up the porch steps.

**CLARA:** Wipe your feet.

He wipes them. She stops in the doorway between the kitchen and the hall, with her hands in her jacket pockets, and doesn't come in.

**WAYNE:** Sit down.

Ellis sits down: father and son at the same table, facing each other, at suppertime, with no supper.

### Two photographs

Wayne takes two photographs out of the box and puts them side by side on the oilcloth, facing Ellis, squared, the way Richard Holloway squares things.

**WAYNE:** Look.

The player looks. This is the whole mission: looking. The camera gives the player Ellis's eyes and nothing to do but move them.

**The first photograph** is a school picture, the kind with the mottled blue background, fall 1972. Hollow Ridge Middle School. A thirteen-year-old girl in a blue blouse with a Peter Pan collar, bangs she obviously cut herself, grinning with her mouth open because she refused to smile the way the photographer asked. A gap between her front teeth. The gray-green Blake eyes. The player has seen this face before, younger: at five in the 1964 portrait, at twelve on the mare in the snapshot. *Grace*, in green felt-tip, on the white border.

**The second photograph** is a wedding portrait, black-and-white, June 1953, on the steps of the Hollow Ridge Methodist Church. A girl of nineteen in a borrowed satin dress, laughing at something off-camera, her dark hair pinned up, her cheekbones high and sharp. She's laughing with her mouth open. A gap between her front teeth. The man beside her, twenty-four, with his hair slicked and his tie crooked, is looking at her instead of the camera. It's Wayne.

*Lorraine*, in pencil, on the back. (The player can turn it over.)

The player can look from one to the other. No timer. Grace's eyes. Lorraine's cheekbones. The same gap, in both. The dark hair.

And the player can look up, at the doorway.

Clara is standing there. Twenty-one. Dark hair to her shoulders. Lorraine's cheekbones. Grace's eyes. The gap in her teeth. Grace's jacket, the horse patch sewn on crooked.

She is exactly the face between the two photographs. She's the girl in the school picture, grown up into the girl on the church steps. She's the eight years that never happened.

> **Design note — the reveal.** This tells the player what Clara is made of, and their own eyes have to put it together: two photographs, a doorway, and time. The player who guessed early that she's "his dead sister's ghost" guessed the tabloid version. What the player has just put together is the older sister Grace never got to be, with their mother's face.

### "There is no Clara"

**WAYNE:** There is no Clara.

**ELLIS:** She's in the doorway.

Wayne doesn't turn around. He doesn't look. His hands are flat on the oilcloth on either side of the photographs.

**WAYNE:** Son.

He says it the way he says it only when he's frightened.

Ellis looks at the doorway.

**ELLIS:** Is that you?

He's asking Clara. Wayne thinks he's being asked.

**WAYNE:** What?

Clara looks at the two photographs on the table for a long time. Then she looks at Ellis.

She doesn't answer.

Then, between one look and the next, the doorway is empty.

**WAYNE:** Your grandmama's name. Grace's middle name.

**ELLIS:** I know her name.

**WAYNE:** Do you.

Ellis stands up. The chair scrapes.

**WAYNE:** Where you going?

Ellis doesn't answer. He takes the Valiant keys off the hook by the door.

Wayne half-rises. He doesn't follow. At the kitchen door, as Ellis goes down the porch steps into the dark:

**WAYNE:** It's gonna rain.

It isn't.

## MISSION 14 — TOLLIVER ROAD

**Playable:** Ellis · **Where:** Cold Branch Road; the South Fork road; Tolliver Road; Tolliver Bend · **When:** Saturday March 20, 6:50 – 8:40 p.m. · **Length:** ~20 min

### The turn

The player drives. No destination marker. Down Cold Branch Road, past Marlon's (Saturday night, the chalkboard lit), across the tracks at Depot Street, and south onto the South Fork road, the way he's driven a hundred times, with one road always on the left that he never takes.

Tolliver Road.

It runs on past the farm and the Bend and comes out on the Tanner Valley road: the back way home. The way you go when it's raining and the low-water bridge is under.

In Chapter I a county sawhorse leaned across the mouth of it (ROAD WORK — LOCAL TRAFFIC), and Clara in the passenger seat said *Not that way*, and he didn't go. The sawhorse has been gone since the fall of 1974. The refusal was always his.

Tonight Clara is in the passenger seat with her boots on the dash, and as the turn comes up she says it again.

**CLARA:** Not that way.

The player can drive past. If they do, the South Fork road goes on to the farmhouse and the low-water bridge and nothing happens; Ellis turns around in a church lot and comes back, and the turn is still there. The mission waits.

He turns anyway. The Valiant's headlights swing across a green county sign, TOLLIVER RD, and the road narrows to a strip of old asphalt with no center line, between fences and dark pasture, going south and down toward the South Fork.

### Who's in the car

There's someone else in the passenger seat.

A girl of fourteen, in a jean jacket that fits her, a child's size, with a horse sewn on the pocket, crooked. Muddy riding boots. A ponytail. She smells like a horse. Ellis rolls his window down an inch without thinking about it.

Grace.

She's looking out the window at the pastures. She looks like a fourteen-year-old who's been riding all afternoon and is annoyed about something.

**GRACE:** You drive like an old man.

The player can answer or not. The dialogue options are Ellis at sixteen: *"Speed limit's forty-five."* / *"You want to walk?"* / silence.

**GRACE:** Dolly threw a shoe. Mr. Tolliver said he'd get it Monday.

**GRACE:** You're gonna be late.

She says these things the way she would have said them, on a Thursday, three years ago, in this car's older brother. The player drives. The Tolliver farm is two miles ahead.

### Older

Every time the player looks at the passenger seat (and they can, freely, with the camera), she's older.

- At the first bend, fifteen. Her hair longer.
- Past the church, sixteen or seventeen. The jacket is bigger now, a grown-up jacket, the same one.
- At the Tolliver farm (lights on in the farmhouse, Floyd's green truck in the yard, the empty paddock where Dolly stood for twenty years), nineteen.

And somewhere between the farm and the bend, twenty-one. Clara. Boots up on the dash.

The player can't catch the change happening. It's always already happened when they look.

Somewhere between Grace and Clara, looking straight ahead through the windshield, she says one more thing, in Grace's voice:

**GRACE/CLARA:** Daddy'll kill us both.

*Daddy.* The word comes out of her like something she didn't mean to say, the way Grace would have said it, and she doesn't take it back.

> **Replay layer.** "Daddy would kill us both" is what Ellis said to Grace in the car on April 12, 1973, when she asked to come to Marlon's. He doesn't remember saying it. Chapter IX gives the player the whole argument.

Past the farm, on the right, a mailbox on a cedar post: HENSLEY. A frame house set back in the dark with one porch light on.

### The bend

Two miles past the farm the road starts to curve right, down toward the creek, blind, with a bank of red clay on the right and no shoulder at all: the asphalt just stops and the clay starts and then the ditch. It's in the steering before it's in the headlights.

Tolliver Bend.

The Valiant's headlights come around, and there it is, on the outside of the curve: a white oak, big, old, with a long scar on the trunk at the height of a car door, where the bark has grown back over it crooked, thick and folded, like a badly healed arm.

The player can stop anywhere: in the road (no one's coming) or half onto the clay. Ellis stops. The engine ticks. The headlights stay on the oak.

He gets out. The player can walk. Clara gets out of the passenger side, the side that was nearest the tree.

The player can walk to the oak and put Ellis's hand on the scar. The prompt is *Touch.* The bark is rough, and then, on the scar, smooth and ridged, like a knuckle.

### "I made you"

Clara is standing a few feet away, in the headlights, with her hands in her jacket pockets.

**ELLIS:** I made you.

She looks at him.

**CLARA:** Does that make me less real?

**ELLIS:** I don't know.

**CLARA:** Well. Holler when you know.

He doesn't have an answer.

**ELLIS:** You've got her eyes. You've got Mama's teeth.

**CLARA:** I've got your shirt.

She plucks at the red flannel under the jacket, his shirt.

**ELLIS:** Why'd you come?

**CLARA:** You asked me to.

**ELLIS:** When?

Clara looks at the oak. At the scar at the height of a car door.

**CLARA:** Right here.

Ellis sits down on the clay with his back against the oak. No timer. Clara sits down beside him, not touching him (she never touches anything).

**CLARA:** Button your jacket.

He buttons it. No cars come. The creek at the bottom of the hill. Frogs, early, it being March. The Valiant's headlights on the two of them and the tree.

The player can Observe here, once. The line is: *The bark grew back over it like a hand over a mouth.*

When the player gets up, Ellis gets up. He doesn't drive home. He drives back up Tolliver Road the way he came, and Clara rides with him, and she doesn't get any younger.

At the bottom of Tolliver Road, where it meets the South Fork road, there's a closed store with a pay phone on the wall outside under a light.

He stops.

### The bend

The player can have Riley ask about the road. If she does, he tells her, plainly, all of it: the sawhorse gone; the girl in the passenger seat who got older every time he looked; the Hensley mailbox; the curve with no shoulder; the oak.

**ELLIS:** It's got a scar on it. Where the car hit. The bark grew back over it all crooked.

## MISSION 17 — DR. LUSK

**Playable:** Ellis · **Where:** US 19; the Department of Psychiatry outpatient clinic, the university medical district, Laurel City; the Starlite · **When:** Thursday April 8, 8:30 a.m. – 2:30 p.m. · **Length:** ~25 min

### The appointment

Riley made the call. It took two and a half weeks to get in, which Dr. Lusk's receptionist said was fast.

Thursday. Wayne drives the F-100 west on US 19 through the Laurel Gap into Laurel City, with Ellis in the passenger seat and the FM converter under the dash set to (if the player moved it in Chapter VII) 97.1, or (if they didn't) 88.9, and off. Neither of them mentions it.

She's forty-four, in a gray suit, with reading glasses pushed up in her hair, and she's careful in the way people are careful when they've seen what careless does.

No timer. If the player holds silence, Dr. Lusk doesn't fill it. She writes something, or doesn't, and waits.

The player chooses the topics, in any order, as they open: *sleep; the tour; the television; Clara; Grace; Raymond; the photographs; Milledgeville.*

A few of the things that are said:

**LUSK:** How are you sleeping?

**ELLIS:** I'm not, much.

**LUSK:** Since when?

It's 1976, and she means it.

### What it is

Near the end, the question he came in with. The player can ask it, or wait, and if they wait she brings it up herself.

**ELLIS:** What is it?

**LUSK:** I don't know yet.

He looks at her.

**LUSK:** Grief can do this. A blow to the head can do it; you had one. Not sleeping can do it. So can some things that run in families. I'm not going to name it today. A name is hard to take back.

> **Design note.** The game never names what Clara is, and Dr. Lusk is the reason it doesn't have to. A good psychiatrist in 1976 declines to put a word on a nineteen-year-old that he'd have to carry.

### The prescription

**LUSK:** Trifluoperazine. Two milligrams, twice a day. It'll make things quieter. It may make her quieter. It may not. It'll also make you slow, and sleepy, and your mouth will be dry, and you might feel stiff or restless, like you can't sit still in your own legs. This other one is for that.

**LUSK:** I don't know. Maybe.

**ELLIS:** I don't want her to.

**LUSK:** I know. That's one of the things we'll have to talk about. Every two weeks.

**ELLIS:** Can I still play?

**LUSK:** Probably. Slower at first. Tell me if you can't.

**LUSK:** Who drove you?

**ELLIS:** My daddy.

**LUSK:** Is he waiting?

**ELLIS:** In the truck.

She looks. The player can come to the window and look with her: the second level of the deck, the F-100, and a man in a hat behind the wheel with the window rolled down an inch, not reading the paper on the seat beside him, just sitting, facing the building.

**LUSK:** Two hours?

**ELLIS:** He's got a radio.

**LUSK:** I'd like to meet him. Not today.

## MISSION 18 — QUIET

**Playable:** Ellis · **Where:** the Blake house; Hollow Ridge in spring · **When:** Monday April 12; Saturday April 24 – Friday April 30 · **Length:** ~20 min

### April 12

The HUD shows the date, the way it does on the days somebody will say the date out loud: MONDAY · APRIL 12.

Four days on the medication. He moves a little slower. The camera follows him a beat late, the way a heavy man turns. When he stands up he stands up in two stages. His hands are steady on things, heavy on them. The world is the same colors it always was. Only Ellis is slower inside it.

7:10 a.m. A truck in the yard: Floyd Tolliver's green Ford with the stock rack. Floyd doesn't get out. He sets something on the porch rail and lifts one hand at the window and drives off toward the South Fork.

A Pyrex dish covered in foil, with a card taped to it in shaky ballpoint:

> *Thinking of you all today. — Ada T.*

The buttermilk pie. Every April 12 nobody eats it, and Tater gets it.

Ellis carries it in and sets it on the kitchen table. The player can do what's always done: put it on the counter for later, which means for Tater. Or there's a new prompt: *Cut a piece.*

If the player cuts a piece, Ellis gets a knife and cuts a wedge and eats it standing at the counter with his fingers.

Wayne comes in from the back porch. He sees it. He stops.

Wayne looks at the pie, and at his son eating it, and goes to the drawer and gets a fork. He sits down at the table. He eats a piece out of the dish without cutting it, straight out of the Pyrex, with the fork. Ellis sits down across from him and gets a fork too.

They eat Ada Tolliver's pie out of the same dish on the third anniversary of Grace's death. Tater, on the floor, watches every forkful, and gets a piece anyway.

**WAYNE:** Ada makes it too sweet.

**ELLIS:** Grace liked it.

**WAYNE:** Grace liked anything with sugar in it.

Ellis laughs. It comes out slow, medicated, a half-second late, and real. Wayne doesn't laugh, but he takes another bite.

> **Design note.** The medication takes Clara. It also gives him this: a morning where he can sit at a kitchen table with his father on April 12 and eat a pie.

If the player doesn't cut a piece, the pie goes on the counter, and Tater gets it, and Wayne comes in and sees the empty dish on the floor, and says "Hm," and washes it.

### Late April

The last week of April. Hollow Ridge in spring: the dogwoods out on the Methodist hill, redbuds along Cold Branch, the creek high and brown and loud at the falls. The medicated free-roam is open. The player can go anywhere Ellis has ever gone.

What's different:
- **Clara is gone.** She doesn't appear anywhere, at any time of day: not the alley behind Marlon's, not the porch, not Stony Knob. The player can look for her anywhere.
- **The quiet.** Nobody points at anything or tells him what day it is, and when he plays, nobody hums. The HUD shows the day of the week, and that's all the help there is.
- **Observe is muted.** The cue comes rarely, and the lines Ellis writes are short and flat: *Dogwood. White.* / *Creek high.* / *Tater asleep.* They go in the notebook with the others.
- **He sleeps.** The first night of free-roam, the player can put him to bed at ten, and the night fast-forwards, the window going from black to gray to gold, and he wakes at nine in the morning with the sun on the bed.
- **The world is the same.** Same colors, same sounds, same people. Marlon waves from the door. Roy has the Valiant's timing adjusted without being asked. Mrs. Odom at the library slides a book across the desk without comment: a new one, *The Complete Poems* of Elizabeth Bishop.

### Saturday

Saturday, April 24. Riley drives out with a bag of groceries and makes pancakes in the Blake kitchen, the only thing she can cook, and burns the first four.

Ellis is on the porch with Tater. She calls through the screen door to ask if Tater's allowed pancakes.

**ELLIS:** He's not allowed anything. He gets everything.

**RILEY:** He's enormous.

**ELLIS:** He's not fat. He's load-bearing.

From inside the kitchen, Riley laughs. A real laugh, the whole thing. It comes out through the screen door onto the porch.

Ellis, on the porch, hears it. He doesn't turn around. He just sits there with it.

Dean calls that afternoon on the kitchen phone to say Frank Dalton and Eddie have found a place to make the second album: a lodge on Stony Knob, above the overlook, that some Laurel City lawyer built in 1958 and never uses. They'll move in in May. "You can see your house from the dock, Blake. Probably. If you squint."

### The porch

Friday, April 30. Dusk.

The porch. Ellis in the chair with his boots on the rail. Tater asleep on his left boot, all sixty pounds of him. Inside, Wayne's kitchen radio: the Engineers, the second week of the Double-A season, a game in Chattanooga, turned up.

On Ellis's knee, memo book 69, open to a clean page, and a pencil. Beside him on the porch boards, the green leather notebook Riley gave him on his birthday, still blank.

The player can do anything. Observe (the cue comes once: *Porch. Dusk.*). Look at the yard. Look at the road. Look at the end of the porch where Clara used to sit on the rail with her boots swinging.

She doesn't come.

The player can wait. No timer: a real-time dusk going to dark, the porch bulb coming on, the Engineers going into the seventh inning, Tater snoring.

The pencil doesn't move.

After a while, Wayne comes to the screen door behind him, and stands there, and doesn't say anything, and goes back in.

Fade out on the porch bulb.

## Chapter VIII — design summary

### What happened
A label wrote down that only one of the Blakes counts. Riley gave her mother's song to Ellis for the radio; later she stopped using and drove to Wayne, both on her own.
