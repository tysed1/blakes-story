# CHAPTER VII — STRANGERS KNOW YOUR NAME

> **Hollow Ridge · Laurel City · Tannersville · the Southeast · New York City · Monday, September 8 – Saturday, December 13, 1975**
> Main-story runtime: ~7 hours · Playable: Ellis Blake, Riley, Cal Mercer, Dean Holloway, in rotation

## Chapter thesis

Everyone loses ownership of something. The record goes out into the world and belongs to whoever buys it. A song made from a private conversation becomes something sixteen hundred strangers sing. A magazine decides who Ellis Blake is and prints the worst day of his life as a biography. Riley's song is credited to Ellis. Cal's sentence about the band is used to prove it's Ellis's band. Dean's father takes back the car, the allowance and the pretense. By December the four of them are famous in a way that has nothing to do with what they are to each other, and the first thing they break is a promise they made in a van.

The player spends most of this chapter enjoying it. That's the point. Fame in this game has to be fun before it costs anything, or the cost means nothing.

## World state at open

- *Borrowed Stone* is pressed and a week from release. Southern Star has ordered 5,000 copies, which Harold Vance calls "optimistic" and his boss calls "the tax shelter."
- The Blakes are known in Laurel City, a curiosity in Tannersville, and a subject of opinion in Hollow Ridge.
- Ellis still works at Roy's four days a week. Riley has moved out of the dorm into a second-floor apartment on Cutler Street, near the college, with Gerald and a mattress on the floor. Cal lives above the dry cleaner. Dean lives at home in Belle Grove, allegedly.
- Autumn 1975: Ford survives two assassination attempts in September. The Reds and the Red Sox in October. "Fame" and "Rhinestone Cowboy" on the radio; *Wish You Were Here* in college dorms; CB radio in every truck at the Starlite; *One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest* opens in November.

## COLD OPEN

The Bowery, New York City. A Saturday afternoon in December. The light is going. It has just started to snow, the dry kind that doesn't stick.

A brick wall on the side of a closed restaurant-supply store, layered with torn posters: a boxing card, a band nobody's heard of yet, a Bicentennial ad already peeling. Four people stand against it in a row, and a man with two cameras around his neck is walking backward into the street to get them all in.

They're cold. Riley has her father's Navy peacoat buttoned to the throat and her glasses on. Cal is in a borrowed overcoat that's too short in the sleeves, over a pressed shirt. Dean is in a satin baseball jacket over a Hawaiian shirt, bouncing on his toes. Ellis is in a black leather jacket that still smells like somebody else.

**PHOTOGRAPHER** *(Denny Ostrow, Rave's staff photographer, forties, a Bronx accent and no patience)*: Closer. Closer. You're a band, not a bus stop.

They shuffle together.

**PHOTOGRAPHER:** Don't smile.

Dean smiles.

**PHOTOGRAPHER:** The drummer can smile. Nobody else.

**DEAN:** Thank you.

**PHOTOGRAPHER:** It wasn't a compliment.

A cab honks. Somebody on the sidewalk says *Is that anybody?* and somebody else says *No.*

The player is Ellis, and the only input the game offers is the camera: where Ellis looks. The lens. The snow. Riley. The street.

Across the Bowery, by a newsstand with its shutters half down, someone in a faded jean jacket is standing in the snow without a coat, watching.

**PHOTOGRAPHER:** Hold it.

Before the shutter fires, the frame freezes and drains to black.

**STRANGERS KNOW YOUR NAME**

> **Design note.** The cold open is the end of the chapter, played three months early and interrupted. The player doesn't yet know why Ellis has a leather jacket, what's in its inside pocket, or why the man is taking the picture. At the end of Mission 19 the scene is played again, all the way through, and the player's choice of where Ellis looks becomes one of the most reproduced photographs of the decade.

## MISSION 1 — NINETEEN

**Playable:** Ellis · **Where:** the Blake house; Marlon's Tavern and the alley behind it · **When:** Monday September 8 – Sunday September 14 · **Length:** ~35 min

### The box

Monday, late afternoon. Ellis comes home from Roy's in the Valiant with grease to the elbows. There's a cardboard carton on the kitchen table, next to the bills pile, addressed in marker to ELLIS BLAKE, COLD BRANCH RD, HOLLOW RIDGE GA, from Southern Star Records in Laurel City. The delivery slip is taped to the top. It's been signed for: *W. Blake*, in the square railroad hand the player has seen on the back of the coupon book.

Wayne is in his chair in the front room with the television on and the sound low.

**WAYNE:** Came for you.

**ELLIS:** I see that.

**WAYNE:** Man in a brown truck. Wanted me to sign.

**ELLIS:** You signed.

**WAYNE:** Hm.

That's all. Wayne doesn't ask what's in it. Ellis doesn't open it in the kitchen. He carries it down the hall to his room, past Grace's closed door, and sets it on the bed.

The player opens it. Ten copies of *Borrowed Stone*, shrink-wrapped, in a nest of shredded newspaper.

The cover is a black-and-white photograph: the 1962 Jazzmaster, no strap, leaning against an unpolished block of gray granite in a yard full of them, early morning, the fog not burned off yet. The title in plain white type along the top. THE BLAKES along the bottom. That's all.

The band thinks the photograph is a joke about the title. Vance's art man took it at seven in the morning in the yard of Hollow Ridge Monument & Vault, across from the cemetery gate, because Ellis said that was where it should be and wouldn't say why.

The back cover lists the songs, the four names, and the credits. The player can read all of it:

> Recorded at Dalton Sound, Tannersville, June–August 1975. Produced by Frank Dalton.
> Thanks: Marlon Pettit and everybody at Marlon's · Roy and Evelyn Cagle · Harlan Vale · Walt and Dot Mercer · Martin Keller and WTCR · Tully Voss · Theo Marchand · Eddie Farris · Mitch at Tenth Street · Rhonda at Dalton Sound, who answered the phone · Tater

And at the very bottom, in Ellis's handwriting, reproduced from the paste-up because he wrote it on the mechanical at the last minute and Vance let it go:

> *for G.*

The player can hold the record, turn it over, look at his own name in type. Loretta hangs on the wall above the bed. The plastic horse is on the dresser.

Then a prompt: Ellis can take one copy back down the hall.

If the player does it, Ellis sets it on the kitchen table, on top of the bills pile, on top of the coupon book, and goes to bed without a word. In the morning it's gone. Nobody mentions it. The player won't find out where it went until Chapter IX.

If the player doesn't, that's all right too. (See Mission 5.)

> **Replay layer.** The monument yard is where Wayne picked out Grace's stone in April 1973 and signed for forty-eight payments. "For G." is on the back of every copy of *Borrowed Stone* ever pressed. When the band asks (Mission 2), Ellis says it stands for Georgia.

### "Rehearsal"

Saturday night Dean calls the house. Wayne answers, says "Hm," and holds the receiver out at arm's length toward the hall without saying who it is.

**DEAN** *(on the phone)*: Rehearsal tomorrow. Marlon's. Two o'clock.

**ELLIS:** Marlon's is closed Sundays.

**DEAN:** Marlon says come in the back.

**ELLIS:** Why are we rehearsing on a Sunday?

**DEAN:** Because we're professionals now, Blake. Professionals rehearse on Sundays. It's in the contract.

**ELLIS:** It's not in the contract.

**DEAN:** Richard found it in the fine print. Two o'clock.

He hangs up. Ellis looks at the receiver.

### Sunday

Sunday, September 14, 1975. Ellis's nineteenth birthday. The HUD says SUNDAY and nothing else. Nobody in the Blake house mentions it. Wayne is gone when Ellis gets up; there's a biscuit on a plate under a paper towel on the stove, which is either for Ellis or for Tater, and has always been ambiguous.

The player can free-roam Hollow Ridge on a Sunday: the churches letting out, the Piggly Wiggly closed, the county's blue laws in effect (Marlon's, like every bar in the county, can't sell on Sunday). Someone has painted a line under the town-limit sign on US 19 overnight in white house paint, not very well:

> HOLLOW RIDGE · POP. 2,100
> *HOME OF THE BLAKES*

(Dean and Tully, at 3 a.m. Sunday. The county will paint over it on Wednesday. Somebody will repaint it by Saturday. This continues for the rest of the game as a piece of the open world; the sign's state on any given day depends on how recently the county truck came through.)

At two o'clock Ellis goes in the back door at Marlon's with his guitar.

### Surprise

Dark inside. Then the lights. About thirty people shout it.

Ellis stands in the doorway with the Jazzmaster case and looks at them: the band, Tully, Marlon, Roy and Evelyn, Wesley Tate and his mother, Patty Holloway, Opal Hensley at her corner of the bar on a Sunday, which she never does, three of Ellis's old classmates, and a hand-lettered banner across the stage that says HAPPY 19TH BLAKE in Dean's writing with the letters getting smaller toward the end because he ran out of banner.

Ellis turns around and walks back out the door.

The player can't stop him. It's the funniest moment in the chapter and the game lets it happen.

### Negotiation

The gravel lot behind Marlon's. Dean comes out after him at a run.

**DEAN:** Where are you going?

**ELLIS:** Home.

**DEAN:** It's your birthday.

**ELLIS:** I know when my birthday is. I told you I didn't want one.

**DEAN:** Everybody wants one.

**ELLIS:** I don't.

**DEAN:** Wesley Tate made a *shirt*, Blake.

That stops him.

The negotiation is a dialogue with a real system underneath it. Ellis names a number of minutes; Dean counters. The player can push hard or give in. Every version ends in a deal, and every deal has terms:

**ELLIS:** One hour.

**DEAN:** Three.

**ELLIS:** One.

**DEAN:** Two, and you have to eat cake.

**ELLIS:** One hour, I eat cake, and I don't make a speech.

**DEAN:** Deal.

They shake. Dean doesn't let go.

**DEAN:** Happy birthday.

**ELLIS:** Let go of my hand.

### The party

One hour of free movement inside Marlon's, with a clock the player can see on the wall. The party is a small social sandbox. Everyone has something.

- **Marlon** is behind the bar giving away beer, which is legal on a Sunday as long as nobody pays for it. "I'm not selling it," he tells everyone who tries to pay. There's a coffee can on the bar that says TIPS in Dean's writing, which Marlon ignores.
- **The cake** is from the Piggly Wiggly bakery, white sheet cake with blue roses, HAPPY BIRTHDAY ELIS in icing. Donna Kay Carson (Sisk that was, Ellis's high-school girlfriend, now married and on the register) took the order. *"She knows how to spell it."* / *"Allegedly."* Ellis eats a piece, as agreed. Dean takes an SX-70 of him eating it with an expression of great suffering.
- **Wesley Tate**, twelve now, in a white T-shirt with THE BLAKES ironed across the chest in black felt letters. The second E is drawn on in marker because the Western Auto ran out of E's. He doesn't say anything. He just stands near Ellis. If the player talks to him, Ellis asks where he got the shirt; Wesley says he made it; Ellis asks why only one E; Wesley explains about the Western Auto; Ellis says it's the best shirt he's ever seen, and means it, and Wesley goes red to the ears.
- **Patty Holloway**, sixteen, sarcastic, standing by the *Fireball* machine pretending not to want to play. If Ellis goes to her, he shows her the kickback trick on the left outlane, and they play three balls, and she beats him on the third, and he demands a rematch with complete seriousness. Dean, across the room, watches this with a confused look he doesn't have a name for. (Ellis is gentle with Patty. Patty is the age Grace would be. Dean won't understand why for a long time.)
- **Roy and Evelyn**. Evelyn gives him a lunch sack: a fried bologna sandwich and a card with a ten-dollar bill in it. Roy says, "Don't try to give it back." Ellis tries to give it back. Roy says, "Go on."
- **Cal** gives him a twenty-foot instrument cable he built himself, with a strip of white tape on each end: ELLIS · 20 FT · DO NOT LEND TO DEAN.
- **Tully** gives him nothing. "I'm here," he says. "That's the present." It's the right present.
- **Opal Hensley** is at her corner with a Coca-Cola. She lifts it an inch when he looks at her. That's all.
- **Deputy Lyle Pruitt** comes in the front door in uniform at the forty-minute mark. The room goes quiet. He looks at the free beer on the bar. He looks at Marlon. Marlon looks back. "Happy birthday, Ellis," Pruitt says, takes a piece of cake on a napkin, and leaves. The room starts up again.

### Riley

At some point before the hour is up, Riley finds him by the back hallway where it's quieter and hands him a package in brown paper.

Inside: a notebook bound in dark green leather, the pages sewn, from a stationer on the Row, nothing like the Western Auto memo books he buys three for a dollar. Heavy. It smells like a shoe store.

**ELLIS:** What is this?

**RILEY:** It's a notebook.

**ELLIS:** It's a nice notebook.

**RILEY:** You can say thank you.

**ELLIS:** Thank you. It's too nice.

**RILEY:** It's paper, Ellis.

**ELLIS:** I'll mess it up.

**RILEY:** That's what it's for.

He turns it over. There's a place on the front cover where initials could have been stamped. They weren't.

**RILEY:** I almost had them put your initials on it. Then I thought you'd hate that.

**ELLIS:** I'd have hated that.

**RILEY:** I know.

He doesn't open it. He puts it inside his jacket, against his chest, and it's too big for the pocket and he leaves it there anyway.

> **Setup.** Ellis doesn't write in the green notebook for nine months. He keeps using the numbered Western Auto memo books. In Chapter IX he starts writing in the green one, and the poems in it are addressed to "M." Riley finds it after he's gone.

### The speech

At fifty-nine minutes Dean climbs on a chair and clinks a beer bottle with a pen.

**DEAN:** Speech!

**ELLIS:** We had a deal.

**DEAN:** The deal was one hour. It's been fifty-nine minutes. The deal expires in one minute, at which point you're a free man and I'm a liar.

The room chants. The player can refuse, and Ellis walks out the back door to applause. Or the player can let him stand up.

If he does, he stands on the little plywood stage where he played every Thursday for six months, looks at the thirty people in Marlon's on a Sunday, and says:

**ELLIS:** Thank y'all for coming to this thing I didn't want.

Laughter.

**ELLIS:** Donna Kay can't spell.

More laughter, and Donna Kay throws a napkin at him.

He stands there a second longer than he means to. The player can see him look at the chalkboard by the door (THURSDAY, FRIDAY, the week's list, and at the top in Marlon's hand, *SUNDAY — CLOSED*) and at Wesley's shirt, and at Roy, and at Riley.

**ELLIS:** Okay.

He gets down.

### The alley

Nine o'clock. The party's over. The lot's empty except for the Valiant. Ellis goes out the back into the alley with a last piece of cake on a napkin, and Clara is sitting on the crate by the rail line, the same crate, with her feet up on a cable spool.

**CLARA:** You walked out of your own party.

**ELLIS:** I walked back in.

**CLARA:** Dean had to drag you.

**ELLIS:** Dean negotiated.

She holds out her hand. He gives her the cake. She doesn't eat it; she holds it on her knee on its napkin. (She never eats anything. The player may or may not have noticed that yet.)

**CLARA:** Nineteen.

**ELLIS:** Yep.

**CLARA:** You look the same.

**ELLIS:** When's yours?

She looks at him.

**CLARA:** My what?

**ELLIS:** Birthday. You never said.

**CLARA:** I don't have one.

**ELLIS:** Everybody has one.

**CLARA:** Pick one, then.

He thinks about it. The player can't choose for him. He says it as if it's the first month that came into his head:

**ELLIS:** November.

**CLARA:** November's fine.

A freight comes through on the main line thirty feet away, and they stop talking and watch it the way they always do, and when it's gone the napkin on her knee is empty and the cake is on the crate beside her where Ellis must have set it down.

He walks to the car. He has the green notebook inside his jacket. He drives home the long way.

> **Replay layer.** Grace Clara Blake was born November 2, 1958. The player was in the hallway outside her room on November 2, 1974, the last night of Chapter II, when she would have turned sixteen. In Chapter IX Ellis writes "New Skin": *I gave you a birthday every year you didn't get one.*

## MISSION 2 — RELEASE DAY

**Playable:** Dean → Riley → Cal → Ellis · **Where:** Tenth Street Records, Laurel City · **When:** Monday September 15, 10 a.m. – 6 p.m. · **Length:** ~25 min

### The plan

It was Dean's idea: go to Tenth Street on release day and watch somebody buy it. Cal said that was insane. Riley said she'd go if Cal went. Cal said he'd go to make sure Dean didn't do anything insane. Ellis said he had to work, and Roy said, "It's your record, go on," and gave him the day.

Mitch has twenty-five copies of *Borrowed Stone* in the NEW bin by the register and has taped a copy of the 45's hand-stamped sleeve to the front window with a card that says LOCAL · HEARD 'EM FIRST.

**MITCH:** You people can't stand in my store all day.

**DEAN:** We'll take shifts.

That's the mission's structure. Four two-hour shifts. At each shift change, one of them comes in the door as the other leaves, and the switch passes between them at the threshold. The store is small, the day is long, and nobody buys the record for a very long time.

### Dean (10:00 – 12:00)

Dean re-files *Borrowed Stone* in front of *Fleetwood Mac* in the rock section, then in front of Linda Ronstadt, then in the front-window display on top of the Eagles. Mitch moves it back each time without comment.

The player, as Dean, can talk to every customer who comes in. Dean is very good at this, and most of these conversations go nowhere. A man looking for Steely Dan. Two girls from the university who want to know if he's anybody. ("I'm the drummer." / "Of what?" / "That's a great question.") A kid who asks if they have *Born to Run* (it came out three weeks ago; they're sold out). Nobody picks up *Borrowed Stone*. A man flips past it without stopping.

**DEAN** *(to Mitch)*: He didn't even look.

**MITCH:** He was looking for Jethro Tull.

**DEAN:** Our cover's better.

### Riley (12:00 – 2:00)

Riley in the classical bins in the back, where nobody under forty goes. She finds an organ record, Bach chorale preludes played on a church organ in Leipzig, and stands there holding it. The player can buy it (it costs $3.49, and she buys it for her mother) or put it back.

A woman with a toddler asks Riley if she works here. Riley says no, then yes, then helps her find a Sesame Street record. Mitch watches this and says nothing.

Nobody buys *Borrowed Stone*. Two people pick it up. One reads the back cover all the way through and puts it down. Riley watches her read it with her heart going.

**RILEY** *(to Mitch, when the woman leaves)*: She read the whole back.

**MITCH:** She liked the picture.

**RILEY:** Maybe.

### Cal (2:00 – 4:00)

Cal fixes the listening-station headphones, which have crackled in the left ear since 1972. He does it on the counter with a pocketknife and electrical tape while Mitch watches with his arms folded.

**MITCH:** That's been broke three years.

**CAL:** Your cord's shot at the strain relief.

**MITCH:** I don't know what that means.

**CAL:** It means it's been broke three years.

He also counts the stock, without being asked, and notes it in the ledger: *9/15/75 — Tenth St., 25 on hand at open.* The player can see him look at the NEW bin every time the door opens and pretend he isn't.

Nobody buys it.

### Ellis (4:00 – 6:00)

The light's going orange on Tenth Street. The head shops putting their incense out on the sidewalk. The player is Ellis, in a record store, waiting for a stranger to want something he made. He can look through bins (Mitch's used country section has a Louvin Brothers record he can pick up and put down), play the *Borrowed Stone* copy on the listening station if the store's empty (it isn't, and he won't), or stand by the window.

At 5:40 a woman comes in wearing a white nurse's uniform under a raincoat. Late thirties, tired, a pocketbook on her arm. She goes straight to the NEW bin like she knows where it is, takes *Borrowed Stone* out without looking at anything else, and carries it to the register.

Ellis stops breathing. The band (who have all come back and are hiding behind the soul section, badly) stop breathing.

**MITCH:** Four ninety-eight.

She pays. Mitch bags it.

Dean can't stand it. He comes out from behind the bin.

**DEAN:** Excuse me, ma'am. Can I ask why you bought that one?

**NURSE:** My son asked for it.

**DEAN:** Where'd he hear it?

**NURSE:** College station. He's in the hospital with his leg. He broke it on a motorbike, he's fine, he's driving me up the wall. He had me write it down.

She shows them the scrap of paper in her pocketbook: *BLAKES — BORROWED STONE — ALBUM NOT THE 45*.

Then she looks at the four of them, and at Ellis, and at the cover in the bag.

**NURSE:** Oh. You're them.

**ELLIS:** Allegedly.

**NURSE:** Would you sign it for him? He'll die.

Ellis signs it on the counter with Cal's Parker Jotter. The player chooses what he writes, from a few versions. The default is: *To Marcus · Stay off the motorbike · Ellis Blake.* Then the others sign it too, Dean with a drawing of a drum, Riley small in the corner, Cal his full name, precisely.

**NURSE:** Is it any good?

**DEAN:** It's the best record ever made.

**ELLIS:** Some of it.

She laughs and leaves. The bell over the door rings.

Nobody says anything for a while.

**CAL:** Twenty-four on hand.

### G

In the van on the way back to Tannersville, Dean has one of the copies open on his knees and is reading the back cover for the fortieth time.

**DEAN:** "For G." Who's G?

**ELLIS:** Georgia.

**DEAN:** You dedicated the record to the state of Georgia.

**ELLIS:** It's a good state.

**DEAN:** That's the most sentimental thing you've ever done.

**ELLIS:** Don't tell anybody.

Riley, in the passenger seat, doesn't turn around. The player can't see her face.

> **Knowledge.** All four of them raised a glass to Grace at Marlon's in May (Chapter V). Dean knows exactly who G is. He asked so Ellis could say "Georgia," and he let him have it. Nobody else in the van says anything.

## MISSION 3 — REGISTRATION

**Playable:** Riley · **Where:** Tannersville College; the Riley house, Linwood · **When:** Friday September 19; Sunday September 21 · **Length:** ~30 min

### The form

Friday, the last day of late registration. Riley's Datsun in the college lot. On the passenger seat: a registration form, a course catalogue with pages dog-eared, and a mimeographed itinerary from Eddie Farris with fifteen fall dates on it, most of them Thursdays through Sundays, three of them in other states.

She fills out the form in the car. The player fills it out with her: a real interaction, choosing courses from the catalogue, checking times against each other, writing in the section numbers. The form wants twelve to eighteen hours.

- PHIL 412 · Mind and Self II · Landry · TTh 10:00
- MUS 302 · Counterpoint · MWF 9:00
- GER 201 · Intermediate German · MWF 11:00 (for reading, she tells herself)
- ASTR 110 · The Solar System · Tu 7:00 p.m. (the constellations; she's always wanted to)

The player can pick others. Whatever they pick, they check it against Eddie's itinerary, and whatever they pick, it doesn't fit. Thursday nights in Macon. A Friday in Columbia. The week of November 10 in Virginia.

She fills it out anyway, all of it, in pen, and signs it: *Margaret A. Riley.*

### The gym

Registration is in the gym. Tables by alphabet under hand-lettered signs, lines snaking between the bleachers, a folding table with coffee and powdered doughnuts, a student worker with a bullhorn who doesn't need one. The player walks Riley through it. People she knows. A girl from her hall who says "Aren't you supposed to be famous?" A boy from Landry's seminar who says he heard they're on the radio in Laurel City. Hannah, across the gym, in a line of education majors, with new friends. Hannah waves. Riley waves. They don't cross the gym.

P through S. The line. Riley in it with the form.

It moves. The player can let it move. At the front, a woman with reading glasses on a chain holds out her hand for the form.

There's one prompt.

Riley folds the form in half, then in half again, and puts it in the back pocket of her jeans.

**WOMAN:** Honey?

**RILEY:** Sorry. I'm in the wrong line.

She steps out of it and walks out of the gym into the September sun.

> **Design note — why there's no choice.** Riley made this decision on the drive over, and the player filled out the form so they could feel what she was leaving: the particular courses, the particular times, a seminar with her name almost on it. The form goes in her pocket and stays in her things. In the epilogue it's in a shoebox with the notebooks, still folded in quarters, the courses in her handwriting.

### Landry (optional)

On the way to the lot she passes the philosophy building. The player can go in.

His door is open. He's on the phone, feet on the desk, pipe unlit. He holds up one finger: *one minute.*

The player can wait or leave. If she waits, he finishes the call (something about a missing grade), hangs up, and looks at her.

**LANDRY:** Miss Riley.

**RILEY:** I'm not registering.

**LANDRY:** I noticed. There's a boy from Macon who's been in my office twice a day this week wanting your seat in the seminar.

**RILEY:** Give it to him.

**LANDRY:** I did. On Monday.

**RILEY:** Then why'd you—

**LANDRY:** I wanted to see if you'd come tell me.

She doesn't know what to say.

**LANDRY:** I've never been to a rock-and-roll concert. My wife says I'd hate it.

**RILEY:** You'd hate it.

**LANDRY:** Send me the record anyway.

That's all. She leaves. (She mails him a copy of *Borrowed Stone* the following week. In the epilogue it's on his office shelf between William James and a Loeb Plato, spine out.)

### Sunday dinner

Sunday, Linwood. The Riley house: a brick ranch with a carport and a dogwood, the dining table with its leaf out because Tommy is home on a weekend pass from Norfolk. Tom Riley carving a roast and making a pun about it that the player can hear coming from a long way off. Joan bringing in the rolls.

Riley tells them at the table. The player chooses when: before grace, during, after the roast, over pie. The later she waits, the more of an ordinary family dinner the player gets first (Tommy's Navy stories, Tom's pun about the roast, an argument about Ford), and the harder it is.

However she says it, it comes out the same.

**RILEY:** I didn't register this semester.

Tom puts his fork down. Tommy looks at his plate.

**TOM:** For how long?

**RILEY:** I don't know.

**TOM:** Is it the band?

**RILEY:** It's the band.

Tom looks at Joan. Joan is buttering a roll. She keeps buttering it.

**TOM:** Well.

The player can let the silence go, or have Riley fill it (a defense, an apology, a joke). If she defends it, Tom says "I didn't say anything." If she apologizes, Tom says "Don't apologize, Maggie, just explain." If she jokes, Tommy laughs, and Tom almost does.

Then Tom picks the fork back up.

**TOM:** Your mother made pie.

### The piano

After dinner. Tom and Tommy in the den with a college football game. Riley in the kitchen drying dishes. Joan washing.

They work for a while. Then Joan turns off the water and dries her hands and goes into the living room, and after a moment the player hears the upright: a Bach chorale, slowly.

Riley goes to the doorway with the dish towel.

Joan plays to the end of the chorale. She doesn't look up from the keys.

**JOAN:** I had a place at Oberlin. In 1949.

Riley doesn't move.

**RILEY:** You never told me that.

**JOAN:** I auditioned in the spring. They wrote in May. Your daddy asked me in June.

**RILEY:** And you didn't go.

**JOAN:** I thought I'd go the next year.

She plays a chord, and doesn't resolve it.

**RILEY:** Why didn't you?

**JOAN:** Tommy.

That's the whole answer. Joan resolves the chord.

**JOAN:** I'm not telling you anything, Margaret. I'm telling you what I did.

The player, as Riley, can go sit beside her on the bench. If she does, Joan slides the hymnal over without a word and plays the chorale again, and Riley sings the alto line, and they don't talk about it. If she doesn't, she stands in the doorway with the dish towel until the chorale ends, and then goes back to the dishes.

Either way, on the way out to the car, Riley gives her mother the Leipzig record if the player bought it in Mission 2. Joan looks at it for a long time.

**JOAN:** Where did you get this?

**RILEY:** A store on Tenth Street.

**JOAN:** What were you doing on Tenth Street?

**RILEY:** Watching somebody buy our record.

**JOAN:** Did they?

**RILEY:** Eventually.

> **Knowledge.** Riley has never heard about Oberlin before tonight. It reframes every Saturday morning her mother has spent alone in the church playing to nobody, which is what "Sunday Clothes" is about. Riley wrote that song without knowing this.

## MISSION 4 — OCCASIONALLY ASTONISHING

**Playable:** Riley · **Where:** the van, US 19 and off-map to Chattanooga · **When:** Saturday October 11 · **Length:** ~20 min

### The reviews

The van, westbound for a Saturday at a college gym in Chattanooga. Cal driving. Ellis on the wheel well with a memo book. Dean on his bench drumming on the wall. Tully asleep on the amplifiers. Riley in the passenger seat with a manila folder of newspaper clippings she's been collecting for three weeks and hasn't admitted to anybody.

**DEAN:** What's that?

**RILEY:** Nothing.

**DEAN:** It's reviews. She's got reviews.

**RILEY:** I've got *some* reviews.

**DEAN:** Read them.

**CAL:** Don't read them.

**DEAN:** Read them in voices.

She reads them in voices. This is a playable scene: the player picks which clipping to read next, and Riley picks a voice for each writer (the player can choose from a few), and the band reacts.

- **The *Tannersville Courier*, Saturday entertainment page.** Two paragraphs. Riley reads it as a Baptist deacon. *"Local musicians of evident sincerity who would be well advised to turn down. The Row has seen this kind of thing before."* Dean: "The Row hasn't seen anything." Cal: "The Row has seen everything. That's the problem."
- **The *Laurel City Herald*.** Warm, a whole column. Riley reads it straight, because it's good, and her voice goes a little careful reading it. *"…and 'Sunday Clothes,' in which Ellis Blake imagines a church organist playing Bach to an empty sanctuary, is the album's tenderest moment…"* She stops.
**DEAN:** Keep going.
**RILEY:** "Ellis Blake imagines."
**ELLIS** *(from the back)*: I didn't write that.
**RILEY:** I know you didn't write it.
**ELLIS:** I'll call them.
**RILEY:** It's fine.
It isn't fine. The player can see her tuck her hair behind her ear.
- **Billboard, "Recommended LPs," three lines.** Riley reads it as a newscaster. *"Regional quartet's debut is occasionally astonishing and frequently loud. Southeastern dealers should stock."*
**DEAN:** "Occasionally astonishing."
**CAL:** That's good. From them, that's good.
**DEAN:** Occasionally. Occasionally!
**ELLIS:** It's more than they say about most people.
- **The *Red and Black*** (a Georgia college paper). A rave, breathless, three columns, clearly written at 3 a.m. Riley reads it as Dean, and Dean is delighted, and it's the best impression in the van.

"Occasionally astonishing" enters the band's language immediately. For the rest of the game, any of them can use it about anything: a sandwich, a motel, a Dean drum fill, a sunset. ("How's the coffee?" / "Occasionally astonishing.")

### Correction

A week later (the player sees it in a short coda at the end of the mission), the *Laurel City Herald* prints a correction in six-point type at the bottom of page 2-C:

> *A review Sept. 28 misattributed the song "Sunday Clothes" on the Blakes' album "Borrowed Stone." It was written by Margaret Riley.*

Cal wrote the letter. He mailed it on Monday without telling anyone. Riley finds out because Dean finds the correction and reads it aloud in the van in a newscaster voice. She looks at Cal in the rearview mirror. He's watching the road.

**RILEY:** Margaret.

**CAL:** That's what's on the copyright.

### The vote

Somewhere past the state line, Cal pulls into a Stuckey's for gas, and Eddie Farris is on the pay phone by the ice machine because he's been chasing them for an hour. The band stands around the pay phone in a knot while Eddie talks. The player holds the receiver as Riley.

**EDDIE** *(on the phone)*: The Civic Auditorium. November first. Sixteen hundred seats. Laurel City.

A noise from Dean that makes a trucker turn around.

**EDDIE:** And I want to take Theo Marchand off the Lantern and put him on the road with you. Front of house. You're going to be playing rooms where the house engineer has never heard of you and doesn't care. You need your own. I need a vote.

The vote is the band's rule: no band decisions alone. Riley relays the question.

**DEAN:** Yes. Obviously. He made us sound like Zeppelin at the Lantern.

**ELLIS:** Yeah.

Riley looks at Cal.

**CAL:** I'll abstain.

**DEAN:** Why? You love that guy's mixes. You said the Lantern tape was the best we ever sounded.

**CAL:** Conflict.

**DEAN:** With what?

**CAL:** Scheduling.

Dean looks confused. Ellis doesn't look at anything.

The player, as Riley, casts the last vote. There's only one option, and it comes fast.

**RILEY** *(into the phone)*: Three yes, one abstain.

**EDDIE:** What's the abstain?

**RILEY:** Scheduling.

She hangs up. She doesn't look at Cal. Cal pays for the gas.

> **Knowledge.** Riley knows about Cal and Theo (Chapter VI, the Blind Tiger, a raised glass). Ellis doesn't, yet. Dean doesn't. Cal abstained from hiring the man he's in love with because it would be dishonest to vote, and that's Cal all over.

### The list

That night, after Chattanooga, in the motel, Riley opens her notebook to the inside cover, where she keeps a list of words she likes (*palimpsest; ramshackle; susurrus; eaves; jubilate; Tuesday*), and adds, in pen: *occasionally astonishing*.

The player can read the rest of the list. It goes back to 1972.

## MISSION 5 — LAST SHIFT

**Playable:** Ellis · **Where:** Hollow Ridge Auto & Service · **When:** Saturday October 18, 7:00 a.m. – 1:00 p.m. · **Length:** ~25 min

### Why today

Eddie's fall dates don't leave room for four days a week at a garage. Ellis told Roy two weeks ago. Roy said "All right" and nothing else, and they've both been polite to each other ever since in a way neither of them can stand.

Saturday. The last half-day. The player works it: a real shift, ordinary jobs, the same diagnostic systems from Chapter I, but Ellis is faster at all of it now and the game lets the player feel that.

### The jobs

- A pulpwood truck with a slipping clutch. Ellis adjusts the linkage in twelve minutes; in Chapter I the same job took the player half an hour.
- A flat on a school bus driver's Pontiac.
- **Mrs. Pardue's Rambler.** It's making a noise she describes, on the phone, as "the goose again, but smaller." Ellis finds the same heat shield from Chapter I, with the same hose clamp, loose again. He replaces the clamp. She's in the office doorway in a cardigan.
  **MRS. PARDUE:** I bought your record.
  **ELLIS:** Where?
  **MRS. PARDUE:** The Western Auto. They had two. I bought one.
  **ELLIS:** Who bought the other one?
  **MRS. PARDUE:** I couldn't say.
  Then:
  **MRS. PARDUE:** Are you writing, Ellis?
  In Chapter I he lied to her. The player now gets three options: "Yes ma'am." / "Some." / say nothing. If he says "Yes ma'am," she says "Good," and pays, and goes. If "Some," she says "Some is plenty." If nothing, she looks at him for a while and says "Hm," and it's the same *hm* as Chapter I, and he'll wonder about it later.

> **Replay layer.** If the player left a copy of *Borrowed Stone* on the kitchen table in Mission 1, the second copy at the Western Auto was bought by the county extension agent's wife. If they didn't, Wayne bought it. Either way Wayne has one. Mrs. Pardue does know who bought the second copy, in the second case, and won't say.

### The pumps

Around eleven, a truck pulls up to the pumps: a green Ford with a stock rack and a bale of hay in the bed. Floyd Tolliver, seventy, overalls, a face like a walnut. Ellis goes out to pump the gas. He hasn't seen Floyd up close in two years.

The player can feel the game hold its breath. Floyd is the Tolliver farm. The farm is on Tolliver Road.

**FLOYD:** Fill it.

Ellis fills it. Floyd stands by the pump with his hands in his overalls.

**FLOYD:** Heard your record.

**ELLIS:** Yes sir.

**FLOYD:** Ada bought it. At the Western Auto in Laurel Gap, they had it too.

**ELLIS:** Yes sir.

**FLOYD:** I don't know about that kind of music.

**ELLIS:** No sir.

Floyd watches the numbers turn on the pump.

**FLOYD:** Dolly died. In March.

Ellis's hand is on the nozzle.

**ELLIS:** I heard.

**FLOYD:** She was twenty-four. That's old for a horse that worked.

**ELLIS:** Yes sir.

The pump clicks off. Ellis hangs up the nozzle. Floyd pays in cash, exact.

**FLOYD:** Your sister would've liked it. The record.

He gets in the truck and drives off toward the South Fork.

The player can do anything next. Go back in the garage. Stand at the pump. There's no prompt. If they just stand there, Ellis stands there until Roy calls him from the bay ("Ellis. Bus driver's here."), and he goes.

> **Design note.** No one in Hollow Ridge has said Grace's name to Ellis in the whole game, and Floyd doesn't either. "Your sister." He says it the way you'd tell someone the weather. It's the kindest thing anyone in this town has ever said to Ellis and it comes from the one family he's been avoiding for two and a half years.

### Danny

One o'clock. Closing time on a Saturday. Ellis sweeps out the second bay. Roy is in the office doing the week's tickets. The player can go in when they're ready.

The office: the parts calendar, the adding machine, Evelyn's paper sack, the framed photograph on the shelf that has been there since Chapter I (a boy of nineteen in Army fatigues, squinting) next to the triangle of folded flag in its wooden case. The player has walked past it a hundred times.

Ellis hangs his shop rag on the nail.

**ROY:** You want your check now or you want me to mail it.

**ELLIS:** Now's fine.

Roy writes it. Tears it out. Hands it over. Ellis looks at it: a week's pay, plus fifty dollars that isn't a week's pay.

**ELLIS:** This is wrong.

**ROY:** I can add.

**ELLIS:** Roy.

**ROY:** Go on.

Ellis folds it into his shirt pocket. He doesn't know what to do next, so he looks at the shelf.

Roy follows his eyes. There's a long pause.

**ROY:** Danny worked that bay. Summer of sixty-six.

Ellis doesn't say anything.

**ROY:** He couldn't tell a carburetor from a coffee pot. Wanted to be a radio announcer. Talked all day. Talked to the cars.

He smiles, a little.

**ROY:** You don't talk to the cars.

**ELLIS:** Sometimes.

**ROY:** Not out loud.

That's all. Roy has never said his son's name in front of Ellis before, and he won't again. He goes back to the tickets.

> **Design note.** This is the only time in the game Roy mentions Danny. Roy lost a son and chose tenderness toward a boy who wasn't his; Wayne lost a daughter and chose anger toward the one he had. The game never compares them. The player will.

### The shirt

Ellis goes to take off his work shirt: the gray Dickies with the oval patch that says ELLIS in red script, the one he's worn since Chapter I.

**ROY:** Keep the shirt.

**ELLIS:** It's the shop's.

**ROY:** It's got your name on it. What am I gonna do with it, hire another Ellis?

Ellis keeps the shirt.

At the door:

**ROY:** Ellis.

**ELLIS:** Yeah.

**ROY:** Don't become an asshole.

**ELLIS:** I'll try.

**ROY:** Don't try. Just don't.

He waves him out without looking up.

### Clean hands

Ellis drives home. At the Blake house he washes up at the kitchen sink with Lava soap the way he has every day for three years, and gets the grease out of the cracks of his knuckles, and it doesn't come back the next day, or the next.

For the next several chapters, when the player is idle as Ellis, one of his idle animations is looking at his hands: turning them over, looking at the clean nails, the way you'd look at a borrowed pair of gloves. The game never explains it.

The gray shirt goes in his closet. It comes out again in Chapter X.

## MISSION 6 — 11:47 P.M.

**Playable:** Cal → relay (Eddie, a stranger, Dean) → Ellis · **Where:** off Tenth Street, Laurel City → the SR 9 Spur → a bedroom past the reservoir → the Starlite → the Blake house · **When:** Tuesday October 21, 11:40 p.m. – 12:40 a.m. · **Length:** ~25 min

### Theo's kitchen

Cal has been spending Tuesdays in Laurel City since September. The band believes this involves a man who sells vacuum tubes.

Theo's apartment off Tenth Street, the one with the fire escape and the plants. The sixth game of the World Series is on the little black-and-white television on the counter with the sound off (Theo can't stand the announcers). The kitchen radio is on WLRC with the sound up, because Theo listens to other people's mixes the way other people read the paper. Cal is at the kitchen table with Theo's reel-to-reel in pieces in front of him. Theo is drying a skillet.

On the television, in silence, a man in a Red Sox uniform slides into home and is called out. Theo doesn't look.

**THEO:** Who's winning?

**CAL:** It's tied.

**THEO:** Is that good?

**CAL:** It's the ninth inning of the sixth game of the World Series.

**THEO:** I'll take that as a yes.

The player, as Cal, can keep working on the Teac (a pinch roller, a bad solder joint on the playback head lead) or watch the game or talk to Theo. It's a quiet domestic scene and the game lets it be quiet.

Then the song on WLRC ends, and the voice that comes on is one the player knows.

**MARTIN KELLER** *(on the radio; twenty-three now, a little deeper, still earnest)*: Ninety-seven-one, WLRC, it's eleven forty-six, I'm Martin Keller, and if you're watching the ballgame you're going to miss this. A year ago I ran a ten-watt station at Tannersville College you could hear from the parking lot if the wind was right. The first record I played on it that mattered was four people from right up the road. They made an album. This is from it.

The first chord of "Still Here."

Cal stops with the soldering iron in the air.

Theo sets the skillet down and turns the radio up.

The player, as Cal, can do nothing, or stand, or look at Theo. There's no prompt that matters. Cal listens to himself on a real radio station in a real city, and the camera stays on his face in a medium shot for longer than the game has ever held on Cal.

At the end of the first chorus he takes a matchbook off the table and writes *11:47* on it with his Parker Jotter.

**CAL:** Bass is buried.

**THEO:** It is not.

**CAL:** It's a little buried.

**THEO:** Your father said that. About the college station.

**CAL:** He was right then too.

Theo laughs and puts his hand on the back of Cal's neck, and leaves it there.

### The relay

The song carries the camera out of Theo's kitchen window, the same grammar as the radio relay in Chapter III, but bigger now, because this signal is fifty thousand watts. Each stop lasts a section of the song. The player controls each person lightly (look, turn the dial, react) and the song keeps playing across all of them without a break.

- **First verse, end** — a Checker cab on Hawthorn Street downtown, the driver drumming on the wheel, a fare in the back asking "Who is this?" and the driver saying "Don't know. Turn it up?"
- **Second verse** — **Eddie Farris** in his Buick on the SR 9 Spur, coming home from a club in the suburbs. He hears the first line and pulls onto the shoulder with his flashers on and sits there with both hands on the wheel. At the chorus he hits the wheel with both palms, twice, and laughs out loud in an empty car. Then he gets out and looks for a pay phone.
- **Bridge** — a bedroom in a subdivision past the reservoir. A girl of sixteen in a flannel nightgown, crouched over a clock radio with a Panasonic cassette recorder held up to its speaker, pushing RECORD and PLAY down together, holding her breath so she won't be on the tape. Her name is on a spiral notebook on the bed: *SHERRY TOLAN*. The player will see her again.
- **Last chorus** — the **Starlite**, in the Laurel Gap. The kitchen radio by the pass-through. Lynette Crowe on her break at the counter with coffee. The cook turning it up with a greasy thumb. Two pulpwood drivers in a booth stop talking. **Dean**, in the corner booth with a piece of chess pie he came here to eat because Lynette's working, hears it and stands up on the booth seat.

**LYNETTE:** Get down.

**DEAN:** It's us.

**LYNETTE:** I know it's you. Get down off my seat.

He gets down. He's crying a little and doesn't care who sees. The pulpwood drivers look at him, then at the radio, then at each other.

**DRIVER:** That's them boys from Hollow Ridge.

**OTHER DRIVER:** One of 'em's a girl.

### Martin

The song ends. The switch goes into the studio: the WLRC control room, the red ON AIR light, Martin Keller at the board in a cardigan with his headphones half on. Every one of the six phone lines on the request line is blinking. The player, as Martin, can watch them blink.

**MARTIN** *(on air)*: That was the Blakes. Hollow Ridge, Georgia. "Still Here." Phones are... okay. Hang on.

He looks at the lines. He looks at the cart rack. He doesn't have permission to do what he's about to do.

**MARTIN** *(on air)*: I'm going to play it again.

He does. It's 11:51.

### The Blake house

From Martin's board the switch travels the only way it can: down the phone line. Dean at the Starlite pay phone, dialing with the receiver jammed between his ear and shoulder, a stack of dimes on the shelf.

The phone rings in the hall of the Blake house.

The player is Ellis, in his room, lying on the bed with a transistor radio on his chest. It's AM only. It has the ballgame on. So does the television in the front room, where Wayne is in his chair. They are listening to the same game in separate rooms, both turned up, the way they do Georgia football on Saturdays.

The phone rings four times. Wayne doesn't get up. Ellis does.

**ELLIS:** Hello.

**DEAN** *(screaming)*: TURN ON NINETY-SEVEN.

**ELLIS:** It's the tenth inning.

**DEAN:** THEY'RE PLAYING IT. MARTIN'S PLAYING IT. HE PLAYED IT ONCE AND NOW HE'S PLAYING IT AGAIN, BLAKE, HE'S PLAYING IT *AGAIN*.

Ellis stands in the hall with the receiver.

**ELLIS:** I don't have FM.

**DEAN:** WHAT?

**ELLIS:** Nothing in the house gets FM.

**DEAN:** THEN GO FIND SOME.

Dean hangs up (he's out of dimes).

### The truck

The player knows where there's an FM radio, even if Ellis doesn't. Ellis stands in the hall for a second, then goes out the kitchen door into the yard in his socks. Tater gets up off the porch and follows him.

Wayne's F-100 in the dark. Unlocked; it always is. Ellis gets in the driver's side and turns the key to accessory. The dash lights come up.

Under the dash, screwed to the bottom of the cracked panel with two sheet-metal screws, is a small aftermarket FM converter, the kind they sell at the Western Auto for nineteen dollars. It's tuned to **88.9**.

Ellis looks at it.

88.9 is WTCR, the ten-watt college station in Tannersville. The one you can only hear from the top of Stony Knob.

The player may remember a dome light at a pull-off below the summit in Chapter III, and a man who said a deputy told him about the broadcast. Ellis doesn't say anything. He turns the dial to 97.1.

The last chorus of the second play. Through one bad speaker in his father's truck, in the dark, with the dog sitting in the gravel by the door looking up at him.

*And I'm still here. And you're still here.*

The song ends. Martin Keller says "That's twice. Don't tell my program director," and goes into a commercial for a waterbed store.

Then the prompt. Ellis can leave the converter on 97.1, or turn it back to 88.9.

- If he turns it back to 88.9, carefully, to the exact line on the dial, nobody ever knows.

Either way, Ellis gets out, and pats his leg for Tater, and goes back inside.

> **Replay layer.** The converter was installed in November 1974, the week of the WTCR broadcast in Chapter III. Wayne has left it on 88.9 for eleven months even though WTCR has been off the air every summer and he can't hear it from the yard anyway. This choice is tracked: in Chapter IX, if Ellis left it on 97.1, Wayne says, "Somebody moved my radio last fall." If not, Wayne says nothing, and on Stony Knob in Chapter IX the converter is still on 88.9.

### Extra innings

The front room. Wayne in his chair. The television: extra innings, the tenth, Fenway Park at night.

Ellis stands in the doorway.

**WAYNE:** Who was that on the phone?

**ELLIS:** Dean.

**WAYNE:** It's midnight.

**ELLIS:** They played it on the radio. In Laurel City. WLRC.

**WAYNE:** Hm.

A pitch. A foul ball.

**WAYNE:** Which one?

**ELLIS:** "Still Here."

Wayne doesn't say anything. The player knows, and Ellis doesn't know that the player knows, that Wayne heard that song once in a funeral home in Tannersville, and asked who it was about, and went white.

**WAYNE:** Twice?

**ELLIS:** Twice.

**WAYNE:** Hm.

Ellis doesn't go back to his room. The player can make him, but the default (and the prompt that pulses) is the couch. He sits down on the end of the couch nearest the door. His father doesn't look at him. They watch the game together in the same room.

The game plays out in real time on the television for as long as the player wants to stay. There's a catch in right field in the eleventh that makes Wayne say a word Ellis has never heard him say. Tater comes in and lies down on Ellis's feet. Nobody speaks. The player can leave at any time. If they stay until 12:34 a.m., they see it:

The twelfth inning. A catcher hits a high fly down the left-field line toward the foul pole, and stands at home plate waving his arms at the ball to stay fair, and Wayne Blake comes halfway out of his chair doing the same thing with both hands.

It's fair. It hits the pole.

Wayne slaps the arm of the chair and says "*Hah!*" out loud, one syllable, like a much younger man.

Ellis laughs. Wayne looks at him. For a second, two men who've just watched the same thing and liked it.

Then Wayne sits back.

**WAYNE:** Well. Go to bed.

Ellis goes to bed.

> **Design note.** The night the Blakes' song breaks in Laurel City is the night Ellis finds out his father has been listening to him from a mountaintop, and the two of them spend it watching a ballgame and not talking about either thing. Nobody in the Blake house can say what this night means, so a man waving a baseball fair says it for them. (The game is real: Game 6 of the 1975 World Series, Fenway Park, Tuesday October 21, ended by Carlton Fisk's home run at 12:34 a.m. Eastern.)

### The next morning

Morning on the radio system: from today, "Still Here" is in WLRC's rotation. The player will hear it in the open world whenever they're in range of Laurel City with an FM set: in taxis, from apartment windows on Tenth Street, in the van. By November it's on two stations in Tannersville that can't hear WLRC, because their program directors read the trade sheets. The WLRC program director comes in at nine to sixty-one phone messages. Southern Star rushes out a single.

## MISSION 7 — WHERE'S ELLIS?

**Playable:** Cal → Ellis → Cal · **Where:** the Civic Auditorium; Tenth Street; the Spur; the downtown rail yard; Engineers Park · **When:** Saturday November 1, 3:00 – 5:30 p.m. · **Length:** ~25 min

### The building

The Laurel City Civic Auditorium: a 1958 municipal hall on the edge of downtown, sixteen hundred seats, a proscenium, a fly loft, a union stage crew, and an echo off the back wall that Theo has been fighting since noon. Sold out. The marquee out front says THE BLAKES · TONIGHT · SOLD OUT, and Dean has already taken three SX-70s of it from across the street.

The player is Cal. He walks the building with a clipboard.

- **Tully**, crew chief now, stands at the loading dock with his hands in his pockets, miserable, because the union stagehands won't let him touch anything. "Union rules." Tully carried every amplifier this band has owned. He watches three strangers carry the Ampeg in, badly. Cal can talk to him. "They're dropping the corners." / "I know." / "You want me to say something?" / "No. I want to hit somebody, but I'm not going to."
- **Theo** at the front-of-house desk in the middle of the orchestra seats, his first night on the road with the band. He has a notebook of the Civic's problems (the back wall, a dead zone under the balcony, a hum in stage left). He doesn't look up when Cal comes over; they are extremely professional in public. "Your bass rig's buzzing." / "It's their power." / "It's always somebody's power."
- **Dean's Vistalite** on a real drum riser, lit from inside by a work light some stagehand has left on the floor, glowing like a jellyfish. Dean's lying on the riser looking up at the fly loft.

### Dex

At three-thirty-five a man comes in through the stage door past the union steward like he owns the building: thirty-one, very thin, a burgundy velvet jacket over a Stooges T-shirt, sunglasses indoors, a Sony cassette recorder on a strap, a spiral notebook, and a film canister he keeps shaking like a maraca.

**DEX:** Dex Lundgren. *Rave.* Harold Vance sent me your record with a note that said "Please." Nobody says please to me. I flew down from Detroit. Is this the bass player?

**CAL:** Cal Mercer.

**DEX:** You look like a bass player. That's not an insult, that's a compliment, the bass players are the only honest men in rock and roll. You want a vitamin?

He shakes the film canister.

**CAL:** No.

**DEX:** Correct answer. Where's the singer?

The player chooses. Every option is a lie, because Cal doesn't know.

- **"Getting strings."**
- **"Parking."**
- **"He'll be here."**

If "Getting strings": Dex says, "I love that. The singer's out getting strings. Where?" and writes it down. Whichever Cal says, it ends up in the magazine in Mission 13 in some form.

It's four o'clock. Ellis isn't here. He drove himself from Hollow Ridge in the Valiant (he wanted to come alone, which he's never asked for before). He isn't at the motor hotel on the Spur; Cal calls from the pay phone in the hallway and the desk says the key's still on the board. His Jazzmaster is here, because Tully brought it in the truck.

At 4:20 Theo says the only thing he can do without a singer is check the singer's mic with Dean talking into it, and Dean does a four-minute monologue about Evel Knievel into it that the union crew enjoys very much.

Cal makes a decision.

**CAL** *(to Dean)*: Keep him busy.

**DEAN:** Who?

**CAL:** *Rave.*

**DEAN:** How?

**CAL:** Talk.

**DEAN:** Oh, I can do that.

> **Consequence.** Dean spends the next ninety minutes with Dex Lundgren and a film canister. Everything Dex prints about the band's "wild man drummer" comes from this afternoon. So does the sentence that will define everything: Dean, high and full of love, telling a stranger with a tape recorder that Ellis Blake is a genius.

### The search

Cal takes the van. The player drives it.

The game doesn't mark a destination. Instead, when the player opens the map, there's a note on it in Cal's handwriting, which he wrote in the van at a red light: a list of places Ellis would go in Laurel City. It's a list only someone who'd been paying attention for a year could make. The player can go to them in any order.

- **Tenth Street, the pawnshop with guitars in the window.** The owner, behind a cage: "Your singer? He came in last week. Looked at a Telecaster for an hour. Didn't buy it. Nice kid. Doesn't talk." Not here.
- **The boiled-peanut stand on the Spur frontage road** (ranked second on Ellis's list of every peanut stand between the two cities; the player may have the list from Chapter IV). A boy of about fifteen in a homemade THE BLAKES shirt (iron-on letters, both E's present) is buying peanuts. He looks at Cal. "Hey. Hey! You're—" / "Yes." / "—the bass player?" / "Yes." / "Can you give this to Ellis?" He hands Cal a folded letter addressed ELLIS BLAKE (SINGER). Cal puts it in his shirt pocket. Not here.
- **The rail yard overpass downtown**, where you can stand on the sidewalk and look down at forty tracks. Nothing, just trains. Cal stands there a minute anyway, because he understands why Ellis would come here, and the player can stand with him.
- **Engineers Park.** The Laurel City Engineers' ballpark, Double-A, closed for the season. The gate by the ticket window is open because a groundskeeper is running a mower on the outfield. Cal knows this is where Ellis came with Wayne on April 12 (it's in the ledger: *4/12/75 — E. off. Engineers game w/ father*). He goes in.

### The grandstand (Ellis)

As Cal comes up the ramp into the stands, the camera finds Ellis twenty rows up behind first base, and the switch happens with the grammar the player knows: the stutter, the focus pull, the HUD fading, Cal walking on out of frame. The player is Ellis.

The field is brown. The scoreboard is dark. The mower's going around the outfield in slow stripes.

Clara is two seats down, with her boots up on the seat in front of her.

**CLARA:** They're going to sing it.

**ELLIS:** Reckon so.

**CLARA:** Martin said. People call every night and ask for it.

**ELLIS:** So they'll sing it.

**CLARA:** Those were my words.

He doesn't answer.

**CLARA:** And you didn't even get them right.

**ELLIS:** What?

**CLARA:** In the alley. I never said "you did, by playing it at all." I said "you keep playing in public, that's basically asking."

**ELLIS:** Same thing.

**CLARA:** It's not the same thing. You made it prettier.

**ELLIS:** It's a song.

**CLARA:** It was a conversation.

The mower turns at the warning track.

**ELLIS:** What do you want me to do? Not play it?

**CLARA:** Tell them it's mine.

**ELLIS:** It's yours.

**CLARA:** Tell *them*. Where they can hear it.

Ellis starts to say something, and stops, because Cal Mercer is walking up the aisle steps toward him with a clipboard. Ellis closes his mouth in the middle of a word.

**CLARA:** He heard you.

> **Replay layer.** Clara is right about the alley. The player can check: in Chapter I she said, "You keep playing in public. That's basically asking." The song changed it. Ellis's memory of their first conversation has already been rebuilt into something prettier, which is exactly what Professor Landry's lecture on memory said happens, and exactly what happened to Ellis's memory of the crash.

### The grandstand (Cal)

Stutter. Cal, at the end of the row. From here, the seats on either side of Ellis are empty.

**CAL:** Soundcheck was at four.

**ELLIS:** I know what time it is.

**CAL:** It's ten after five.

**ELLIS:** I know what time it is, Cal.

Cal sits down, one seat away, in the seat Clara was in a moment ago. (The player, as Cal, can't know that. The player, as a person, may.)

They watch the mower for a while.

**ELLIS:** There's sixteen hundred people.

**CAL:** Fifteen hundred and eighty. Eddie comped twenty.

Ellis laughs, once, through his nose.

**ELLIS:** What if they sing it?

The player chooses Cal's answer. Every option is technical, because that's how Cal answers emotional questions.

- **"Then drop out on the second verse and let them. Dean'll hold the time."**
- **"Then you'll be in tune for once."**
- **"Then we'll turn the monitors up."**

Ellis looks at him.

**ELLIS:** Huh.

He doesn't ask who Ellis was talking to. He stands up.

**CAL:** There's a man from a magazine.

**ELLIS:** I know.

**CAL:** Dean's talking to him.

**ELLIS:** Oh, no.

**CAL:** Yeah.

### The van

Driving back. A red light on Hawthorn Street. Cal reaches under the seat and takes out the green ledger, and turns back to December 1974, and the player sees the page: *12/20/74 — Marlon's — Friday. $200.00. Toward Marlon loan $50.00 (bal. $250.00).* And in the margin beside it, small, a word struck through with a single straight line: *Clara?*

He looks at it until the car behind him honks. He puts the ledger back under the seat.

At the next light he takes the letter out of his shirt pocket and hands it to Ellis.

**CAL:** Fan mail.

**ELLIS:** From where?

**CAL:** A peanut stand.

Ellis reads the outside: ELLIS BLAKE (SINGER). He puts it in his jacket without opening it. (The player can read it later from the inventory. It's from a fifteen-year-old named Kevin who wants to know how to get "that sound" out of a Sears guitar, and who explains that his father left too.)

> **Knowledge.** A year ago Cal heard Ellis mention a Clara nobody had met (Chapter IV). Now he has heard Ellis talking to someone who wasn't there. He also knows from Marlon where Grace was going the night she died. He puts these facts next to each other in the ledger, in his head, and doesn't know what the sum is. He won't ask. Cal thinks every problem has a cause and a fix, and he's afraid this one doesn't.

## MISSION 8 — SIXTEEN HUNDRED

**Playable:** all four (rotating during the performance) · **Where:** the Civic Auditorium · **When:** Saturday November 1, 8:30 p.m. – 1:00 a.m. · **Length:** ~45 min

### Before

The dressing room. 8:25. The union stage manager bangs on the door: "Five minutes." Through the wall, sixteen hundred people make a sound none of them has ever heard from the inside: a low pressure, like weather coming in.

Dean is jiggling one knee so hard the makeup mirror rattles. Riley is sitting cross-legged on the counter with her glasses on, reading the set list over and over. Cal has his bass on already. Ellis is by the door.

Cal's set list, on the back of a union time sheet:

> LOW WATER
> ICE MACHINE
> LINWOOD
> BORROWED STONE
> PARIETAL HOURS
> NO NAME
> SUNDAY CLOTHES
> TOMORROW PROBLEM
> STONY KNOB
> STILL HERE
> ——
> SHAPE NOTE

**DEAN:** Kick drum.

They go to the side of the stage, in the dark, behind the curtain, where the Vistalite is waiting on its riser, and each of them touches the kick drum. Dex Lundgren is standing in the wings with his notebook out, watching this.

**DEX:** What is that? Is that spiritual?

**DEAN:** It's a drum.

**DEX:** What does it mean?

**DEAN:** It means we touched the drum.

Dex writes this down with visible happiness.

### The room

Bigger rooms change the performance system, and the player feels it immediately.

The crowd model at the Civic is slow and enormous. In Marlon's, a woman stopped stirring her drink. At the Lantern, eight hundred people moved like one body. Here, sixteen hundred people are in seats with armrests and ushers, and their response arrives late, like thunder after lightning: the player does something, and the room answers a beat and a half later, from the front rows back to the balcony. The stage monitors are bad (the Civic's, not Theo's), so Ellis can barely hear Riley. The room's echo off the back wall comes back at them a quarter-second late. The band has to play to each other harder than they've ever had to, facing inward, the hollow square, or they come apart.

The performance rotates through all four, a song or two each, with switches on the band's own musical cues (Cal's headstock, Dean's sticks, Riley's nod):

- **Dean** opens "Low Water" alone on the floor tom, and the player, with *read the room*, sees the crowd as Dean does: a huge dim field of energy, bright in the first ten rows where the college kids have pushed forward out of their seats, and cold and dark under the balcony. The task is to find where it's warm and play to that.
- **Riley** on "Linwood" and "Sunday Clothes." On "Sunday Clothes," Ellis sings harmony under her, because it's her song. The player chooses her phrasing. If the player looks out, there are three girls in the fourth row who know every word, and one of them is crying, and it isn't about Ellis.
- **Cal** on "Borrowed Stone" and "Parietal Hours." The player's job is mostly *lock*, holding Dean back from rushing into the echo. Cal is watching Ellis more than he usually does. The player can use *attention* to look at him, and the game lets Cal look for as long as the player wants.
- **Dean** on "Tomorrow Problem." He throws his sticks up on the last chorus and the whole front section shouts the title back at him (*THAT'S A TOMORROW PROBLEM*), and the balcony, a beat and a half late, shouts it too. No crowd has sung Dean's song before. He'll remember it for the rest of his life.
- **Ellis** on "No Name" and "Stony Knob," and then on the one they've all been waiting for.

### "This one's for Clara"

Between "Stony Knob" and "Still Here," the stage goes dark except for a single light on Ellis. He's supposed to count it in.

He steps to the mic. The player gets no prompt here. The line is fixed, because of the grandstand.

**ELLIS:** This one's for Clara.

That's all. He counts it in.

The game gives the player one look at each of the others as the first chord rings, through Ellis's *attention*:
- **Riley** is looking at the stage-left wing, where it's dark. Then she looks at her hands.
- **Cal** is looking at Ellis.
- **Dean** is grinning at him, because Dean assumes Clara is a girl in Hollow Ridge, and he's delighted.

And in the stage-left wing, past the fly rail, in the dark, a young woman in a jean jacket is standing with her arms folded, watching.

### The second verse

First verse. Ellis sings it. By the second line, the front rows are singing it with him.

*You sat on a crate in the alley in the cold…*

By the end of the verse, it's the whole floor. The player can feel it through the controller: a low vibration under the music that isn't the bass.

Then the chorus, and the balcony's in.

Before the second verse, the game offers Ellis the *space* verb: step back from the mic and don't sing. (If the player chose Cal's first answer in the grandstand, the prompt pulses. If not, it's there, quietly.) If the player takes it, Ellis steps back.

And sixteen hundred people, in a municipal auditorium in Laurel City, sing the second verse of "Still Here" without him:

*Everybody in this town can see my business.*
*Nobody here knows you.*

Off-mic, behind the kit, Dean says it to Cal, and he means it:

**DEAN:** Are they doing that for us?

**CAL:** Yes.

**DEAN:** All of them?

**CAL:** Keep time.

If the player, as Ellis, uses *attention* to look at the stage-left wing during the second verse, Clara is watching the crowd sing her words, and her face is very hard to read. Under sixteen hundred voices, Ellis can hear her humming the harmony, the high part, the part Riley sings, and she's singing it better.

If the player doesn't take the *space* verb, Ellis sings the whole song and the crowd sings along underneath him, and it's still enormous, and he never finds out what it sounds like without him. Both are complete.

The last chorus. The player brings the song home: Ellis turns all the way around to face Dean, the band's oldest signal, and Dean brings the song down, and it ends.

Sixteen hundred people stand up. It arrives at the stage a beat and a half late, like everything in this room.

Ellis doesn't turn back around to face them for a few seconds. He stands facing the drum riser. Dean, behind the Vistalite, is the only one who can see his face.

> **Replay layer.** Sixteen hundred strangers sang "Nobody here knows you" to a young man none of them knew, who was looking at someone none of them could see. The first-time player hears a love song. The second-time player hears the crowd get it exactly right, in unison, without knowing it.

### Encore

"Shape Note." Nine minutes of drone. The crowd, who came for "Still Here," stands through it at first out of respect, then sits down, then goes very quiet, and by minute six a lot of them are listening in a way they didn't know they could. Theo, at the front-of-house desk, has taken his hands off the faders.

The player can be anyone for the encore and switch freely between the four with the band cues. There's nothing to win. It's the band playing the thing they love most for people who didn't ask for it.

### The stage door

Midnight. A hundred people in the alley behind the Civic under a sodium light, with records and programs and pens. The band comes out one at a time, and each of them has a different stage door.

- **Dean** signs everything, including a girl's forearm and a man's bald head, and takes an SX-70 of the crowd from the top of the steps.
- **Riley** signs records. A boy of about seventeen asks if she's Ellis's girlfriend. The player chooses her answer (a joke, a question back, "I'm the one who plays the twelve-string," or nothing). Whatever she says, he nods and asks her to sign it "to Doug."
- **Cal** comes out carrying his bass. A girl walks up to him, hands him her pocketbook, says, "Would you hold this," and goes to get Ellis's autograph. Cal holds it. She comes back, takes it, says "Thanks," and doesn't ask for his. Cal stands there a moment. Then Tully, beside him, says, "I'd have asked," and Cal laughs, really laughs, for the second time this chapter.
- **Ellis** comes out last.

The crowd moves toward him and the player feels it: the camera tightens, the sound closes in. People say his name. People say *Ellis* the way you'd say the name of someone you know, and none of them know him.

A girl of sixteen pushes to the front with a copy of *Borrowed Stone*. The player may recognize her: the girl with the cassette recorder and the clock radio, *SHERRY TOLAN*, eleven days ago in a subdivision past the reservoir.

**SHERRY:** I taped it off the radio. Both times.

**ELLIS:** Both times?

**SHERRY:** The second one's better. He turned it up.

Ellis signs her record. Then she asks it.

**SHERRY:** Who's Clara?

Behind her, right behind her shoulder, in the sodium light, Clara is standing in the crowd with her hands in her jacket pockets.

The player gets a choice, and every option is small: *"Old friend."* / *"Just somebody."* / say nothing. If the player says nothing, Sherry waits, and Clara says, "Tell her," and the prompt comes back with only one option in it.

**ELLIS:** Old friend.

**CLARA:** Old?

Ellis almost laughs. Sherry thinks he's laughing at her question and goes red, and he says "No, it's a good question," and she goes away happy.

Dex Lundgren, leaning on the wall beside the stage door with his notebook, writes down *Old friend.*

### The interviews

The dressing-room hallway, 12:30 a.m. Dex interviews them one at a time on a folding chair with the Sony running. This is the chapter's interview system, and the game tells the player nothing about what it will do with their answers.

Each interview is a dialogue in the standard grammar (timing, topic, disclosure, silence). The game records the player's choices. In Mission 13 the player will read what was printed.

**Dean** (already interviewed all afternoon; this is a victory lap). Dex asks what the Blakes' sound is. The options: *"Space death."* / *"Like a train falling off a mountain."* / *"Loud."* Dex asks where Ellis was at soundcheck. Dean covers for him (every option is a cover). Dex asks what Ellis is like. There's only one option, and it's the one that matters, because Dean is high and happy and loves his friend:

**DEAN:** Blake's a genius, man. I'm not kidding. He's the real thing. The rest of us are just trying to keep up.

(What he means is that he loves him. It will be printed as the first sentence of the article.)

**Riley.** Dex asks what it's like to be the girl in the band. The options: *"I'm the one who can read music."* / *"One of us isn't a boy."* (the band's running joke) / talk about the arrangements (a long answer about voice-leading, her mother, and how the twelve-string drone works under Ellis's glide) / silence. Dex listens to all of it with real interest. He asks one follow-up about her mother.

**Cal.** Dex asks whose band it is. The options: *"It's ours."* / *"Nobody's. It's a band."* / *"Ask the bank."* If the player picks another answer, Dex pushes ("Come on. It's Ellis's band. Everybody knows it's Ellis's band.") and the prompt comes back, and this time Cal says it:

**CAL:** It's ours.

**Ellis.** The last interview. Dex turns the Sony toward him. Ellis has the Kevin letter in one pocket and a lot of adrenaline in the rest of him and nothing to say to a man from Detroit. The interview is built almost entirely on the silence mechanic: the player can hold the button through any question, and Ellis says nothing, and Dex waits, and then asks another. Questions: *Where do the songs come from?* ("Hollow Ridge.") *What's Hollow Ridge like?* ("Small.") *Why'd you quit the garage?* ("I didn't quit. I left.") *Who's Clara?* (Dex asks, then answers himself, "I know, I know. Old friend.") *What do you want?* This last question, the player can answer with silence, or with one line: *"To go home."* / *"Sleep."* / *"I don't know."*

Dex turns off the Sony.

**DEX:** You're the worst interview I've done in two years.

**ELLIS:** Thank you.

**DEX:** No. It's great. It's going to be great.

> **Design note — the interview system.** Nothing the player says here is misquoted. Every quote is real; the selection is Dex's. In Mission 13 the player will find that every answer they gave survived, and that the article is built only from the ones that fit the story Dex already wanted to tell.

### The money

1:00 a.m., the dressing room. Cal counts the settlement on the makeup counter under the lightbulbs, in stacks, facing the same way. The player can watch him do it and read the numbers off his ledger page:

> *11/1/75 — Civic Aud., L.C. — 1,580 pd @ $4.50 = $7,110 gross. Band 1/3 after hall & promoter — $2,400.00. Eddie 15% — 360.00. Crew (T.V. 60, T.M. 90) — 150.00. Fund 20% — 378.00. Shares (4) @ 378.00.*

Cal hands each of them $378 in cash.

Ellis holds it. The player can look at it. At Roy's, Ellis made about seventy dollars a week. This is five weeks in one night. He folds it once and puts it in his shirt pocket, and then takes it out and looks at it again.

Dean has already spent his, in his head, twice.

## MISSION 9 — EAST SLOPE *(optional)*

**Playable:** Ellis · **Where:** the Hollow Ridge Methodist cemetery · **When:** available in free roam from Sunday November 2 until the end of the chapter · **Length:** ~10 min

After the Civic, a location unlocks in Hollow Ridge that has been on the map all game and has never had a prompt: the Methodist cemetery on the hill above Main Street. The game doesn't mark it. If the player walks Ellis through the gate, a quiet cue appears.

### The hill

November. The maples on the hill are bare; the cedar on the east slope isn't. The town below: Main Street, the depot, the rail line, Marlon's roof. The monument yard across from the gate, with its rows of blank granite, where the album cover was taken.

The Blake plot is under the cedar. Two stones and a space.

> CLARA TATE BLAKE
> 1901 – 1970
> *She hath done what she could*

> GRACE CLARA BLAKE
> NOV. 2, 1958 – APR. 12, 1973

And beside them, bare ground, measured out, reserved.

The player can read both stones as long as they want. Many players will not have seen Grace's middle name before, or her grandmother's name, and the name of the young woman they've been listening to for seven chapters, cut into granite twice.

Ellis doesn't react to it. He's known these stones for years.

### What's there

The grass around both stones has been trimmed by hand, close, with shears; the rest of the hill is mowed and ragged. There's a coffee can with fresh zinnias, the last of the year, in front of Grace's stone. Ellis knows who trims the grass. The player doesn't have to.

Interactions, all optional:
- Pull the chickweed at the base of the stones.
- Straighten the coffee can.
- Sit down on the ground with his back against the cedar.

### Alone

The important thing about this place is who isn't here. Clara never comes to the cemetery. On the walk up the hill, if she was with him on Main Street, she's gone by the gate, and the player may notice that the game has never once shown her here. For as long as Ellis stays, he is completely alone, and the game is the quietest it has been since Chapter I.

If the player sits long enough (about two minutes, real time), a prompt appears: *Speak.* It offers three things.

- **"They played it on the radio."** Ellis says it to the stone, plainly, like telling someone the news at supper. Nothing answers.
- **"I'm sorry."** Ellis says it. Nothing answers. The wind moves the cedar. A freight goes by below the town. The camera doesn't move closer or farther away. Nothing happens.
- **Hum.** Ellis hums the harmony line from the Louvin Brothers' "When I Stop Dreaming," the high part, Grace's part. He's never sung it before in the game. It's not very good. He stops halfway.

Then he can leave, whenever the player wants.

> **Design note.** The cemetery is the one place in the game where Ellis is truly alone with the fact of his sister's death, and the one place his mind won't bring Clara, because Clara can't survive in a place that proves Grace is dead. The player who finds it has seen the name on the stone and will carry it into Chapter VIII. The player who doesn't find it loses nothing they need.
> **Tracked.** If Ellis pulled the chickweed, Wayne notices it in Chapter IX: "You been up the hill." / "How do you know?" / "Somebody did the chickweed."

## MISSION 10 — BELLE GROVE

**Playable:** Dean · **Where:** the Holloway house, Belle Grove; the Belle Grove Shell; the Starlite; the dry cleaner's · **When:** Sunday November 9, noon – 2:30 a.m. · **Length:** ~25 min

### The study door

Dean drives home to Belle Grove on Sunday at noon in the Chevelle after a Friday in Columbia and a Saturday in Augusta and not very much sleep since Thursday. The player drives. Dean's jaw is tight and the player can hear, faintly, under the engine, that he's grinding his teeth.

The circular drive. The pool covered for the winter. Mrs. Bea at the front door in her church hat, just back.

**MRS. BEA:** Your daddy's in the study.

**DEAN:** On a Sunday?

**MRS. BEA:** Door's open.

The study door in the Holloway house is never open on a Sunday. It's open.

### The desk

Richard Holloway at his desk in a cardigan. Carol on the leather sofa with her ankles crossed. Patty is not in the room and is definitely listening from the stairs.

On the green blotter, squared to its edges the way Richard squares everything, are two things.

The first is a letter on Tannersville College stationery, dated June 16, 1975, addressed to Dean at the Belle Grove house: *…regret to inform you that, following the spring term, your academic standing does not permit continued enrollment…* Dean got it out of the mailbox in June before anyone else did and put it in the glove compartment of the Chevelle, where it has been all summer.

**RICHARD:** The bursar called me on Thursday. To ask why I'd paid fall tuition for a student who was dismissed in June. They're very sorry for the confusion. They're sending a refund.

The second thing is a small round mirror, a single-edged razor blade and a cut-off drinking straw, laid side by side on a folded handkerchief.

**CAROL:** That was in the pocket of your blue jacket. I was taking it to the cleaners.

The player, as Dean, gets the dialogue grammar, and every option is a Dean option: a joke, a denial, an explanation, silence. The game lets the player try all of them in any order, and the room absorbs them.

- **Joke** ("I've been auditing the college. It failed."). Richard doesn't smile. Carol almost does.
- **Deny** ("That's not mine." / "Whose is it?" / "…Tully's?"). Carol: "Tully doesn't wear a blue jacket from Brooks Brothers, honey."
- **Explain** ("Everybody does it, it's for staying up, it's not a big deal, we played three nights in a row"). Richard listens to the whole thing with his hands folded, like a client explaining a tax position.
- **Silence.** Richard waits exactly as long as Dean does, and then a bit longer.

Carol, at some point, whichever path:

**CAROL:** I've been to parties, Dean. I know what a mirror is for.

### Terms

When the player runs out of options, Richard opens a folder. He's written it down. Of course he has.

**RICHARD:** I'm not going to pay for that. So I'm not going to pay for anything. The allowance stops today. The car...

He looks at a page.

**RICHARD:** The car is titled to the firm. It's been a depreciating asset of Holloway, Crane and Associates since 1971. I'll need the keys.

**DEAN:** You're repossessing my car.

**RICHARD:** The firm is.

The player can refuse. If Dean refuses, Richard waits, and Carol says, "Give your father the keys," in a voice the player hasn't heard her use, and Dean does. If the player doesn't refuse, Dean takes the keys out of his pocket and puts them on the blotter next to the razor blade, and squares them.

**RICHARD:** You can come to dinner on Sundays. Dinner is at six. You don't have to call.

That's Richard's version of *I love you*, and the player can hear it even if Dean can't.

**DEAN:** Is that in the contract too?

**RICHARD:** It's in mine.

### Upstairs

Dean packs. His bedroom is a teenager's museum: a Keith Moon poster, an Evel Knievel model on a ramp, trophies from Little League, a stack of *Creem* back issues. The player chooses what goes in the duffel from a dozen objects. The SX-70 and the film. George Carlin's *Class Clown*. A photo of Patty at nine on a pony. Three shirts. The cigar box of matchbooks from every venue since the Blind Tiger (if the player forgets it, it comes later). The Vistalite is at Vale's; the kit isn't a problem.

In the bathroom cabinet there's a film canister behind the Band-Aids. The player can take it or leave it. If they take it, it's in the duffel. If they leave it, Carol flushes it that night, and nothing about the story changes except what the player knows about themselves.

### Patty

At the bottom of the stairs, sitting on the last step, where she's been all along: Patty, sixteen, with the cigar box of matchbooks in her lap.

**PATTY:** You forgot these.

**DEAN:** Thanks.

**PATTY:** You're an idiot.

**DEAN:** I know.

**PATTY:** No, you don't.

She follows him out the front door and down the circular drive in her socks on the cold concrete. At the end of the drive she hands him a sealed envelope, a greeting-card envelope, violet, with ELLIS on the front in careful print.

**DEAN:** What's this?

**PATTY:** None of your business.

**DEAN:** You're writing to my singer?

**PATTY:** He's not your singer. Give it to him.

The player, as Dean, can hold it up to the sky to see through it. The game lets you. You can't read anything; it's a good envelope. Dean puts it in his jacket.

**PATTY:** Sunday. Six o'clock. You heard him.

**DEAN:** I heard him.

She goes back up the drive. She doesn't look back. At the front door she does.

### The pay phone

The Belle Grove Shell, a half-mile down the hill. A pay phone in a glass booth. Dean has a duffel, a cigar box, a violet envelope, a camera and about eleven dollars. The player chooses who to call, and can call more than one.

- **Riley.** Her Cutler Street number. She answers on the third ring. "Dean? Where are you?" He tells her. "Come over. You can have the couch." The player can accept or decline. If Dean accepts, the game gently redirects: halfway through the conversation he says "Actually, I'm fine, I've got somewhere," because he doesn't want Riley to see him like this, and he hangs up before she can argue.
- **Cal.** No answer. (It's Sunday. Cal's in Laurel City.)
- **Tully.** He answers on the first ring, as if he's been sitting by the phone, which he has not; he was cleaning a carburetor on the kitchen table. "Where are you?" "The Belle Grove Shell." "Stay there."

Twenty-five minutes later Tully's '64 Chevrolet pickup pulls up to the pumps. Dean throws the duffel in the bed and gets in.

**TULLY:** Where to?

**DEAN:** Drive.

**TULLY:** Good enough.

It's the Chapter I exchange, backward. Neither of them mentions it.

### The Starlite

The Laurel Gap, after dark. The Starlite's sign buzzing pink. Lynette on shift. Tully drops Dean at the door and goes to sit in the truck with the radio, because he knows.

Dean gets a booth. Lynette brings coffee without being asked and sees the duffel.

**LYNETTE:** What happened?

He tells her. He makes it funny: the study door open on a Sunday, the car being a depreciating asset of Holloway, Crane and Associates, Patty in her socks. It's a very good story and he tells it well. Lynette doesn't laugh once.

**LYNETTE:** You can't stay at my place.

**DEAN:** I didn't ask.

**LYNETTE:** You were about to.

He thinks about it.

**DEAN:** I was about to.

**LYNETTE:** Bobby's four.

**DEAN:** I know.

**LYNETTE:** He asks about you. He thinks you're a cowboy because of that jacket.

**DEAN:** It's a baseball jacket.

**LYNETTE:** He's four.

She goes and gets him a piece of chess pie and sits down across from him for her whole fifteen-minute break without saying much. It's the only fifteen minutes of the day he sits still.

At one point the player gets a prompt: *Wash up.* The Starlite bathroom. Dean at the sink, cold water on his face. Then the film canister comes out, if he took it, or a folded paper packet from his wallet if he didn't; there's always a spare. The player can't stop it. The camera stays on the mirror, on his face, not on what he's doing. He comes back out wiping his nose, and sits down, and Lynette looks at him, and knows, and doesn't say anything, and that's worse.

> **Design note — dependence.** Earlier chapters let the player choose whether Dean uses at a party. This one doesn't. By November 1975 it isn't a choice Dean is making, so it isn't one the player gets. The game shows it without music, without slow motion, without a cutaway. A man in a truck-stop bathroom, and then back to his pie.

### Cal's door

Two in the morning. Tully drives Dean to Tannersville, to the street by Vale Music, to the dry cleaner's with the apartment over it and the steam still coming out of the vent in the alley at 2 a.m. Dean climbs the outside stairs and knocks.

Cal opens the door in a T-shirt. He's only just back from Laurel City. He looks at Dean, the duffel, the cigar box, and Tully at the bottom of the stairs.

**DEAN:** Hey, Dad.

**CAL:** How long?

**DEAN:** Three days.

Cal holds the door open.

**CAL:** There's a cot.

(It becomes three weeks, and then it doesn't end.)

## MISSION 11 — LEDGER

**Playable:** Dean → Cal · **Where:** Cal's room over the dry cleaner, Tannersville · **When:** Wednesday November 12, 3:50 – 5:30 a.m. · **Length:** ~15 min

### Four in the morning

Cal's room: one room and a kitchenette. Steam heat from the dry cleaner below, which smells like hot cotton and cleaning fluid. Everything labeled. A shelf of clocks Cal has fixed or is fixing, eleven of them, all ticking slightly out of step so the room never quite goes quiet. Science fiction paperbacks. A bass on a stand. Cal asleep on his back on the bed with his glasses folded on the nightstand. Dean on Tully's Army cot in the corner, wide awake.

It's 3:50 a.m. The player is Dean. His knee is bouncing. He can't sleep, for the obvious reason. He's too loud when he moves. He tries to be quiet and the cot squeaks.

The ledger is open on the kitchen table under the lamp, because Cal fell asleep at it. Cal's been at it for three nights. The player may have noticed him: up late, erasing, adding columns on the back of an envelope, muttering. Since the Civic Auditorium the band's money has gotten complicated: crew pay, per diems, a first royalty statement from Southern Star that says they're unrecouped by $11,340, record sales at shows, Eddie's expenses, van repairs, the band fund. Something's off by $270 and Cal can't find it.

Dean looks at the ledger because he's awake and it's there.

### The puzzle

This is a real, small puzzle: two facing pages of Cal's entries for October and early November, and a reconciliation at the bottom that doesn't match the bank statement tucked into the back. The player can solve it any way they like: add columns, compare entries against receipts paper-clipped to the page.

Dean doesn't do it that way. If the player hesitates, he says it out loud to nobody:

**DEAN:** Two-seventy. Divides by nine.

The trick his father taught him at the dinner table when he was eleven: if an error divides evenly by nine, somebody transposed two digits. The player looks for a pair of numbers that could be each other backward, and finds it: *10/24 — Macon, gross $1,630.00*, and on the deposit slip, *$1,360.00*.

Then, once the player has found that one, Dean keeps going on his own, faster than the player could: he catches that Eddie was reimbursed twice for the same $14.60 gas receipt (an honest mistake), rebuilds the per-diem totals into a schedule on a clean page, and projects, in pencil in the margin, how many more records they'd have to sell before Southern Star ever pays them a royalty. (The number is large.)

He makes each correction properly: one line through the wrong figure, the right figure written above, his initials beside it. *D.H.* The way his father does it.

Then he turns back through the book. October 1974. The first Friday: *10/18/74 — Marlon's — Friday — $40.00.* Pages of it. And his own name, again and again: *Dean — owes fund $20.00. Dean — owes fund $15.00. Dean — adv. against share $30.00.* He adds them up without meaning to. $340.

He has eleven dollars.

He writes, under the last one, in pen: *D.H. — to be paid in full.* He underlines it.

### Coffee

Stutter. The switch goes to Cal, waking up.

5:10 a.m. The smell of coffee. Cal puts his glasses on. Dean is at the kitchen table with two mugs.

**DEAN:** Morning, Dad.

Cal comes over. He sees the ledger. He reads. He turns the page, and turns it back. The corrections in a neater hand than his. The initials. The per-diem schedule. The Macon transposition, circled.

**CAL:** You found the two-seventy.

**DEAN:** Divides by nine.

**CAL:** What?

**DEAN:** If the mistake divides by nine it's a transposition. Everybody knows that.

**CAL:** I didn't know that.

**DEAN:** Richard knows that.

Cal looks at him for a long time. Dean is grinding his teeth. His pupils are big in the lamplight at five in the morning. Cal sees all of it, and the player sees Cal see it.

**CAL:** Were you up all night?

**DEAN:** I'm always up all night.

**CAL:** Where'd you learn this?

**DEAN:** At the dinner table. For eight years. Pretending not to listen.

He drinks his coffee.

**DEAN:** Don't tell anybody.

**CAL:** Why?

**DEAN:** Because then they'll expect it.

Cal thinks about that longer than it deserves.

**CAL:** Okay.

**DEAN:** Promise.

**CAL:** I said okay.

### Sundays

Cal goes to shave. Dean, from the table, through the bathroom door:

**DEAN:** Where do you go on Sundays?

Cal's razor stops.

**DEAN:** You were gone all day Sunday. You're gone every Sunday. Tully says you go to Laurel City.

**CAL:** I visit somebody.

**DEAN:** A girl?

Cal shaves.

**CAL:** Somebody.

**DEAN** *(delighted)*: Dad's got a girlfriend.

Cal doesn't answer. The player, as Cal, can't make him. The razor goes on.

> **Replay layer.** Cal doesn't lie; he withholds. He also didn't correct Dean. In Chapter VIII, after Ellis says the worst thing he ever says, Dean will bring Cal a coffee and say, "For the record, I figured you were just boring."

> **Object.** The ledger now has Dean's initials in it, eleven times, and one line in Dean's hand: *to be paid in full.* It's never paid. The epilogue.

## MISSION 12 — ROOM 614

**Playable:** Ellis · **Where:** the Monroe Hotel, Richmond, Virginia (off-map) · **When:** Saturday November 15, 1:40 – 2:30 a.m.; Sunday morning · **Length:** ~20 min

### The sixth floor

The Richmond run: Charlotte Thursday, Raleigh Friday, Richmond Saturday. Tomorrow's Norfolk, where Riley's brother is stationed and has promised to bring half his ship.

The Monroe is a 1920s hotel downtown that's been going quietly to seed since the war: a lobby with a dry fountain, a bellman who's seventy, a sixth floor with long hallways of dark carpet and a single ice machine at the end by a window that looks at a brick wall. Eddie got four rooms for the price of three because the manager's daughter likes "Still Here." Riley's in 608. Ellis and Dean in 610. Cal and Tully in 612. Theo in 614.

After the show, after the diner, after Dean's fallen asleep in his clothes with the television on, the player is Ellis, in the hallway, at 1:40 a.m., in socks, walking toward 608.

The line of light under Riley's door is on. She's waiting up. This is what they do on the road.

### The radiator

Clara is sitting on the radiator under the window at the end of the hall by the ice machine, with her boots up on the ice machine, blocking nothing, sitting in his way anyway.

**CLARA:** Where are you going?

**ELLIS** *(quietly)*: Bed.

**CLARA:** That's not your room.

**ELLIS:** Go to sleep.

**CLARA:** I don't sleep.

He keeps walking. She says it to his back, just loud enough:

**CLARA:** She doesn't know you.

He stops.

**ELLIS:** She knows me.

**CLARA:** She knows what you told her in a field. That's not the same thing.

**ELLIS:** That's what knowing somebody is.

**CLARA:** Is it.

The player can walk on (the prompt to Riley's door is there, glowing) or turn around. The game lets the player go to the door. If they do, Ellis stands at 608 with his knuckles an inch from the wood, and Clara says, from the end of the hall, "Go ahead. Tell her about the car," and his hand drops, and he turns around. Every path turns him around.

He walks back down to the ice machine. He's angry now, the quiet kind.

**ELLIS:** What do you want?

**CLARA:** I want you to stop pretending they know you. Sixteen hundred people sang my words and not one of them knows who they were singing to.

**ELLIS:** So?

**CLARA:** So they don't know you.

A pause. She looks at the door with the light under it.

**CLARA:** Neither does Riley.

It's the first cruel thing she has ever said to him. The game doesn't underline it; there's no music. It sits in the hallway.

**ELLIS** *(too loud)*: She knows me better than you do.

**CLARA:** Nobody knows you better than me.

**ELLIS:** You're not even—

He stops. He doesn't know how the sentence ends. He's never known.

**CLARA:** Not even what?

He doesn't answer.

### The girl with the bucket

A sound behind him. The player turns the camera.

At the other end of the hallway, by the stairwell door, there's a girl of about seventeen in a Blakes T-shirt from tonight's merch table with an empty ice bucket in her hands. She's not a guest. She came up the stairs because the manager's daughter told her which floor. She's been standing there long enough.

She's looking at Ellis. At the end of the hallway where he's been talking. At the radiator, which has no one on it.

**GIRL:** Sorry. I was just...

She sets the bucket down on the carpet very carefully, as if it might go off, and goes through the stairwell door, fast. Her footsteps go down six flights.

The bucket stays in the middle of the hall.

Clara is still on the radiator.

> **Consequence.** The girl's name is Denise Allard. She tells her friends, who tell theirs. In Chapter VIII a letter appears in *Rave*'s reader mail from a girl in Richmond who says she saw Ellis Blake in a hotel hallway at two in the morning having a fight with nobody. The magazine prints it under the headline SPACE DEATH, INDEED. It's the first public crack, and it's a joke.

### 614

A door opens behind him, farther down the hall. 614.

Cal comes out in his socks, carrying his shoes. Theo is in the doorway behind him in an undershirt. Theo says something too low to hear, and puts his hand on the side of Cal's face, just for a second, and Cal laughs, quietly, a laugh the player has never heard from him. Theo closes the door.

Cal turns and sees Ellis at the end of the hall by the ice machine.

Neither of them moves.

The player, as Ellis, gets nothing. No prompt. Ellis just stands there with his hands at his sides, and so does Cal, with his shoes in his hand.

**CAL:** Ice machine working?

**ELLIS:** Somebody left a bucket.

Cal nods. He goes into 612 and closes the door.

Ellis stands in the hallway. He looks at the radiator. Clara's gone. He looks at 608. The light under Riley's door is still on.

He goes back to 610 and lies down next to the television and doesn't sleep. The player can watch the clock on the nightstand. At 3:10, if they go look (the game lets Ellis walk back out into the hall), the light under 608 has gone out.

### Breakfast

Sunday morning. The coffee shop off the Monroe lobby: red stools, a counter, Sunday papers, Tully reading the sports. Cal is in a booth by himself with coffee and eggs he isn't eating. He's braced. The player can see it in his shoulders.

Ellis comes in and sits down across from him.

Nobody says anything. A waitress pours Ellis coffee. The player can let the silence go as long as they want; Cal won't break it.

Then:

**ELLIS:** He seems all right.

Cal looks at him.

**CAL:** He is.

**ELLIS:** Can I have the ketchup.

Cal passes the ketchup.

Riley comes in a minute later with wet hair and sits down next to Cal. She looks at Ellis.

**RILEY:** You didn't come by.

**ELLIS:** I fell asleep.

She looks at him a moment longer than she needs to. Then she takes his toast.

> **Knowledge.** Ellis now knows about Cal and Theo. He tells nobody, including Riley, who already knew. Cal knows that Ellis knows. Riley doesn't know that Ellis knows, and she knows Ellis lied about falling asleep, and doesn't know why. Nobody knows about the girl with the bucket except Ellis and, soon, Richmond.

> **Setup.** "He seems all right" is the kindest thing Ellis says in this chapter, and he'll take it back in the cruelest way possible in Chapter VIII: "At least mine isn't somebody I keep in a hotel room." The player who was in this hallway will know exactly which hotel room.

## MISSION 13 — WHO THE HELL IS ELLIS BLAKE?

**Playable:** relay (Dean → Riley → Cal → Ellis) · **Where:** a newsstand on the Row; Cutler Street; Vale Music; the Ridge Pharmacy and Main Street, Hollow Ridge · **When:** Tuesday November 25 · **Length:** ~25 min

The January 1976 issue of *Rave* goes on sale on Tuesday, November 25, 1975. The cover is somebody much more famous. Down the left side, in yellow type:

> **WHO THE HELL IS ELLIS BLAKE?**
> **Dex Lundgren in the Georgia Hills**

Six pages inside. Photographs from the Civic Auditorium, all of Ellis. In one of them the rest of the band is visible at the edge of the frame. In the others they've been cropped out.

The mission is a relay. Each member finds the article in their own place, and the player reads it with them: the passages that concern them, in the magazine's typeface, with the interview answers the player chose in Mission 8 appearing in it. Nothing is misquoted. Everything is selected.

### Dean

A newsstand on the Row. Dean, on foot now, buys six copies with most of his remaining money and reads it on the sidewalk, and for the first page he's the happiest man in Tannersville.

The article opens with him:

> *"Blake's a genius, man," says Dean Holloway, the Blakes' drummer, a curly-headed Tannersville rich kid with a chipped tooth, a Hawaiian shirt in November and a film canister of what he calls vitamins. "I'm not kidding. He's the real thing. The rest of us are just trying to keep up."*
> *He's right about the first part.*

Dean laughs out loud on the sidewalk. Every Dean quote is in it, exactly as said: *space death* or *a train falling off a mountain* or *loud*, whichever the player chose; the story about the kick drum ("*It means we touched the drum*"). Dean is the only one of the four Dex quoted at length, because Dean talks in headlines.

Then the player turns the page with him and it's a full-page photograph of Ellis alone at the Civic, backlit, head down, and under it, in large italic type, a pull-quote:

> *"The rest of us are just trying to keep up."*

Dean stops laughing. He reads it twice. He didn't say it like that. He said exactly that.

He keeps five copies and gives the sixth to a man at the bus stop.

### Riley

Cutler Street. Riley at her kitchen table with the copy Dean left in her mailbox with a note on it (*YOU'RE IN IT*), Gerald on the windowsill, tea going cold.

Her paragraph:

> *And then there's Riley (just Riley), the band's lone woman, a college girl in wire-rims with a twelve-string and a comeback for everything. "One of us isn't a boy," she told me, when I asked what it was like to be the girl in the band.*

If the player had her give the joke answer, that's the quote. If the player had her talk about voice-leading and her mother and the drone for two minutes, the paragraph instead reads:

> *…a college girl whose mother plays the organ in a Presbyterian church, which may explain the choirboy harmonies she lays down under Blake's voice.*

And that's all of it. Two minutes about counterpoint became a clause about her mother. If the player had her say *I'm the one who can read music*, it's there, word for word, followed by: *Blake can't, which tells you something about what matters.*

"Sunday Clothes" is mentioned once: *the one Riley sings.*

Riley reads it all the way through. She reads the part about Grace (below). She sits for a while. Then she gets up and gets the scissors from the drawer, and the scrapbook of reviews from under the bed, and cuts it out, all six pages, carefully, and pastes it in. The player can make her stop. If they do, she puts the scissors down, and then picks them up again five seconds later and does it anyway. She pastes it in.

### Cal

Vale Music. Cal at the counter on his lunch break with the magazine flat on the glass case. Mr. Vale reading over his shoulder, which he's never done in four years.

> *"It's ours," says bassist Cal Mercer, with the patience of a man who has said it before. He's wrong, and he knows it. This is Ellis Blake's band the way the weather is God's.*

Cal reads it twice. The player can see his pen tap twice on the glass, the thing he does before he speaks. He doesn't speak.

**VALE:** Hm.

**CAL:** What?

Mr. Vale doesn't answer. He goes back to the repair bench. After a minute, from the bench, without turning around:

**VALE:** I played in a territory band for eleven years. Nobody ever wrote down the name of anybody but the man in front. I don't remember his name now. I remember the bass player's.

Cal goes into the back room and fixes a radio that nobody asked him to fix.

### Ellis

Hollow Ridge. Ellis walks into the Ridge Pharmacy for a pack of Winstons at 4 p.m. and the soda counter goes quiet.

On the wire magazine rack by the register, where the Ridge Pharmacy has never carried *Rave* in its life, there are three copies left out of ten. Mr. Ridge ordered them special from the distributor in Laurel City when he heard. Everybody on Main Street already has one.

**MR. RIDGE:** You're in it.

**ELLIS:** So I hear.

He pays for the cigarettes. He pays for a copy. He takes it out to the Valiant and reads it in the lot with the engine running for the heater.

The player reads it with him. The passages already seen, and then the part the whole town already read:

> *Blake doesn't talk about his family. He doesn't have to. In Hollow Ridge, everybody talks about it for him. His mother left when he was nine. His younger sister, Grace, was killed in the spring of 1973 on a mountain road outside town, in a car her brother was driving. She was fourteen. Three people told me this in one afternoon: the barber, a woman at the pharmacy counter, and a man buying seed at the feed store, and all three told it kindly, the way you'd tell a stranger which road to take.*
>
> *Who is Clara? "Old friend," he says, and he says it the way men say the names of women who are married or dead. "This one's for Clara," he told sixteen hundred people in Laurel City, and sixteen hundred people sang it back to her, whoever she is.*
>
> *I asked him what he wants. He didn't answer for so long I thought the tape had run out.*

(And then whichever the player chose: *"To go home," he said finally. Then he lit a cigarette and looked at me like I'd stolen something.* Or *"Sleep."* Or *"I don't know," which is the most honest thing anyone has said to me this year.* Or, if the player held silence: *He never did answer. I think that was the answer.*)

The last line of the article:

> *The Blakes' debut album is called Borrowed Stone. The cover is a guitar leaning on a blank headstone. Nobody in the band will tell you what it means, and in Hollow Ridge, nobody has to.*

Clara is in the passenger seat. She's been reading along. She puts one finger on the page, on her own name.

**CLARA:** I'm in a magazine.

Ellis doesn't say anything.

**CLARA:** "Whoever she is."

She says it pleased, like a joke. Then she looks at him, and he's not reading anymore. He's looking at the pharmacy window, where three people at the soda counter are pretending not to look back.

**CLARA:** Let's go.

### What he does

The player drives. The game doesn't say where. Options on Main Street, all open:

- **Home.** Empty; Wayne's at work. Ellis puts the magazine in his room, face down, the way he does with things of Grace's.
- **Marlon's.** Marlon has a copy behind the bar, face down. "He came in here. On the Monday. I told him we were closed." / "You weren't closed." / "I was for him." That's all.
- **Poteet's barbershop.** Gene Poteet, sixty, the barber, the first of Dex's three people. If Ellis goes in, Gene stops sweeping. "I'm sorry, son. He asked me and I told him. I didn't think about it." The player can have Ellis say "It's all right" or nothing. Either way, Ellis sits in the chair, and Gene gives him a haircut he doesn't need, and doesn't charge him, and neither of them says another word. It's how you say *it's all right* in Hollow Ridge.

> **Design note.** Nobody betrayed Ellis. That's what makes this hurt. The town knew, the way small towns know, and a man from Detroit with a tape recorder only had to ask three times. The worst day of Ellis's life is now part of his biography in a national magazine, next to the word *genius*, and every stranger who ever meets him from now on will know it before he says hello.
> **Downstream.** Wayne reads it on Wednesday. The player never sees him read it. In Chapter IX, in the glovebox of the F-100 with every clipping about the band, the *Rave* article is folded open to the paragraph about Grace. In Dayton, Ohio, a woman named Lorraine Hubbard reads it on Friday in a grocery-store checkout line (Mission 19).

## MISSION 14 — THE EXTRA CHAIR

**Playable:** Riley · **Where:** the Riley house, Linwood, Laurel City · **When:** Thanksgiving, Thursday November 27, noon – midnight · **Length:** ~35 min

### Why he's here

Riley invited Ellis in October. He said maybe. On Tuesday night, after the article, Joan called Riley at Cutler Street and said, "Bring him," and hung up, which is not like Joan.

Wayne works Thanksgiving; the railroad doesn't stop, and the Blakes haven't done Thanksgiving since 1972 anyway. Ellis told Riley, "We don't really do it," the way you'd say you don't really do golf.

So Ellis is at the Rileys' for the long weekend. He'll sleep in Tommy's room. (Tommy's on his ship.) Tom has already made two jokes about the hallway floorboards and which ones squeak.

### The table

The dining room in Linwood. The leaf in the table. The good china with the gold rim that has been rubbed half off by forty years of washing. The player, as Riley, helps set it and knows exactly where everything goes.

Seven places. At the table: Tom, Joan, Riley, Ellis; Joan's sister, Aunt Frances, a widow and a talker; old Mr. Halvorsen from next door, eighty-one, invited every year since his wife died. And at the corner by the sideboard, a seventh place: a plate, a napkin, a glass, silverware, and an empty chair.

**ELLIS:** Who's that for?

**RILEY:** Nobody. Whoever.

**JOAN** *(bringing the rolls)*: My mother always set a place for whoever might come. You never know who's going to be on the road on a holiday.

**ELLIS:** Does anybody ever come?

**JOAN:** Mr. Halvorsen did, once.

**MR. HALVORSEN:** Nineteen sixty-eight. I've been coming ever since. They've never gotten rid of me.

### Dinner

A long, warm, playable dinner. The player, as Riley, can join conversations or rescue people from them, pass things, look at people. It's the ordinary made precious, and it's funny.

- **Tom** tries a pun on Ellis at the first possible moment: *"Ellis, do you know why the turkey joined the band? Because it had the drumsticks."* Ellis, deadpan: *"That's terrible, Mr. Riley."* Tom is thrilled. Nobody in his family has groaned properly in years. They get along from that moment.
- **Aunt Frances** asks Ellis whether he's "the one in the magazine," and Riley kicks her under the table, and Aunt Frances says "*Ow*, Margaret," and the subject changes.
- **Mr. Halvorsen** tells Ellis about riding the train through Hollow Ridge in 1922, when the depot had a lunchroom and you could get a whole chicken for a quarter. Ellis is genuinely interested. He asks about the lunchroom. Riley watches him be good with an old man.
- **Joan** gives Ellis the drumstick without asking. She refills his iced tea before it's empty. She never mentions the magazine. The player, as Riley, notices every one of these things.

Then:

**JOAN:** Is your mother...?

**ELLIS:** She's in Ohio.

**JOAN:** Oh.

A small pause.

**JOAN:** And your father's working today?

**ELLIS:** Yes ma'am. Railroad doesn't stop.

**JOAN:** No. It doesn't.

Riley flinches at *She's in Ohio*. The player may know why: it's Wayne's sentence, the only one he ever gave his children about their mother, and Ellis says it in his father's exact rhythm.

### The chair

The camera is Riley's all through dinner, which means the chair at the corner by the sideboard is empty all through dinner.

But Ellis keeps glancing at it. Once when the turkey comes in. Once when Aunt Frances says something about young people these days. Once, during grace (Tom's, short, with a pun in it), when everybody's head is bowed, the player can have Riley open her eyes and look, and Ellis's eyes are open, and he's looking at the empty chair, and there's the corner of a smile on his face, like somebody at a table who has just seen a friend do something rude and funny.

The player knows what Riley knows. Nobody else at the table notices a thing.

> **Replay layer.** From Ellis's side, Clara is in the seventh chair, in her jacket, with her boots up on the rung of the chair and a roll she isn't eating. Joan's mother set a place for whoever might come. Somebody came.

### The article in the hall

After dinner, Riley goes to the hall closet for Mr. Halvorsen's coat and finds her father's copy of *Rave* on the hall table under the mail. Tom has read it. In pencil, in the margin next to *the band's lone woman*, in the careful block capitals of a man who has graded chemistry lab reports for twenty-six years, he's written:

> HARMONY IS NOT DECORATION

and underlined it twice.

Riley stands in the hall with it. The player can have her laugh, or not. Either way she puts it back exactly where it was.

### The piano

The dishes. Joan washes; Riley dries; the radio on low; Mr. Halvorsen asleep in Tom's recliner in front of the football; Tom and Aunt Frances arguing about the Bicentennial.

Ellis drifts into the living room. The upright piano. On the music rack, under the Presbyterian hymnal and a book of Bach chorales, an oblong maroon songbook with a spine the player might recognize if they've looked closely at a shelf in the Blake house: *The Sacred Harp.*

Ellis picks it up. He turns pages. He doesn't have to look for anything; he knows where things are in it.

Joan comes in from the kitchen, drying her hands.

**JOAN:** Do you know those?

**ELLIS:** Some. Most of them, I reckon.

**JOAN:** I went to a singing once. Nineteen sixty-eight, in Alabama. A church in a field. I bought the book at the table and I've never been able to make them work.

**ELLIS:** They don't work on piano.

**JOAN:** Then how do they work?

He thinks about it.

**ELLIS:** Loud.

Joan laughs, surprised.

**ELLIS:** You sing the shapes first. Then the words.

**JOAN:** Show me.

The player, as Riley, is in the kitchen doorway with the dish towel. She's been here before, in September, watching her mother at this piano. The camera holds on her.

Ellis opens the book to a page he doesn't need to look at. He sings the tenor line, which in this music is the melody, first in the shape-note syllables his grandmother taught him (*fa sol la fa sol la mi fa*), plain and loud, not performing, the way you'd sing in a field.

Joan leans over the book, and finds the treble line with her finger, and reads it, and on the second time through she sings it with him. She's a trained church musician and she reads fast, and she's never sung like this in her life: open fifths, full voice, nobody to please.

Riley watches her mother light up.

The player can have Riley join them. There's an alto line in the book. If she comes in, it's three parts in a Linwood living room, and on the next time through Ellis switches to the words:

*Brethren, we have met to worship*
*and adore the Lord our God…*

If she doesn't join, she stands in the doorway and watches, and that's complete too.

At the end, Mr. Halvorsen has woken up and is applauding from the recliner. Tom is in the other doorway holding a gravy boat. Joan has her hand flat on the open book, and she's a little out of breath, and she says, to nobody in particular:

**JOAN:** Well.

It's the same *Well* she said on the phone in June when Riley declined Edinburgh. It means something completely different.

> **Design note.** The quietest scene in the chapter. A boy with no mother and a woman with a daughter who's leaving the life she chose for her, singing a hymn from a book neither of their churches uses, in a room where there's an empty chair. Nobody says anything that could be quoted. That's the point.

### Tommy's room

Midnight. Riley in her childhood bedroom (the horse-show ribbons she never won, a Joni Mitchell poster, a map of the constellations on the ceiling in glow-in-the-dark stars her father put up when she was ten). Ellis across the hall in Tommy's room. The house asleep.

The player can stay in bed, or cross the hall. The floorboards Tom warned about are real; the player has to step around them, and if they don't, one of them creaks and they freeze and wait to hear if anyone stirs.

If she crosses: Tommy's room, Navy pennants, a model of the *Enterprise*, the carrier. Ellis is awake, sitting up in a single bed with his back against the wall. She sits on the end of it. They talk in whispers.

**RILEY:** Was she at dinner?

A pause.

**ELLIS:** Yeah.

**RILEY:** Where?

**ELLIS:** The chair.

**RILEY:** The extra one.

**ELLIS:** Your mama set a place.

Riley puts her hand over her mouth so the laugh won't wake anybody. Then it isn't a laugh anymore, and she keeps her hand there.

**RILEY:** Did she behave?

**ELLIS:** She put her feet on the rung.

**RILEY:** Mama would've hated that.

**ELLIS:** She knew.

A long quiet. Somewhere downstairs a clock.

**RILEY:** We said we'd talk to somebody.

**ELLIS:** You said.

**RILEY:** Ellis.

**ELLIS:** She sat in a chair, Riley. She didn't do anything.

The player chooses what Riley does with that: push (*"That's not the point"*), let it go, or say the true thing (*"I'm not scared of her. I'm scared of what happens to you."*). Whichever the player picks, Ellis's answer is the same, very tired:

**ELLIS:** After New York.

**RILEY:** After New York what?

**ELLIS:** After New York I'll talk to somebody.

She knows it's a promise he doesn't intend to keep. She takes it anyway, because he has never put this in the future tense before.

She goes back across the hall. She misses a floorboard. It creaks. From Tom and Joan's room, very faintly, Tom's voice: *"Goodnight, Maggie."*

> **Tracked.** "After New York" is the second promise in the game Ellis makes knowing he might not keep it (the first was "Promise," to Clara, in Chapter IV). Riley brings it up in Chapter VIII.

## MISSION 15 — HOLD THE LIGHT

**Playable:** Ellis → Riley · **Where:** US 19 in the Laurel Gap; the Blake house · **When:** Sunday November 30, 9:40 – 11:55 p.m. · **Length:** ~15 min

### Rain

The drive back from Linwood. Riley's Datsun, Riley driving, Ellis in the passenger seat, the leftovers Joan packed in a grocery sack at his feet. It started raining at the county line and it's coming down hard now on the two-lane through the Laurel Gap, the wipers not keeping up.

The player is Ellis, and there's nothing to do but be a passenger. The game gives the player the passenger camera, and Ellis's right hand on the door handle, and his left forearm, which he's rubbing without knowing it (it aches before rain; it's been aching since Linwood). Every set of oncoming headlights, the camera tightens a little.

Riley notices. She slows down from fifty to forty without saying anything. He notices her notice. Neither of them mentions it.

### The dip

A low place in the road a few miles east of the Starlite where the ditch floods. A pulpwood truck comes the other way fast and throws a sheet of water across the Datsun's hood, and the engine coughs, and catches, and coughs, and dies.

Riley coasts it onto the shoulder. Flashers on. Rain on the roof like gravel.

**RILEY:** Well, shit.

**ELLIS:** Pop the hood.

**RILEY:** It's pouring.

**ELLIS:** It's wet. That's the whole problem. Pop the hood.

He gets out into it.

### The engine

The diagnostic system from Roy's garage, in the rain, at night, by flashlight: listen (Riley cranks it; it turns over fine and won't catch), smell (no gasoline; not flooded), look.

**ELLIS:** Get the flashlight out of the glovebox. Come here.

She comes around with the flashlight and her father's peacoat over her head.

The player finds it: water inside the distributor cap, which on this Datsun sits low and forward where the splash hits it. Ellis unclips the cap. He needs something dry. The player can use his shirt tail (soaked), Joan's dish towel from the leftovers sack (the right answer, and the player may think of it), or the peacoat lining (Riley will object).

**ELLIS:** Hold the light.

She holds it. It wanders, because she's watching him instead of the engine.

**ELLIS:** Down. Left. There. Hold it still.

He's drying the inside of the cap, the rotor, the plug wires one at a time, working fast with his whole body hunched over the engine bay to keep the rain off it, the way you'd shield a match.

**ELLIS:** I love you, hold it still.

He doesn't look up. He doesn't hear himself. He keeps working.

The flashlight beam doesn't move a quarter of an inch for the rest of the job.

### High beams

Around the bend, a pickup comes the other way with its high beams on.

The whole engine bay goes white. Ellis's hands stop. He straightens up, too fast, and his wrist hits the radiator hose, and he turns toward the light with the distributor cap in his hand and doesn't move.

The pickup goes by in a hiss of spray. Its taillights go around the bend.

Ellis is still standing there.

She puts her hand flat on his back, through the wet shirt.

**RILEY:** Hey. Ellis.

He comes back. He looks at the cap in his hand like he's not sure how it got there.

**ELLIS:** I'm fine.

He bends back over the engine.

> **Setup.** A pickup with high beams on a two-lane road at night in the rain is what came around Tolliver Bend on April 12, 1973. Ellis doesn't remember that yet. His body does. The game plants the reaction here, small and unexplained, so that when a light hits his eyes on a stage in Chapter X, the player's body remembers it too.

### Switch

Stutter. The camera slides off Ellis and settles on Riley, and the player is holding the flashlight.

From here: the rain, the hood, the back of Ellis's wet shirt, his hands working in the circle of light that she's keeping perfectly still. The player, as Riley, can't move the light (if they try, she doesn't). She's smiling in the rain and trying not to.

The player gets the dialogue grammar:

- **"What did you say?"** Ellis: "Hold it still." / Riley: "Before that." / Ellis, not looking up, after a second: "I don't know. Hold it still." His ears have gone red. He knows.
- **"Ellis."** He says "What," and she says "Nothing," and he says "Then hold it still."
- **Silence.** She holds the light and doesn't say anything, and neither does he.

The distributor cap goes back on. He clips it.

**ELLIS:** Try it.

She gets in and turns the key. It catches on the second try.

### The porch

They drive the rest of the way to Hollow Ridge with the heater on high and the windows fogging and Joan's dish towel (if that's what he used) steaming on the dashboard. Nobody says anything about anything. Riley drives forty.

Cold Branch Road. The Blake house. The one bulb over the porch is on. Wayne's truck is in the yard.

Ellis gets out and leans back down into the open door.

**ELLIS:** Call me when you get home.

**RILEY:** It's an hour.

**ELLIS:** Call me anyway.

She drives off. He stands on the porch until her taillights are gone.

### 11:52

The Blake hall. Ellis, in dry clothes, sitting on the floor next to the telephone table with his back against the wall, not pretending to be doing anything else. Tater asleep across his legs.

The phone rings at 11:52 and he has it before the first ring's over.

**RILEY** *(on the phone)*: I'm home.

**ELLIS:** Okay.

A pause on the line. The player, as Ellis, can say something or not. The options are all small: *"Good."* / *"Was it raining there?"* / silence.

Then Riley. This choice belongs to her, and the game gives it to the player by switching the phone call to her end: Cutler Street, the kitchen, the wall phone, her hair still wet. The options:

- **"I love you too."**
- **"Goodnight, Ellis."**
- **Silence**, until he says goodnight.

Whichever she chooses, the call ends a moment later, and the game switches back to the hall in Hollow Ridge, where Ellis hangs up and sits there a while longer.

Down the hall, Wayne's bedroom door has been open a crack. It closes, quietly.

> **Tracked.** Whether Riley said it back. It isn't mentioned again until the epilogue, in one of the notebook poems addressed to "M.," which differs by one line depending on what she said.

## MISSION 16 — LOUD

**Playable:** Ellis · **Where:** the Blake kitchen; the Lantern, Laurel City · **When:** Wednesday December 3 (kitchen); Friday December 5 (show) · **Length:** ~25 min

### A ticket

Wednesday. The Blake kitchen. Eddie mailed Ellis a strip of four guest tickets for Friday's Lantern show, a homecoming night before New York. The player can put one on the kitchen table on top of the bills pile, on the coupon book, the way they may have left the record in September.

If they do, it's gone in the morning.

If they don't, Wayne comes anyway. (Roy buys two at the Lantern box office on Thursday on his lunch hour and gives one to Wayne at the depot without explanation.)

Either way, the coupon book is under whatever Ellis leaves, and if the player looks: *Coupon 33 of 48.*

### The Lantern

Friday. The Lantern, eight hundred sold, the room that changed them a year ago, their room. It feels smaller than it used to, and louder, and better. The Lantern's new house engineer is at the side, a little in awe of Theo, who's back at his old desk in the middle of the floor mixing his own band.

Clara is in the stage-left wing during the first three songs, sitting on an amp case, kicking her heels against it.

During "Ice Machine," the player can use *attention* to look out past the lights into the room. The back of the Lantern, by Theo's desk.

Wayne Blake is standing there.

In his work khakis. In his hat. A railroad section foreman at a rock show, with eight hundred kids giving him three feet of room in every direction without knowing why. A girl offers him a joint; he looks at it, and at her, and says something the player can't hear that makes her apologize. Theo, without looking up from the board, reaches into a drawer under the desk and holds out two cotton balls. Wayne looks at them. He takes them. He puts one in his left ear and holds the other one in his hand for the rest of the night.

When Ellis looks back at the stage-left wing, Clara is gone.

### "Borrowed Stone"

The set list has "Borrowed Stone" fourth.

With Wayne in the room, the player gets a choice the band will never understand. Before the song, Ellis can lift his headstock toward Cal (the band's *change coming* signal) and call something else in its place. Cal will follow, and he'll look at Ellis afterward, and not know why.

Or Ellis can play it.

If he plays it, the player hears it all the way through, with the lyric that's been in every copy of the record for three months:

*There's a book on the table with the pages torn out,*
*one for every month since the rain.*
*Daddy pays on a stone like you'd pay on a truck.*
*Nineteen more and it's ours.*

With *attention*, the player can look at the back of the room during the chorus. Wayne doesn't move. He's looking at the floor. When the song ends he's still looking at it.

(There are fifteen coupons left, not nineteen. The song was written in August. It's already out of date.)

### Loud

After. The Lantern's loading dock, 12:40 a.m. Wayne's F-100 is parked in the alley between Tully's truck and a dumpster, because Wayne parked where he'd have parked a work truck.

Wayne is leaning on the fender with a Camel. Ellis comes out with a towel around his neck.

**ELLIS:** Well?

**WAYNE:** Loud.

**ELLIS:** Yeah.

A long pause. Wayne smokes.

**WAYNE:** I liked it.

Ellis doesn't know what to do with that. The player gets no prompt for a second, and then gets one, and it's only silence.

**WAYNE:** That drummer.

**ELLIS:** Dean.

**WAYNE:** Your sister would've hated him.

In the whole game Wayne has not mentioned Grace in front of Ellis without anger. He does it now the way you'd mention the weather. Ellis almost laughs.

**ELLIS:** She'd have married him.

**WAYNE:** That's what I mean.

Now Ellis does laugh. Wayne almost does. He looks down the alley.

**WAYNE:** Read your magazine.

**ELLIS:** It's not my magazine.

**WAYNE:** That fellow ever comes to Hollow Ridge again, I'll put him in the creek.

It's said flat, without heat, and it's the most protective thing Wayne has ever said on Ellis's behalf, and the player can feel the anger in it finally pointed somewhere other than at his son.

### The girl

Wayne drops the cigarette and steps on it. He doesn't get in the truck. He's working up to something.

**WAYNE:** That girl.

**ELLIS:** What girl.

**WAYNE:** In the song. The one on the radio.

A pause.

**WAYNE:** She still around?

The player chooses Ellis's answer. The options: *"Sometimes."* / *"Why?"* / *"No."* / silence.

- **"Sometimes."** Wayne nods, slowly, as though that's the answer he expected, and it's the one he was afraid of.
- **"Why?"** Wayne says, "No reason," and it's so obviously a lie that Ellis stares at him.
- **"No."** Wayne looks at him for a long moment and says, "Hm." He doesn't believe it.
- **Silence.** Wayne waits, and then nods as if Ellis had said *sometimes*.

Then Wayne reaches out and fixes his son's collar, where the jacket's folded under from the towel. It takes a second. It's the first time the player has seen Wayne touch Ellis.

**WAYNE:** You eat anything?

**ELLIS:** Not yet.

**WAYNE:** Hm.

He gets in the truck and drives off down the alley.

Later, in the van, Ellis puts his hand in his jacket pocket for his cigarettes and finds a twenty-dollar bill folded in quarters that wasn't there before.

He's made $378 a night since November. Wayne makes about $280 a week.

The player can look at the twenty for as long as they want. Ellis puts it back in the pocket. He doesn't spend it. (It's still there in Chapter X.)

> **Design note.** Wayne leaves money where it can be found. He has done it all game: the heater, the half a tire, the Maxwell House can. Tonight he does it with his hands, in front of his son, disguised as fixing a collar.

## MISSION 17 — TWENTY-ONE DATES

**Playable:** Cal · **Where:** the back room at Vale Music, Tannersville · **When:** Saturday December 6, 2:00 p.m. · **Length:** ~20 min

### The meeting

The back room at Vale Music, where they rehearsed in the winter: the repair bench, the stacked amp boxes, the space heater, Mr. Vale out front with a customer. Eddie called the meeting. All four are here. Tully's in the doorway. Eddie has a folder.

The player is Cal, which means the player sees everything and has the fewest good options.

**EDDIE:** It's done. Sawtooth. Twenty-one dates, January fourteenth through February fifteenth. Detroit, Cleveland, Toledo, Chicago, Milwaukee, the whole winter. Arenas, eight to fifteen thousand. You open. Thirty-five minutes a night. And New York next Friday, which you know about.

Dean stands up.

**DEAN:** *Arenas.*

**RILEY:** Wait. What's done?

**EDDIE:** The deal. It's signed.

**CAL:** Who signed it?

Eddie looks at Ellis.

**EDDIE:** Ellis said yes Tuesday. I called the house. They needed an answer by Wednesday or it went to somebody from Chicago.

Everyone looks at Ellis.

### Tuesday

**RILEY:** When did you say yes?

**ELLIS:** Tuesday.

**RILEY:** It's Saturday.

**ELLIS:** I know what day it is.

**CAL:** We have a rule.

He doesn't have to say which one. It's the one he made, in the van, in April: no band decisions alone.

**ELLIS:** He needed an answer. You were all out. I called Riley. I called Dean at your place. I called you.

A pause.

**ELLIS:** You weren't home. Tuesday.

Cal looks at him. Ellis looks back. The player, as Cal, understands exactly what Ellis could say next, and that he isn't saying it.

The player gets a prompt to press the point anyway (*"You could have waited."*). If they do, Ellis says, "Till when?" and Cal has no answer that doesn't end in Laurel City. If they don't, Cal lets it go. Either way:

**CAL:** Fine. Tuesday.

**DEAN:** Who cares? It's *arenas.* Richard's going to have to read about it in the paper.

### The list

**EDDIE:** Thirty-five minutes. Seven songs, eight if they're short. Ellis already gave me a list.

He reads it off a page in his folder.

**EDDIE:** "No Name." "Low Water." "Borrowed Stone." "Stony Knob." "Still Here." "Ice Machine." "Shape Note," cut down.

A silence in which everyone does the arithmetic of what isn't on it.

**DEAN:** Where's "Tomorrow Problem"?

**ELLIS:** It's a joke.

**DEAN:** It's the one they sing.

**ELLIS:** They sing it because it's a joke.

**RILEY:** Where's "Sunday Clothes"?

**ELLIS:** Thirty-five minutes, Riley.

**RILEY:** "Shape Note" is nine.

**ELLIS:** We'll cut it to six.

**RILEY:** You'd rather play six minutes of drone to fifteen thousand people waiting for Sawtooth than my song.

**ELLIS:** They're not waiting for us. They don't know who we are. We've got thirty-five minutes to make them remember. The real ones do that.

**RILEY:** The real ones.

**ELLIS:** You know what I mean.

**RILEY:** Say what you mean.

The player, as Cal, can intervene here with a practical suggestion (*"We can fit eight if we cut the drone to four."*). If they do, nobody hears it. It doesn't stop anything, and Cal knows it won't as he says it.

**ELLIS:** Mine cost something.

The space heater ticks.

Riley goes very still. Dean looks at the floor. Tully, in the doorway, looks at Ellis and then out at the store.

### The sentence

Now the player gets Cal's choice, and it's a timing choice: speak now, or hold.

If the player holds, Ellis keeps going, because nobody stopped him, and it gets worse:

**ELLIS:** You want to play a song about curfew in a dormitory, fine. Dean wants to yell about his homework, fine. Somebody wrote six pages about what those songs are about, and it wasn't about curfew.

And then Cal speaks anyway.

If the player speaks, Cal says it before Ellis can say the rest. Either way it's the same sentence, said quietly, the way Cal says everything:

**CAL:** Pain doesn't automatically make everything you do important.

Nobody moves.

**ELLIS:** You don't know anything about it.

And here the game gives the player, as Cal, the one prompt that could end the argument: *Tell him what Marlon told you.*

The player can press it. Cal opens his mouth.

**CAL:** I know more than you—

He stops. The prompt dims and goes out. He promised Marlon. The player can press it again and nothing happens.

**CAL:** No. I don't.

Ellis picks up his jacket and walks out through the store. The bell on Vale's front door rings behind him. Mr. Vale, at the counter with a customer, doesn't turn around.

> **Design note.** Cal's sentence is one of the few lines in the game in which a character says something true and clear, and it had to be Cal, and it had to cost him. He's right, and he's being cruel, and he's holding back the one fact that would prove Ellis wrong in a different way, because he gave his word to a man at a bar at two in the morning. The player tried to make him break it and the game didn't let them. On replay, "I know more than you" is the most loaded half-sentence in the chapter.

### After

The back room. The heater ticking. Eddie with his folder.

**EDDIE:** So. Is it a yes?

Nobody answers.

**EDDIE:** I'm going to take that as a yes.

**RILEY:** It's a yes.

Everyone looks at her.

**RILEY:** It's a yes. We're going. But he doesn't get to write the set list.

**CAL:** I'll write it.

**RILEY:** I know you will.

She leaves too, the back way, into the alley.

Dean sits down on an amp box next to Cal. Tully goes out front and asks Mr. Vale about a guitar he doesn't want, to give them the room.

**DEAN** *(quietly)*: He's right about one thing. "Tomorrow Problem" is a joke.

**CAL:** It's a good joke.

Dean thinks about that.

**DEAN:** It's a really good joke.

> **The van promise, first breach.** In April the four of them made four promises in the van. Tonight two of them broke: *no band decisions alone* (Cal's, broken by Ellis on Tuesday) and *no treating each other like employees* (Riley's, broken by Ellis this afternoon, with a set list). The other two break in Chapter VIII.

## MISSION 18 — FOUR ROOMS

**Playable:** relay, Ellis → Cal → Riley → Dean → Ellis · **Where:** Hollow Ridge; east Tannersville; Cutler Street; the Starlite · **When:** Saturday December 6, 11:40 p.m. – 2:10 a.m. · **Length:** ~15 min

### The grammar

Until now every switch has had something to carry it: two people passing, a song moving between radios, a phone line, a van on the road. Tonight the four of them are in four different places across the whole map and not speaking to each other, and the switch has to find a way to cross that distance.

It uses the railroad. One line runs the length of the map, from the Laurel City yards through the Laurel Gap, along the south edge of Hollow Ridge, and through the Tanner Valley to the yard on the east side of Tannersville. Freights run it all night. In each of the four rooms, a train whistle sounds at some point, and on the whistle the camera lets go of one person and finds the next, forty or ninety miles away, where another whistle is sounding on the same line.

> **Design note.** Wayne Blake maintains a stretch of that track. The game never mentions it tonight.

### Ellis

Hollow Ridge, 11:40 p.m. Ellis's room. Wayne asleep. Loretta on the wall. The leather notebook from Riley on the dresser next to the plastic horse, still blank.

Ellis is at the desk with Patty Holloway's violet envelope, which Dean gave him on the Richmond run three weeks ago and which he's read enough times that the fold has gone soft.

The player can read it.

> *Dear Ellis,*
> *Dean doesn't know I'm writing this. If he's reading it over your shoulder, hi Dean, you're an idiot.*
> *I listen to "No Name" a lot. The one about the kitchen light. I know it isn't about my brother. But since he moved out Mama leaves the porch light on all night, every night, and Daddy turns it off when he goes up and she comes back down and turns it on again, and neither of them says anything.*
> *I just wanted to ask: in the song, do they come back? You never say.*
> *Patty Holloway*
> *P.S. I beat you at pinball fair and square.*

Then the player writes Ellis's answer with him. It's the same line-by-line interaction as the songwriting system, choosing among phrasings, but the sentences here are plainer than anything Ellis writes in a song. The central choice is whether to tell her the truth about the song.

If he tells her:

> *It's about my mother. She left when I was nine. She didn't come back. I wrote it when I was sixteen and I don't know if the person in the song comes back either. I'm sorry that's not a better answer.*
> *Your brother's different. He's got nowhere else to eat on Sundays.*

If he doesn't:

> *I don't know. I never finished figuring that out.*
> *Your brother will come back. He's got nowhere else to eat on Sundays.*

Either way it ends:

> *Tell your mama to leave the light on.*
> *You did not beat me fair and square. The left flipper sticks.*
> *Ellis*

Clara is on the floor with her back against the bed, reading it upside down.

**CLARA:** You were right today.

He keeps writing.

**CLARA:** At the music store. About the songs. You don't need them.

He stops. He looks at her.

**ELLIS:** Yes I do.

Clara doesn't answer. She looks at the letter. After a while she starts humming, low, under nothing.

Below the town, a freight blows for the Depot Street crossing.

> **Setup.** "Yes I do" is Ellis choosing the band over Clara, out loud. It's small and he says it annoyed. In Chapter X he turns toward the living on a stage. It starts at a desk in December with a letter to a sixteen-year-old.

### Cal

The whistle carries the camera forty-five miles east along the line to the rail yard in east Tannersville, where a yard engine is shoving cars, and to the lit window of Mercer Radio & TV across the street from it.

Midnight. Cal at his father's bench. He came here because Dean's asleep on the cot at his place and he couldn't be in that room. Walt Mercer came down the stairs in his bathrobe because he saw the light, and has been sitting on the other stool for forty minutes without asking anything, handing Cal tools before Cal asks for them.

The radio on the bench is tuned to whatever the customer left it on, as a matter of principle. It's a country station: a Row record with strings.

The player can work on the radio (a Philco with a bad filter capacitor) with Walt. They don't talk. The player can try to make Cal say something; the options are all things Cal would never say out loud (*"I said something true to somebody I love and I meant it to hurt."*) and if chosen, Cal opens his mouth, and closes it, and asks for the long-nose pliers instead. Walt hands them over.

After a long time, Walt says:

**WALT:** Your mother read the magazine.

**CAL:** Yeah.

**WALT:** She was mad.

**CAL:** At who?

**WALT:** The man that wrote it.

A pause. Walt solders a joint.

**WALT:** You want to stay here tonight? Your room's made up.

**CAL:** Yeah.

**WALT:** She'll make pancakes.

**CAL:** I know.

The Philco comes on under their hands, warming up, the dial light first and then the sound. Across the street a yard engine blows two shorts.

### Riley

The whistle carries it three miles west across the city to the river side of Tannersville, where a southbound freight is crossing the trestle below the college, and up to a second-floor kitchen on Cutler Street.

1:00 a.m. Riley at her kitchen table with her mother's Martin and a spiral notebook. Gerald on the windowsill. Tea gone cold.

She's writing a song, and it's about neither Ellis nor her mother. The first line on the page, in her handwriting, is the phrase from the inside cover of her notebook: *Occasionally astonishing.* The player can work on it with her, picking chords (she's in open D; the player can hear why) and moving words around. It's a song about being the thing people notice only sometimes, and it's funny, and it's angry in a way she can't be out loud yet.

> **Setup.** "Occasionally Astonishing" is the first song on Riley's first solo record, in 1979. The player is writing its first verse at one in the morning in December 1975.

At some point the player can have her stop and pick up the phone and dial the Blake house. It rings three times in Hollow Ridge. A sleep-thick voice answers: "Hm?" It's Wayne. Riley holds the receiver and doesn't say anything. "Hello?" He says it twice. She hangs up.

She writes the second line.

Down at the river, the freight's horn goes on and on across the trestle.

### Dean

The horn carries west again, sixty miles, back through the Tanner Valley and past Hollow Ridge and into the Laurel Gap, where a westbound freight is rolling past the back of the Starlite so close the sugar dispensers shake.

2:00 a.m. Dean at the counter. Tully brought him, and is asleep in the truck. Dean's been here since midnight. His jaw is working. He's drumming on the counter with two spoons.

Lynette on shift, refilling the ketchups.

**LYNETTE:** You're grinding your teeth.

**DEAN:** It's a rhythm.

**LYNETTE:** Go home.

**DEAN:** I don't have one.

**LYNETTE:** Go to Cal's.

**DEAN:** Cal's at his mama's.

She sets down the ketchups.

**LYNETTE:** Then sit there till six and I'll drive you.

He stops drumming.

**DEAN:** Can I ask you something?

**LYNETTE:** You're going to.

**DEAN:** Is "Tomorrow Problem" a joke?

She looks at him like he's asked if the sky is up.

**LYNETTE:** It's a song about not doing your homework.

**DEAN:** Yeah.

**LYNETTE:** Bobby sings it.

Dean looks up.

**DEAN:** He does?

**LYNETTE:** In the bathtub. He doesn't know the words. He just yells the tomorrow part. Every night. I have to hear it every night, Dean. Thanks for that.

Dean doesn't say anything. For once. He puts the spoons down on the counter, side by side.

The freight goes by outside and the whole diner hums with it.

### Ellis

The last whistle carries east, a dozen miles, back to Hollow Ridge, to the same Depot Street crossing as the first, and up Cold Branch Road to the lit window.

2:10 a.m. Ellis has finished Patty's letter and addressed it. Clara's gone. He's writing on the back of a Western Auto receipt, because he doesn't tear pages out of his notebooks. It's a set list, thirty-five minutes, and the player can see it:

> NO NAME
> LOW WATER
> SUNDAY CLOTHES

## MISSION 19 — NORTH

**Playable:** all four (rotating) · **Where:** the van, I-81 through Virginia (off-map); New York City (off-map): a hotel on lower Lexington Avenue, a steakhouse in midtown, a showcase club on Bleecker Street, a bar on the Bowery, a secondhand store on St. Marks Place · **When:** Tuesday December 9 – Saturday December 13 · **Length:** ~60 min

### The receipt

Tuesday, 3:30 p.m., the alley behind Vale Music. The van and Tully's truck, loaded. Theo riding with Tully. They haven't all been in one place since Saturday, and nobody's mentioned Saturday.

The player is Cal, at the wheel, checking mirrors.

Ellis climbs in last, and before he goes to the wheel well, he leans between the front seats and puts the folded Western Auto receipt on the dashboard in front of Cal.

Cal unfolds it and reads it. Then he reaches into his shirt pocket, where he keeps his own list, and unfolds that, and puts them side by side on the dash. The player can see both.

They're the same seven songs. Cal's is in a different order. Cal's cuts "Shape Note" to five minutes, not four.

Cal folds Ellis's receipt and puts it in the ledger. He puts his own back in his pocket.

**CAL:** Five minutes.

**ELLIS:** Four.

**CAL:** Five.

**ELLIS:** Whatever Cal says.

In the back, Dean, who has seen *TOMORROW PROBLEM* on the dash from his bench, starts drumming on the wall, and Riley, in the passenger seat, who has seen *SUNDAY CLOTHES*, opens her crossword and says to it:

**RILEY:** Occasionally astonishing.

Cal pulls out of the alley.

### Night driving

North through the Tanner Valley and off the edge of the map. The travel transition: the corridor, the state line, the sign. Then Virginia: the long valley between the ridges, I-81, dark by six.

A truck stop near Wytheville at 11 p.m. Cal has driven eight hours. He's falling asleep at the pumps.

**ELLIS:** I'll drive.

Cal looks at him. The player, as Cal, has the choice, and it's the most loaded choice Cal gets in the chapter, because Cal knows what Ellis was driving the last time he drove someone at night.

- **Give him the keys.**
- **"I'm fine."** Ellis waits. Cal nearly falls asleep standing up. Riley says, "Cal. Let him." The prompt comes back with one option in it.

Cal gives him the keys.

The switch goes to Ellis inside the truck stop, paying for gas. A trucker at the counter, a big man in a quilted vest, sees the van and the Georgia plates and the look on Ellis's face, and slides something across the counter folded in a paper napkin.

**TRUCKER:** Four hundred miles?

**ELLIS:** About.

**TRUCKER:** Stay awake, stay alive.

Two small white tablets, scored with a cross.

The player can take them or leave them. If Ellis takes one, the drive that follows is sharp and bright and loud inside his head, the radio vivid, his thoughts a little too fast. If he leaves them ("I'm all right"), the trucker says "Suit yourself," and the drive that follows has a drowsiness system: the road drifts, the lane lines swim, and the player has to catch it. Neither changes the story. The game keeps a record.

### Keep going

I-81 at night in December. Dry, cold, clear. Stars over the ridges. The heater that only works on the passenger side. The van at exactly fifty-five miles an hour, both of Ellis's hands on the wheel.

Behind him: Dean asleep on his bench with his mouth open. Cal asleep sitting up on the amplifiers in back, arms folded. Riley asleep in the passenger seat against the window with her crossword on her chest. Tully's taillights a quarter-mile ahead.

On the engine cover between the two front seats (the doghouse, the Econoline's hump), Clara is sitting with her boots up on the dashboard.

She's quiet for a long time. The player drives. This is one of the longest uninterrupted drives in the game, around eight real minutes, and the game lets it be long.

**CLARA:** You're driving.

**ELLIS** *(barely audible)*: Yeah.

**CLARA:** At night.

**ELLIS:** It's not raining.

**CLARA:** You've got all of them in here.

He looks in the mirror at Dean's open mouth. At Riley.

**ELLIS:** Yeah.

Clara looks at the road.

**CLARA:** Keep going.

> **Design note.** Clara says "keep going" and "stay" and "come back." She never says "go on." When she says "keep going," it's always in a car.

At 3:12 a.m., somewhere past Harrisonburg, the player can tune the radio (the van's FM converter, the one Vale installed). A station out of Washington, faint, a late-night DJ with a soft voice:

**DJ** *(on the radio)*: …a band out of Georgia I don't know one thing about, except that my phone won't stop ringing when I play it. So here it is again.

"Still Here," through one speaker in the dash, four hundred miles from home, in a state none of them has ever set foot in.

Ellis reaches over and turns it down, so it won't wake them.

### The skyline

Dawn on the New Jersey Turnpike: refineries, gas flares, marsh, gulls, a gray sky, and then, all at once, off to the right across the flats, Manhattan.

Dean wakes up and sees it and screams. Everybody wakes up. Riley's crossword goes on the floor. Cal says "What? What?" Dean is pointing out the window with both hands and can't form words.

**RILEY:** Oh my God.

**CAL:** Is that it?

**DEAN:** *That's it!*

Ellis, driving, doesn't look for a long time, because he's driving. Then he does.

The Lincoln Tunnel. Tile walls, fumes, the long curve. Then light, and they're in it.

### The city

New York is an off-map mission space: a dense, compact slice of lower Manhattan, Midtown and the streets between, playable on foot, loud, cold, enormous, full of people who don't look at anybody. The player can walk it as any of the four during free time across Wednesday and Thursday. A few of the things that happen:

- **The hotel.** The Albemarle, lower Lexington Avenue, cheap, radiators that bang all night. The desk clerk reads GEORGIA on Ellis's registration card and hums the banjo part from *Deliverance*. Dean hums the guitar part back, perfectly, and the clerk laughs and gives them a better room.
- **Riley** at a newsstand on Astor Place that has every magazine in the world, including *Ms.* and *Rave*, both. She buys *Ms.* She turns *Rave* around so it faces the wall.
- **Cal** in a music store on West 48th Street, holding a bass he'll never buy, while a salesman talks to him about it for forty minutes because Cal knows more about it than the salesman.
- **Dean** on the Staten Island Ferry, round trip, three times, because it's a quarter and it goes past the Statue of Liberty, taking SX-70s of her from every angle.
- **Ellis** walking. Just walking. Clara with him, delighted, in the crowds on Fourteenth Street. *"Nobody looks at anybody here,"* she says. *"I like it."* The player can Observe on almost any corner; the notebook fills with strangers.

### Lunch

Thursday. A steakhouse in Midtown with dark wood and old waiters. Harold Vance flew up. So did a man from Monarch Records, the New York major label Southern Star is negotiating with to distribute its records nationally.

**Gil Tarver**, thirty-five, a good suit, a better haircut, a warm handshake for each of them, and he's done his homework. He calls Riley "Margaret" because that's what's on the copyright, and then "Riley" the moment she corrects him, and never slips again. He asks Cal real questions about publishing and listens to the answers. He laughs at Dean's jokes, genuinely. He tells Ellis he's played "Borrowed Stone" for his mother. He's very, very good.

At one point a Monarch A&R man at the end of the table leans over and says to Ellis, "You boys have banjos down there?" and hums a few bars. Everyone laughs a little too hard.

**ELLIS:** No sir. We couldn't afford banjos.

**DEAN:** We had to share a spoon.

Gil laughs hardest.

After, at the coat check, the player is Cal, waiting for his borrowed overcoat, and hears Gil around the corner with Vance, low:

**GIL:** The franchise is the singer, Harold. The rest is packaging. Good packaging. But packaging.

Cal takes his coat. Gil comes around the corner and sees him, and his smile doesn't change at all.

**GIL:** Cal! Great to meet you. Great questions today.

Cal tells nobody.

> **Setup.** Gil Tarver and the key-man clause, Chapter VIII. "The franchise" is what he'll call Ellis to his face by February.

### The showcase

Friday, December 12. A 400-seat room on Bleecker Street with a stage at one end, tables, a bar, and tonight, three hundred people who are paid to decide things: Monarch's staff, radio people, agents, critics, a man from a TV show, other bands' managers. Everybody has a drink. Nobody has a reason to like anybody.

The Room system in an industry showcase is the coldest crowd model in the game. The player, as Dean, can use *read the room* and see it: a flat gray field, no heat anywhere, a few people talking through the first song at a volume the band can hear from the stage. Intensity doesn't work on this room. Volume doesn't work. The things that won the Civic don't register.

Dean's field shows three warm points: a table of waitresses by the kitchen door; Dex Lundgren at the bar with his sunglasses on; and a woman alone at a two-top near the back with a notebook who is watching Riley and nobody else.

> **Character introduced.** **Nina Sorensen**, twenty-nine, critic at a New York weekly. She'll write the first serious piece about Riley as a musician in Chapter VIII.

The trick (and the game lets the player find it, without telling them) is to stop playing to the room. If the player, as any member, uses *attention* to face the band instead of the crowd, and the others follow, the band closes into the hollow square, playing to each other as if they were in the chapel at Dalton Sound at two in the morning, and the gray field begins, slowly, to warm from the back where Nina's sitting.

The set is the one on the receipt and on Cal's list:

- **"No Name,"** into **"Low Water."** The room keeps talking.
- **"Sunday Clothes."** Riley's lead. This is where it turns, if the player has the band facing inward: Nina Sorensen puts her pen down; the talking at the front tables stops, then the bar. The player, as Riley, controls her phrasing and harmony choices, and the song, in this room, is entirely hers.
- **"Still Here."** Before it, the player, as Ellis, can say "This one's for Clara," or not. (If he does, Dex, at the bar, grins into his drink.)
- **"Tomorrow Problem."** The industry crowd doesn't know it. The waitresses by the kitchen door do (they've heard it on the radio from Washington) and shout the tomorrow part, and the bartender joins them, and a man from Monarch looks around to see who's shouting and then shouts it too, embarrassed.
- **"Stony Knob."**
- **"Shape Note,"** five minutes, because Cal said.

At the end, Gil Tarver, standing at the back by the door, says one word to Harold Vance that the player can see him say across the room and can't hear: *Sold.*

### The Bowery

After midnight. Dean wants to see "the place Dex told me about." A bar on the Bowery with a bad awning and a worse bathroom, a long narrow room, a stage at the back about the size of Marlon's.

A band is playing: four boys in leather jackets and torn jeans, songs ninety seconds long, three chords, as loud and fast as they can, no solos, no drone, no space. The Blakes stand in the back with their coats on.

The player can be anyone. Each of them hears something different:

- **Ellis** goes to the front and stands there for the whole set without moving. It's the first music he's heard in a year that isn't reaching for anything.
- **Cal:** "They're playing three chords." / **Ellis** (if nearby): "They're playing them like they mean it."
- **Dean** watches the drummer and loves him, and says so, loudly, to the drummer, between songs.
- **Riley** likes it more than she expected and less than Ellis does.

At the bar afterward, a skinny kid in a leather jacket with a torn T-shirt under it looks at Riley's peasant blouse and Ellis's brown corduroy jacket.

**KID:** Nice. Hippies.

**DEAN:** We're from Georgia.

**KID:** Hippies from Georgia. Squeal like a pig.

**ELLIS:** You play in that band?

**KID:** Bass.

**CAL:** You're rushing.

**KID:** What?

**CAL:** On the second song. You're rushing the changes.

A pause.

**KID:** Yeah. I know.

They end up buying each other beers until two.

### The jacket

Saturday morning. Cold. Real cold, New York December cold with a wind off the river, and the brown corduroy jacket Ellis has worn since Chapter I isn't enough.

A secondhand store on St. Marks Place, down four steps, racks of other people's coats. The player, as Ellis, can try on anything. Most of it doesn't fit. A peacoat that's too small in the chest. A sheepskin that makes Riley laugh so hard she has to sit down on the steps. An Army field jacket Tully would approve of.

And a black leather jacket, secondhand, a little big in the shoulders, soft at the elbows, smelling faintly of someone else's cigarettes. Eighteen dollars.

It fits.

**RILEY:** Well, look at you.

**ELLIS:** Don't.

**RILEY:** You look like one of those kids last night.

**ELLIS:** I look warm.

He pays with the twenty Wayne put in his jacket, if the player kept it (in which case he has to break it, and he stands at the register a second before he does). If the player spent it, he pays with something else. The corduroy jacket goes in a paper sack.

> **Design note.** The bible says the leather jacket becomes "the jacket on the poster." It's bought because a boy from Georgia was cold. Nobody who ever puts that poster on a wall will know that.

### The mail

Saturday, noon. The coffee shop off the Albemarle lobby. Eddie comes in with a rubber-banded stack of fan mail Southern Star forwarded to the hotel: forty-odd letters, most of them from girls, some from boys who want to know how to get "that sound," one from a prisoner in Alabama.

He drops it on the table in front of Ellis.

**EDDIE:** Your public.

Ellis flips through them with one hand, eating toast with the other. Clara is across the booth from him with her boots on the seat.

Then he stops.

A plain white envelope. Blue ink. A looping, careful hand the player has seen before, once, in Chapter I, on the back of a birthday card in a cigar box in a room with the door closed:

> *Ellis Blake*
> *c/o Southern Star Records*
> *Laurel City, Georgia*

A small printed return-address label: *L. HUBBARD · 118 MAYFAIR DR · DAYTON, OHIO 45405.* Postmark: DAYTON OH, NOV 29 1975.

When Ellis looks up from it, the other side of the booth is empty. Clara isn't there. The player may notice she hasn't gone the way she usually goes (out a door, around a corner). She simply isn't in the booth anymore.

The player gets three options:

- **Open it.** Ellis turns it over. He gets his thumb under the flap. He stops. The prompt dims. He puts it in the inside pocket of the leather jacket.
- **Throw it away.** He gets up and drops it in the trash can by the register, and walks to the coffee shop door, and stops with his hand on it. He goes back. He takes it out. There's a coffee ring on one corner. He wipes it on his jeans and puts it in the inside pocket of the leather jacket.
- **Put it in his pocket.** He puts it in the inside pocket of the leather jacket.

Nobody at the table notices anything. Dean is reading a letter from a girl in Tampa out loud in a Southern-belle voice. Eddie is doing the check.

> **Knowledge.** Lorraine Mathis Blake Hubbard read the *Rave* article (Mission 13) on Friday, November 28, in a grocery checkout line in Dayton, and learned from a magazine that her son is famous and that her daughter died in a car he was driving. She wrote him that weekend. Ellis never opens the letter. At his funeral, Wayne gives it back to her unopened, and she takes it. The game never reveals what it says.

> **Replay layer.** Clara disappears when the letter appears, the same way she leaves when Wayne comes into a room. On a second playthrough, after Chapter VIII, the player will know why: part of Clara's face is the face of the woman who wrote it.

### The photograph

Saturday, 3:40 p.m. The Bowery. The brick wall with the torn posters. Snow starting, the dry kind.

The cold open plays again, all the way through this time, and now the player knows everything it didn't know three months ago: why he's in a leather jacket, and what's in the inside pocket of it, and what the band said to each other in a back room a week ago, and what they sang in a Linwood living room, and whose names are cut into a stone on a hill.

**DENNY OSTROW:** Closer. You're a band, not a bus stop.

They shuffle together. Riley in her father's peacoat, glasses on. Cal in the borrowed overcoat, too short in the sleeves. Dean in a satin baseball jacket over a Hawaiian shirt. Ellis in eighteen dollars of somebody else's leather, one hand in the pocket over the envelope.

**DENNY:** Don't smile.

Dean smiles.

**DENNY:** The drummer can smile.

Across the Bowery, by the newsstand with the shutters half down, Clara is standing in the snow with no coat on, watching.

The only input is where Ellis looks. The player can move his gaze freely while Denny shoots a whole roll, and the frame that becomes famous is the one where the player held Ellis's gaze longest.

- **The lens.** Ellis looking straight into the camera, his face unreadable. Known, later, as *the stare.*
- **Across the street.** Ellis looking past the photographer, off the left edge of the frame, at something none of the other three are looking at. Known, later, as *the look.* In the 1996 documentary, fans argue about what he was looking at.
- **Riley.** Ellis looking sideways at Riley, who's looking at the camera. Riley will hate this one for twenty years.
- **Up.** Ellis with his face tilted up into the falling snow, eyes half closed. Known, later, as *the snow picture.*

Dean smiles in every version.

**DENNY:** Got it.

The four of them break apart immediately, stamping, blowing on their hands.

**DEAN:** Can we go inside now?

**CAL:** There's a coffee shop on the corner.

**RILEY:** I can't feel my face.

**ELLIS:** Your face is fine.

They go. The player walks with them. Behind them the wall with the torn posters, and the newsstand across the street, and the snow.

Cut to black.

**CHAPTER VII COMPLETE**
**STRANGERS KNOW YOUR NAME**

> **Tracked.** The photograph the player made becomes the most reproduced image of The Blakes: *Rave*'s February cover, the poster on dorm walls in Chapter VIII, the backdrop of the *Night Stage* set, the festival program in Chapter X, the cover of Dex Lundgren's 1977 book, and the poster for the 1996 documentary. Every one of them uses the player's version.

## Chapter VII — design summary

### What happened
The record came out, and for a while nobody bought it, and then a nurse did. A DJ played a song twice on a Tuesday while a catcher waved a ball fair. Ellis left the garage with his name on a shirt. Sixteen hundred people sang a private conversation back to the person who made it up. A magazine decided who Ellis Blake is and printed his sister's death as the proof. Riley filled out a registration form and put it in her pocket. Dean lost a car and found out he's good at numbers. Cal kept his secret and his promise, and said the truest sentence in the chapter. Wayne came to a rock show, said "loud," and fixed a collar. A letter from Dayton went into the pocket of a jacket that was about to become famous.

### The reveals, and what's held back
- **Revealed (optional):** GRACE CLARA BLAKE and CLARA TATE BLAKE, on stones on the east slope, for players who find the cemetery.
- **Revealed to the world:** Ellis's sister died in a car he was driving; his mother left; "Old friend." The public now knows the facts and none of the truth.
- **Revealed to the player:** Wayne has had an FM converter tuned to the college station for a year. Ellis's body reacts to high beams in the rain. Clara vanishes when Lorraine's handwriting appears.
- **Held back:** Clara's face; what happened in the car; the other truck; Grace's last words; what Lorraine wrote.

### Systems introduced or extended
- **Fame layer.** From Mission 6, "Still Here" is in WLRC's rotation and the in-game radio plays it. NPC recognition in Laurel City; homemade shirts; stage-door crowds; the town-limit sign painted and repainted; the Ridge Pharmacy stocking *Rave*. Tannersville is slower to recognize them than Laurel City, and Hollow Ridge is divided.
- **Interview system.** Answers recorded in Mission 8, rendered selectively in Mission 13. Nothing is misquoted; everything is chosen.
- **Room at scale.** The Civic (slow crowd, delayed response, bad monitors); the Lantern (home); the showcase (the coldest crowd model; won only by playing inward).
- **Four Rooms.** The first no-proximity switch, carried along the rail line by train whistles.
- **Observe (the notebook).** Chapter VII has eleven Observe prompts, more than any chapter since I, because fame gives Ellis more strangers to look at and less time to look. Each writes one line into the current memo book (numbers 61–66 this chapter). Any the player collects can surface in the *Rave* excerpts in Chapter IX; authored fallbacks cover players who never stopped.
  - Wesley's shirt (M1): *The E he drew himself is bigger than the others.*
  - The nurse's note (M2): *ALBUM NOT THE 45, in a hand that writes on charts all day.*
  - The sink at home (M5): *Three years of grease and it came out in one washing.*
  - The converter (M6): *88.9 on a dial in a truck in the yard. A year of static he drove up a mountain for.*
  - The stage door (M8): *They say my name like they're giving something back.*
  - The east slope, if Ellis sits (M9): *The grass cut close by hand around two stones. The rest of the hill he leaves to the county.*

  - I-81 at 3 a.m. (M19): *Everybody I've got, asleep behind me at fifty-five.*
- **Tracked choices this chapter:** the record on the kitchen table; the FM converter dial; the chickweed; Riley's answer on the phone; whether "Borrowed Stone" was played with Wayne in the room; what Ellis told Patty about "No Name"; the film canister behind the Band-Aids; the photograph.

### Riley
Her chapter as much as anyone's. She walked out of a registration line, learned about Oberlin, sang an alto line in a hymn with a boy from Hollow Ridge and her mother, had her song credited to Ellis and corrected to "Margaret," was reduced to a comeback in a magazine, heard "I love you" with a flashlight in her hand, and started writing a song that isn't about either of the people she's spent the year writing about. She knows about Clara and got a promise ("After New York") she knows is hollow.

### Cal
Ignored at a stage door, quoted in a sentence that says the opposite of what he meant, abstained from hiring the man he loves, discovered that Dean can do his job better than he can, was seen by Ellis in a hallway and heard "He seems all right." He said the chapter's hardest true thing and refused to break a promise to Marlon to win the argument. He also overheard what Gil Tarver thinks the band is.

### Dean
Cut off, sleeping on Cal's cot, using every day now, and the only person in the band who got quoted accurately. His love for Ellis ("Blake's a genius") became the first sentence of the myth. He found a transposition at four in the morning and wrote *to be paid in full* under his own debts. He learned that a four-year-old sings his song in the bathtub.

### Wayne
Signed for the box. Has had a radio tuned to his son for a year. Watched a ballgame on the same couch. Read the magazine (off-screen). Came to the Lantern, said "Loud" and "I liked it" and "Your sister would've hated him," and asked whether that girl is still around. Put twenty dollars in his son's pocket with his own hands.
